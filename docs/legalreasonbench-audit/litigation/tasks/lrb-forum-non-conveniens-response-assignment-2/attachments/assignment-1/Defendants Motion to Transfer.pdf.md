Defendants John Smith, M.D. (“Dr. Smith”), DuPage Health, S.C. (“DuPage Health”), and Safe

doctrine of forum non conveniens, move this Court to transfer this action from the Circuit Court

In support, Defendants state as follows: 
I. INTRODUCTION 
1. Defendants do not contest that venue is technically proper in Cook County. Defendants move 
instead under the equitable doctrine of forum non conveniens, which “allows a trial court to 
decline jurisdiction in the exceptional case where trial in another forum with proper jurisdiction 
and venue ‘would better serve the ends of justice.’” (First Am Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.2, ¶5.) The 
private and public interest factors governing that doctrine, viewed in their totality, strongly favor 
litigating this action in DuPage County — the single county in which every Defendant resides or 
is located, in which the labor-and-delivery care giving rise to Plaintiff's claims was rendered, and 
in which the overwhelming majority of the fact witnesses and documentary evidence are found.

III. LEGAL STANDARD 
“Forum non conveniens is an equitable doctrine founded in considerations of fundamental 
fairness and the sensible and effective administration of justice.” (Langenhorst v. Norfolk 
Southern.pdf, p.5, discussing Vinson v. Allstate.) The doctrine “causes a court to look beyond 
the criterion of venue” and to consider “the relative convenience of a forum.” (Langenhorst v. 
Norfolk Southern.pdf, p.5, discussing Bland v. Norfolk & Western Ry. Co.) The “intrastate 
application of the doctrine of forum non conveniens presupposes the existence of more than one 
proper venue within the State and focuses the inquiry upon the relative convenience of the 
plaintiff's chosen venue.” (Bradbury v. St. Marys Hospital.pdf, p.2, ¶5.) 
A trial court “must apply an uneven balancing test to various private interest factors affecting the 
convenience of the litigants and public interest factors affecting the administration of the courts.” 
(Bradbury v. St. Marys Hospital.pdf, p.2, ¶5.) The private interest factors include “(1) the 
convenience of the parties; (2) the relative ease of access to sources of testimonial, documentary, 
and real evidence; and (3) all other practical problems that make trial of a case easy, expeditious, 
and inexpensive.” (First Am Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.3, ¶4.) The public interest factors include 
“(1) the interest in deciding localized controversies locally; (2) the unfairness of imposing the 
expense of a trial and the burden of jury duty on residents of a county with little connection to 
the litigation; and (3) the administrative difficulties presented by adding further litigation to court 
dockets in already congested fora.” (First Am Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.3, ¶4.) 
“The burden is on the defendant to show that relevant private and public interest factors ‘strongly 
favor’ the defendant's choice of forum to warrant disturbing plaintiff's choice.” (Langenhorst v. 
Norfolk Southern.pdf, p.7, ¶2.) The private and public factors are not weighed against one 
another; rather, “the trial court must evaluate the total circumstances of the case in determining 
whether the defendant has proven that the balance of factors strongly favors transfer.” (First Am 
Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.4, ¶3; Langenhorst v. Norfolk Southern.pdf, p.7, ¶2.) A defendant “must 
show that the plaintiff's chosen forum is inconvenient to the defendant and that another forum is 
more convenient to all parties,” though a defendant “cannot assert that the plaintiff's chosen 
forum is inconvenient to the plaintiff.” (First Am Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.4, ¶3.) 
A plaintiff's choice of forum is ordinarily entitled to substantial deference, but that deference is 
reduced “when neither the plaintiff's residence nor the site of the accident or injury is located in 
the chosen forum.” (First Am Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.4, ¶1.) “When the plaintiff is foreign to the 
forum chosen ... this assumption is much less reasonable and the plaintiff's choice deserves less 
deference.” (First Am Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.4, ¶1.) In a wrongful-death action specifically, “it 
is the decedent's residence at the time of death that is of significance,” and where the record does 
not establish that residence, a reviewing court cannot properly weigh the deference owed to the 
plaintiff's forum choice. (Bradbury v. St. Marys Hospital.pdf, p.3, ¶3; p.4, ¶6 (“we do not know

IV. THE PRIVATE INTEREST FACTORS STRONGLY FAVOR TRANSFER TO 
DUPAGE COUNTY 
A. Convenience of the Parties. 
Every Defendant resides in, or is located in, DuPage County. (Affidavit of John Smith, M.D., 
¶¶2–4, Ex. A; Affidavit of [Records Custodian], ¶¶2–4, Ex. B.) Cook County is convenient to no 
Defendant. This case is therefore unlike Guerine, where the Illinois Supreme Court found 
transfer unwarranted in part because one individual defendant “lives in Cook County and never 
filed a forum non conveniens motion,” such that “Cook County is a more convenient forum for 
all parties.” (First Am Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.7, ¶3.) Here, by contrast, no Defendant is 
connected to Cook County in any way, and Plaintiff has not shown — and, on the present record, 
cannot show — that she or the decedent resided in, or had any connection to, Cook County. Trial 
in DuPage County would be more convenient for every party to this litigation, and Defendants 
are aware of no party for whom Cook County would be more convenient. 
B. Relative Ease of Access to Sources of Proof. 
The labor-and-delivery records, electronic fetal-heart-rate and contraction-monitoring tracings, 
physician orders, nursing assessments and flowsheets, and neonatal records concerning the 
standard-of-care conduct at issue in this case were all generated at, and are maintained at, Safe 
Hospital in DuPage County. (Davis Complaint.docx, Reviewing Physician's Written Report, p.9, 
list items 1–6; Affidavit of [Records Custodian], ¶¶3–5, Ex. B.) The only records generated 
outside DuPage County are the records of Baby Davis's subsequent treatment at Cook County 
Hospital between August 28 and September 16, 2024 — treatment that post-dates, and is not 
alleged to be part of, the standard-of-care conduct on which liability turns. (Davis 
Complaint.docx, Reviewing Physician's Written Report, p.9, list item 7.) 
C. Availability of Compulsory Process and Cost of Attendance of Witnesses. 
The physicians, nurses, and other personnel who monitored and managed Sara Davis's labor and 
Baby Davis's delivery — the witnesses whose testimony will determine whether the standard of 
care was breached — worked at Safe Hospital in DuPage County and, on information and belief, 
reside in DuPage County or the surrounding counties. (Affidavit of John Smith, M.D., ¶6, Ex. A; 
Affidavit of [Records Custodian], ¶6, Ex. B.) While Illinois Supreme Court Rule 237 makes 
compulsory process technically available for in-state witnesses regardless of venue (see 
Bradbury v. St. Marys Hospital.pdf, p.4, ¶2), the practical cost, travel burden, and scheduling 
difficulty of compelling DuPage County-based treating providers to appear for trial in Cook 
County is substantially greater than the burden of trying this case in the county where those 
providers live and work. 
D. No Predominant Connection to Cook County. 
This case is readily distinguishable from Guerine and Langenhorst, in each of which the Illinois 
Supreme Court denied transfer because potential witnesses were scattered among several 
counties and no single county enjoyed a predominant connection to the litigation. In Guerine,

## PDF page 5

potential witnesses resided in Cook, De Kalb, Kane, Du Page, and Winnebago Counties, and the 
Court held that transfer is warranted only where “the potential trial witnesses are scattered 
among several counties, including the plaintiff's chosen forum, and no single county enjoys a 
predominant connection to the litigation.” (First Am Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.8, ¶4.) In 
Langenhorst, the majority likewise found that witnesses were dispersed across St. Clair County, 
Clinton County, Indiana, and Missouri, with no predominant connection to either proposed 
forum. (Langenhorst v. Norfolk Southern.pdf, p.10–p.11.) Here, unlike Guerine and 
Langenhorst, the situs of the alleged negligence, the residence and location of every Defendant, 
and the location of virtually every standard-of-care witness converge in a single county — 
DuPage. This is precisely the “predominant connection” the Supreme Court found lacking in 
Guerine, and its presence here strongly favors transfer. 
V. THE PUBLIC INTEREST FACTORS STRONGLY FAVOR TRANSFER TO 
DUPAGE COUNTY 
A. Local Interest in Deciding Localized Controversies Locally. 
DuPage County has a direct and substantial interest in adjudicating a controversy concerning the 
quality of obstetric care rendered by a DuPage County physician, a DuPage County medical 
group, and a DuPage County hospital, to a patient who sought that care in DuPage County. Cook 
County's only connection to this case is that Baby Davis was later transferred to, received 
treatment at, and died in, a Cook County hospital — a circumstance that, unlike in Bradbury, did 
not result from any Cook County conduct by these Defendants and does not implicate the 
standard-of-care issues that will determine liability. Compare Bradbury v. St. Marys 
Hospital.pdf, p.2, ¶5 (Cook County had an interest because the decedent died there, while 
Kankakee County had an interest because “the action involves alleged acts of medical negligence 
occurring within its borders”). Here, the alleged acts of medical negligence occurred exclusively 
within DuPage County's borders. 
B. Unfairness of Burdening Cook County Residents with Jury Duty. 
It would be unfair to impose upon Cook County citizens the burden and expense of jury duty in a 
case that does not involve any Cook County resident, any Cook County business, or any conduct 
occurring in Cook County. (See First Am Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.3, ¶4; Langenhorst v. Norfolk 
Southern.pdf, p.6–p.7 (public interest factors include “the unfairness of imposing trial expense 
and the burden of jury duty on residents of a forum that has little connection to the litigation”).) 
DuPage County residents, by contrast, have a direct stake in the quality of obstetric care 
provided within their own county and are the appropriate community to sit in judgment of this 
dispute. 
C. Administrative Difficulties and Court Congestion. 
Defendants respectfully request that the Court take judicial notice of the following statistics from 
the 2023 Annual Report Statistical Summary of the Illinois Courts, compiled and published by 
the Administrative Office of the Illinois Courts, a certified excerpt of which is attached hereto as 
Exhibit C. See Bradbury v. St. Marys Hospital.pdf, p.4, ¶5 (“judicial notice may be taken of the 
statistics issued by the Director of the Administrative Office of the Illinois Courts in assessing 
court congestion”); Langenhorst v. Norfolk Southern.pdf, p.18 (relying on “Administrative

Office of the Illinois Courts, [Annual Report of the Illinois Courts], Statistical Summary” data 
comparing pending caseloads and time from filing to verdict by county). Unlike the defendants 
in Bradbury, who forfeited any benefit from these statistics because they “never requested that 
the trial court take notice of those statistics and never quoted any such statistics to either the trial 
court or to this court” (Bradbury v. St. Marys Hospital.pdf, p.4, ¶5), Defendants here place the 
relevant data squarely before the Court. 
First, Cook County's 2023 clearance rate — the percentage of newly filed and reinstated cases 
the circuit closed during the year — was 60.5%, compared to 99.0% in the 18th Judicial Circuit 
(DuPage County). (2023 Annual Report Statistical Summary.pdf, p.21.) Cook County's five-year 
clearance-rate trend has likewise remained well below 100% in every year reported (2019: 
78.3%; 2020: 74.1%; 2021: 86.0%; 2022: 71.8%; 2023: 60.5%), while DuPage County's 
clearance rate has remained at or near 100% in most of those same years (2019: 100.6%; 2020: 
82.5%; 2021: 110.7%; 2022: 102.1%; 2023: 99.0%). (2023 Annual Report Statistical 
Summary.pdf, p.21.) A clearance rate below 100% means a circuit is closing fewer cases than it 
receives, so that its backlog necessarily grows; DuPage County's docket, unlike Cook County's, 
is essentially keeping pace with its incoming filings. 
Second, at the close of calendar year 2023, Cook County reported 145,608 pending civil cases 
(of 498,378 pending cases of all types), compared to 10,877 pending civil cases (of 101,244 
pending cases of all types) in the 18th Judicial Circuit. (2023 Annual Report Statistical 
Summary.pdf, p.19, p.46, p.49.) Defendants note, in the interest of full candor to the Court, that 
the Statistical Summary itself cautions that “due to changes in Cook County's case management 
system, there are variances in the number of cases being reported as open on January 1, 2023, 
when compared to the number of cases reported as open on December 31, 2022” (2023 Annual 
Report Statistical Summary.pdf, p.49), and Defendants accordingly rely principally on the 
clearance-rate comparison above, which does not depend on that reporting change. 
Third, Defendants further note, again in the interest of candor, that the Statistical Summary 
reports that DuPage County closed 17 “Law > $50,000” cases by jury trial in 2023 with an 
average time lapse of 51.4 months between filing and verdict, and that Cook County did not 
report any comparable time-lapse-to-verdict data for 2023. (2023 Annual Report Statistical 
Summary.pdf, p.159, p.160, p.163.) Because the record does not contain a directly comparable 
Cook County figure against which to measure that statistic, Defendants do not rely on it here, 
and instead rest the court-congestion factor on the clearance-rate and pending-caseload data set 
forth above, consistent with the Supreme Court's guidance that court congestion “is a relatively 
insignificant factor, especially where the record does not show the other forum would resolve the 
case more quickly,” but that record-supported congestion data may properly inform the totality-
of-the-circumstances analysis. (Langenhorst v. Norfolk Southern.pdf, p.12; see also First Am 
Bank v. Guerine.pdf, p.8, ¶3 (court congestion alone is not dispositive but may be considered).)

motion, unsupported by any affidavits, that offered only “unsupported and unverified assertions.” 
(Bradbury v. St. Marys Hospital.pdf, p.3, ¶4; p.5, ¶1.) Defendants have taken care not to repeat
