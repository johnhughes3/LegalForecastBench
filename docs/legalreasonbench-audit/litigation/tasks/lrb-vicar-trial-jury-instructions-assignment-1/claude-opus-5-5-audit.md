## O1: Abuse-of-discretion penalties can fire on a brief that recites the general rule or argues in the alternative

Status: **arguable**; category: `ambiguous_or_unjudgeable`; criteria: criterion_2, criterion_26.

Penalty misfire: the rubric is right that de novo review governs whether the evidence warranted a justification instruction (criterion_1, from Poe), and the real Eighth Circuit decision applied de novo review. But both penalties are worded as things the brief 'identifies' or 'states', at -30 each against 175 positive points, and the judge sees only that text. The attached Poe itself begins its standard-of-review paragraph with 'A district court has broad discretion when formulating jury instructions' before giving the de novo rule, and the attached White Horse says jury instructions are reviewed for abuse of discretion. A careful government brief often states the general rule, explains that sufficiency of the evidence for an affirmative defense is reviewed de novo, and adds that the ruling stands under either standard. Read literally, that brief 'identifies' an abuse-of-discretion standard and 'states' the court was within its discretion, and could lose 60 points while applying the correct standard. Eighth Circuit practice is also not uniform: Earth reviewed the refusal of a requested self-defense instruction for abuse of discretion. The penalties are sound against a brief that adopts abuse of discretion as the governing standard; the risk is to briefs that mention it correctly.

## O2: 'No reasonable belief' penalties can catch the rewarded retreat and provocation arguments

Status: **arguable**; category: `ambiguous_or_unjudgeable`; criteria: criterion_25, criterion_27.

Penalty misfire: the justifications say that because some evidence shows Nelson fired first, arguing that Patton lacked a reasonable belief is weak. That is a fair strategic view, and the supplied Facts say Nelson 'opened fire as the Trailblazer approached'. But each criterion penalizes any mention, at -20 each against 175 positive points. Under the attached Ellison, 'the use of force generally is not justified when the person knows that he can avoid the need to use it with complete safety by retreating or taking an alternate course', and 704.1(1) measures reasonable deadly force by whether it is reasonable to believe it 'necessary'. A brief that makes the rewarded retreat argument (criteria 9 and 13) in the statute's terms, saying that because the group could simply drive away Patton could not reasonably believe deadly force was necessary, trips criterion_25. Likewise, 704.6(3), which the rubric itself cites for criterion_15, and the pursuit facts support arguing that Nelson's shots were lawful defensive force, so that Patton faced no 'unlawful force' as 704.3 requires; that trips criterion_27. These are the rubric's preferred theories in statutory vocabulary, not errors. A response that argues the reasonable-belief element on the ground that Nelson posed no threat would properly be penalized.

## O3: Rewards the conspiracy forcible-felony bar but penalizes the intimidation bar on reasoning that fits both

Status: **arguable**; category: `internal_inconsistency`; criteria: criterion_21, criterion_28, criterion_29.

The justification for criteria 28 and 29 treats arguing that intimidation with a dangerous weapon bars justification as circular, because it amounts to saying the shooting itself is the forcible felony. Criterion_21 awards 15 points for arguing that Patton's conspiracy to commit a forcible felony bars justification under 704.6(1). But 704.6(1) bars only one 'participating in a forcible felony', and conspiracy under 706.1 (an agreement plus an overt act) is not listed in 702.11 and is not a felonious assault: Iowa treats a crime as a felonious assault only 'if it is a felony and it necessarily includes an assault' (Vavrik). On the rubric's theory, then, the forcible felony Patton was 'participating in' is the planned May 10 shooting itself, the same act the rubric calls circular when it is framed as intimidation (Vavrik lists the 'shoots' alternative of 708.6 as a felonious assault). The plea (pages 4-6, read from the rendered scan) does support pre-planning, which is relevant to provocation and to the preplanned-drive-by point (criterion_16), but not plainly to a separate 704.6(1) bar. The penalties have an independent, defensible basis: on the Facts, Nelson was in the bushes rather than in an occupied vehicle, so 708.6 is a poor fit. Grading effect: a careful brief that treats the two forcible-felony theories alike is either denied 15 points (relying on neither) or charged up to 30 (relying on both). Unresolved: I found no Iowa decision on whether the 704.6(1) forcible felony must be distinct from the force claimed as justified, so I do not say which way the rubric should go, only that it goes both ways.
