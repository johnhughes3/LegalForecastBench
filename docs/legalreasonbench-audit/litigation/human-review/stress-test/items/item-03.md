## Strongest objections

1. **A protective second-tier petition is the cautious default, because the deadline is irrevocable and the filing is cheap.** Second-tier certiorari must be filed within 30 days of rendition (Fla. R. App. P. 9.100(c)(1)), and the deadline is jurisdictional. The Order was signed and e-stamped October 25, 2025 (Order p. 6, per the AI transcription of image6.jpg), so the deadline fell on about November 24, 2025, four weeks after the assignment's October 27 memo date. A memo that recommends against filing gives up the option for good. A filed petition can be dismissed voluntarily if the remand succeeds. Construction began October 6, 2025 (LCU Project.pdf p. 1), and the project has been planned since 2010 (p. 4). A defender would say no careful lawyer advising a Board with a construction project underway lets the window close. **Answered by the note?** No. The note argues the merits of appealing at all, not whether to preserve the right to appeal. It is the most material objection John did not consider.

2. **Remand does not cure the bias finding, because the same Board re-decides.** Order Part II (pp. 3–5) finds that the County's conduct, including the 2019 remand history, the Harper purchase and the 2022 Kingston Settlement, "predetermined, or at very least created prejudice and bias," the approval. That finding concerns events before the hearing that a new hearing cannot undo. On remand, petitioners can argue that the same Board remains disqualified for the same reasons. A defender would say only second-tier review can remove that finding, so the remand alone does not let the County "reach the same conclusion" securely. **Answered by the note?** Partly. John calls the County's bias-based justification for the appeal "very debatable," but he treats the issue as mootness, not as a risk that the remand decision itself will be attacked. In the real case, petitioners did object on due-process grounds at the February 2026 remand hearing (CitizenPortal report, Feb. 18, 2026, seen only through a search summary; see below).

3. **The FAA holding may constrain the remand.** Order Part III (pp. 5–6) holds that the "most restrictive, current versions" of the FAA advisories, incorporated through LDC § 34-1102, "should have been considered." The Petition quotes the FAA's recommendation of "a distance of 5 miles" between an airport's operations area and a hazardous wildlife attractant (Petition.docx extracted text, lines 497 and 531). The Order notes the site is within 5 miles of the airport (p. 2). If the holding means the most restrictive FAA recommendation governs, the remand may not be able to re-approve this site, and only an appeal could correct the holding. Both native model answers treated this ground as a principal target for second-tier review (Opus 5.5 response, "Bottom line," calling it the Order's most significant holding; Luna response, "Executive recommendation," calling the airport issue one of the strongest appellate points and one carrying "material risk"). **Answered by the note?** No. John's premise that "the trial court's ruling here allows the government to reach the same conclusion it originally did" is accurate for Parts I and II. Part III is ambiguous: it is phrased as a failure to "fully consider[]," but it could be read as substantive. The February 2026 re-approval suggests the narrower reading prevailed in practice, but the solver could not know that.

4. **The appeal had real merits, and the rubric author's choice is one experienced counsel actually made.** *Hillsborough County v. G.L. Acquisitions Corp.*, No. 2D2024-1958 (Fla. 2d DCA July 9, 2025), granted second-tier certiorari and quashed a circuit court order that relied on an individual commissioner's comment. It held that "the County Commission speaks through its written Resolution." Order Part I rests on just such a comment ("Move the item based on the direction of the board over the last four years," p. 2). Resolution Z-23-035 adopts the Hearing Examiner's findings. The Order also ends "REVERSED" (p. 6), which a circuit court on certiorari cannot do: under *Broward County v. G.B.V. Int'l, Ltd.*, 787 So. 2d 838 (Fla. 2001), it may only quash. The real County Attorney's office filed the petition. Both native model answers recommended it. The protocol treats "a judgment call where the criterion takes a reasonable side" as Not defective. **Answered by the note?** Partly. John acknowledges that the real County appealed, and he concedes that appealing is a judgment call. He does not engage the merits, and his phrase "that's really a judgment call" can be read against his own Defective verdict (see objection 5).

5. **Protocol application: is this Arguable rather than Defective?** The reconciliation rated the issue arguable because "reasonable litigators could disagree about which recommendation is best" (reconciliation R7). **Answered by the note?** Implicitly, and correctly. The protocol's rule on criteria requiring one of several approaches makes a criterion Defective when "it rejects an approach a careful litigator would adopt on this record." Disagreement about which permissible strategy is *best* does not make the misgrading arguable. If remand alone is a permissible competent recommendation, a criterion that withholds 20 points from it, plus 20 more under criterion 24, is Defective. John applied the same rule in item 2. The note would be stronger if it said this expressly instead of calling the question "a judgment call."

## Stronger arguments for the call

- **The rubric's premise uses the wrong standard.** The source note justifies the recommendation "[b]ecause the Order contains reversible errors." Second-tier certiorari does not correct reversible error. It asks only whether the circuit court afforded procedural due process and applied the correct law, *Haines City Cmty. Dev. v. Heggs*, 658 So. 2d 523, 530 (Fla. 1995), and relief requires "a violation of a clearly established principle of law resulting in a miscarriage of justice" (*G.L. Acquisitions*, slip op., quoting *Miami-Dade County v. Omnipoint Holdings, Inc.*, 863 So. 2d 195 (Fla. 2003)). Review is discretionary even when no other remedy exists. A memo that weighs that narrow, discretionary standard against a remand the Board fully controls, and concludes the appeal is not worth it, is applying the correct law. The rubric's reason for preferring the appeal rests on an ordinary-appeal standard that does not apply.
- **The dual track is partly self-defeating, and the rubric's own law says so.** Criterion 10's source quotes *G.B.V.*: a quashal "leaves the subject matter ... pending before the tribunal ... as if no order or judgment had been entered," with the parties free "to proceed further as they may be advised." A new decision on remand replaces the quashed one, which is why the real County had to invoke mootness exceptions (per John's quotation of Pet. § VII). The criterion assumes "an appeal can be pursued parallel to a remand" at no cost. A careful lawyer could reasonably conclude that the remand makes the appeal largely academic and recommend the remand alone.
- **The rubric itself treats the appeal as optional.** Criterion 16 (+5) credits stating that the County "could appeal," alongside the "could" options in criteria 17–22 (remand, new application, voluntary cessation, governmental immunity, negotiated conditions). The prompt asks the memo to "identif[y] options" and then "recommend[] a legal course of action." The rubric gives 5 points for identifying the appeal and 20 for recommending it, which makes the recommendation the heaviest-weighted point in the memo. A memo that identifies every option, analyzes second-tier review correctly, and recommends remand alone loses 40 of 170 positive points (23.5%) under criteria 23 and 24.
- **The record supports a remand-first judgment.** The Order's defects in Parts I and II are procedural: a motion based on matters outside the record, and the appearance of predetermination. A fresh hearing on a clean record directly addresses them. The Hearing Examiner and staff had already recommended approval (Resolution recitals).
- **Native grading shows the wording also misfires.** Gemini 3.8 Flash failed the Luna answer partly because it recommended *preparing* a second-tier petition conditionally and noted that review "is not an appeal as of right" (reward-details for gpt6luna-xhigh). That is a judge's misapplication, not the defect John found. Still, it shows that the "should file a timely appeal" wording rewards an unqualified recommendation over a careful conditional one.

It does not "essentially acknowledge[] that the remand was likely to moot the appeal." The note should say "conceded the remand might moot the appeal." I could not open the petition to confirm the quotation or the § VII, pp. 45–47 pin cite (see below).

- **"Rubrics 23 and 24 ... require the government to simultaneously pursue an appeal and remand."** That is accurate for the pair. Criterion 23, the sampled one, requires only the appeal recommendation; criterion 24 adds the concurrent remand. The note's reasoning is about criterion 23, so this is a wording point only.
- **Imprecise phrasing.** The note uses "appeal" for second-tier certiorari, following the criterion's wording. This is harmless, but "second-tier certiorari petition" is the accurate term, and it matters to the argument (see the standard above).

## Defect basis and environment origin

- **Basis: professional judgment.** No authority makes a second-tier petition legally required or legally wrong here. The defect is that a 20-point "should" criterion rejects a permissible, competent recommendation, remand alone, on a record where the choice is genuinely open. The source note's "reversible errors" premise misstates the second-tier standard (*Heggs*, 658 So. 2d at 530). That is an objective legal error in the rationale, but the criterion's text, "should file a timely appeal," does not itself state an incorrect legal proposition. So the basis stays professional judgment, not unambiguous objective error.
- **Origin: not the environment.** The supplied Order, Petition, Resolution and project page give the solver what it needs to make either recommendation.

## Suggested revision of the note

> Criterion 23 (+20) requires the memo to recommend that the County "file a timely appeal" (a second-tier certiorari petition) in the Sixth DCA, and criterion 24 (+20) requires a concurrent remand to the Hearing Examiner. I agree with Sol that the appeal is not a necessary recommendation here, and there are good reasons not to make it. The Order quashes the rezoning, which under *Broward County v. G.B.V. Int'l, Ltd.*, 787 So. 2d 838 (Fla. 2001) (quoted in the rubric's own criterion 10 source), leaves the application pending before the County "as if no order or judgment had been entered." Its main defects (Order Parts I–II, pp. 2–5: a motion based on "the direction of the board over the last four years," and the appearance of predetermination) are procedural, and the Board can address them on a clean record at a new hearing. Second-tier review, by contrast, is discretionary and narrow: it asks only whether the circuit court afforded due process and applied the correct law, *Haines City Cmty. Dev. v. Heggs*, 658 So. 2d 523, 530 (Fla. 1995), and the rubric's stated premise ("the Order contains reversible errors") applies the wrong standard. A new decision on remand also largely moots the appeal, so the rubric's assumption that the two tracks run in parallel at no cost is wrong. There are real arguments for filing: the 30-day deadline is unrecoverable; the bias finding attaches to the same Board; the FAA holding (Part III) is ambiguous about whether it limits the remand; and *Hillsborough County v. G.L. Acquisitions Corp.*, No. 2D2024-1958 (Fla. 2d DCA July 9, 2025), supports the County on Part I. The real County did file, and its petition conceded that compliance with the Order "might arguably render this specific dispute technically moot," defending review chiefly to undo the bias finding. But weighing those points is a strategic call, and a careful lawyer could recommend remand alone. The rubric already credits identifying the appeal as an option (criterion 16, +5). Requiring it as the recommendation takes 20 points (40 with criterion 24) from a memo that analyzes second-tier review correctly and concludes it is not worth pursuing. Under the protocol's rule for criteria requiring one of several approaches, that rejects a permissible approach, so the criterion is Defective on professional judgment.
