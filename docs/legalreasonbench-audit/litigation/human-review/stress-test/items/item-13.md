# Item 13: lrb-forum-non-conveniens-response-assignment-1 — criterion_22

Bottom line: John has the mechanism right. The penalty's word "Mentions" could, read literally, reach a memo that reports the defendants' local-interest argument before rebutting it under *Bradbury*. But on this record the call fits Arguable better than Defective. The motion itself never says Cook County "lacks interest"; it concedes *Bradbury*'s holding and argues only that Cook's connection is thin. "Mentions that [a legal proposition]" naturally reads as asserting it. And the native evidence runs against a misfire: 0 of 6 judge votes fired, including on a Luna passage that credited the "wholly unconnected" argument under "What is strong for Defendants." The note itself says only that a grader "could" misapply the penalty. I recommend a substantive field change, Defective → Arguable (AI reasoning stays Correct), disclosed as a post-tally reviewer disagreement and not made as a silent edit. Confidence is medium, because John's reading is defensible.

## Strongest objections

1. **The protocol's Defective test asks whether the criterion *would* misgrade a competent response; the note shows only that it *could*.** Under the README, Defective means the criterion, "[a]pplied as written to the task as given, … would grade a competent response wrong"; Arguable covers cases where "[r]easonable litigators could disagree about whether the criterion would misgrade a competent response, or the problem would affect only unusual responses," and "borderline or low-impact concerns are Arguable." John's operative sentence is a possibility claim ("a grader could apply this penalty"), and his opening says the misspecification "seems to impact the grading." His own native-runs bullet then says it did not. Both the flagging model (Claude Opus 5.5 blind, O2) and the reconciliation rated this exact concern **arguable**, reasoning that "[t]he judge's 'commit this failure' wrapper may lead it to penalize only an endorsement."
   - *Does the note answer it?* Partly. John answers with the criterion 25 misfire (same "Mentions" verb, 3/3 against both runs). That is relevant evidence, but see objection 3 for why it is a weak analogy. He does not explain why a possible misreading crosses from Arguable into Defective.

2. **The motion does not argue that Cook County "lacks interest," so a faithful summary of it does not trigger the literal text.** Motion Part V.A (PDF p. 5) says: "Cook County's only connection to this case is that Baby Davis was later transferred to, received treatment at, and died in, a Cook County hospital — a circumstance that, unlike in Bradbury, did not result from any Cook County conduct by these Defendants…." It then expressly acknowledges *Bradbury*: "Compare Bradbury … (Cook County had an interest because the decedent died there…)." The background paragraph (PDF p. 2, ¶ 7) and Part V.B (no "Cook County resident, … business, or any conduct occurring in Cook County") speak of a thin connection, not an absent interest. The penalty's proposition, that Cook "lacks interest," appears in a response only if the memo itself recharacterizes the argument in those terms or adopts it. John's note says the defendants argue Cook has "limited interest," which is accurate. But the penalty targets "lacks interest," and the gap between the two is what keeps the risk low.
   - *Does the note answer it?* No. The note treats "mentions the defendants' argument" and "mentions that Cook County lacks interest" as the same thing.

3. **Criterion 25, and items 16 and 22, penalize a fact, not a proposition, so their misfires are not good evidence here.** Criterion 25 penalizes mentioning "that Cook County's overall clearance rate is 60.5%." That is a number any summary of motion Part V.C (PDF p. 6) will reproduce, and the judges fired 3/3 because the number appeared. Item 16 (60.5% again) and item 22 (Patton's state guilty plea) are likewise record facts. Criterion 22's object is an evaluative legal conclusion. "The memo mentions that Cook County lacks interest" ordinarily means the memo says so; it does not mean the memo reports that someone else says so. All six judge rationales read it that way. Sonnet (Opus run): "it is critiquing Defendants' motion for making that argument, not adopting it as the author's own analysis." Gemini (Luna run) described the response as "relying on Bradbury to refute the defendants' argument that Cook County lacks interest." That is the summary-then-rebuttal pattern the note worries about, and it was cleared.
   - *Does the note answer it?* No. The note lists criterion 25 and items 16 and 22 as the same wording problem without distinguishing a fact from a proposition.

4. **The native runs include a near-worst case, and it was cleared.** Luna's memo, under the heading the prompt calls for, "What is strong for Defendants," says DuPage's interest "gives force to the argument that Cook jurors should not be burdened with a dispute wholly unconnected to their county" (`model-runs/lrb-forum-non-conveniens-response-assignment-1/gpt6luna-xhigh/response.md`, line 68). Opus's memo restates the argument directly: "Cook County's only tie is that Baby Davis later received treatment and died there" (`.../opus55-low/response.md`, line 215, §F). Both were cleared 3/3. These are the competent, strengths-then-weaknesses responses the note says are at risk. Two runs are a small sample, but they are the only direct evidence, and they point toward Arguable.
   - *Does the note answer it?* Partly. The note reports the 0/3 results accurately but does not engage with the Luna passage, which is the closest a competent memo is likely to come.

5. **No objection to the penalty's substance.** A defender might also argue that a competent memo could legitimately conclude that Cook's interest is weak, because the family chose the transfer (prompt: "they had him transferred to the Lurie's Children's Hospital"). In *Bradbury*, by contrast, "it was the defendants that arranged for the plaintiff's decedent to be transferred" (Bradbury PDF p. 4). This objection does not succeed. *Bradbury* raised who arranged the transfer only in rejecting the *Bland* treating-physician argument under the private factors. Its public-interest holding, "Cook County has an interest because the decedent died here" (PDF p. 4), does not depend on it. A memo saying Cook's interest is weaker than DuPage's does not say Cook "lacks" interest. John is right that "the theory underlying the rubric is sound."

## Stronger arguments for the call

- **The weight makes even modest risk significant.** At −30 against 128 positive points, one misreading costs 23% of the available score, twice the largest required criterion (criterion 11, 15 points) and nearly the three largest combined (15 + 10 + 10 = 35). A defender of John's Defective call can argue that the drafting error is plain on the face of the text: "Mentions" where "Concludes" or "Concedes" was meant, as Opus's suggested fix proposes. On this view, the protocol's materiality requirement is met by the size of the penalty and not by the observed misfire rate. This is the best case for Defective, and the note could make it explicitly.
- **The prompt invites exactly the risky content.** The prompt asks the solver to note "what's strong" about each argument. The motion's *Bradbury* distinction has real record support: in *Bradbury*, the defendants arranged the transfer (Bradbury PDF p. 4), while here the family did (prompt). A careful memo is therefore likely to credit the argument somewhat, and some phrasings ("Defendants' best point is that Cook has no real interest in DuPage care") would fall within the literal text. The note could cite this to show that the at-risk response is not unusual.
- **Graders are not uniform.** Criterion 25 shows these same judges applying "Mentions" literally, so the protective reading in criterion 22 depends on the judges' interpretive charity, not on the rubric's text. A different judge panel or a human grader working fast might not extend it.

These points support treating the concern as real, but they do not show that the criterion *would* misgrade a competent response. On balance, they fit the Arguable definition ("[r]easonable litigators could disagree about whether the criterion would misgrade").

## Errors in the note

- **Tension within the note.** It says the misspecification "seems to impact the grading," but its native-runs bullet reports that the penalty never fired (0/6 votes). The note should say the risk is to unobserved responses, or rely on criterion 25 only for what it shows.
- **"Mentions" the argument vs. "mentions that Cook County lacks interest."** The note's operative sentence assumes that reporting the defendants' argument is the same as mentioning "that Cook County lacks interest." The motion does not use that proposition (Part V.A concedes *Bradbury*'s holding by "Compare" cite), so the equivalence is not established.
- **"Related items" grouping is loose.** Item 1 is a "States" penalty, but its recorded defect is an objective legal error (the AC-2-6 withdrawal rule is conditional), not a mention-of-the-other-side misfire. Items 16 and 22 penalize factual mentions; item 13 penalizes a proposition. The list also includes item 13 itself. None of this is a factual error, but the grouping overstates how similar the items are.
- **Citations check out.** *Bradbury v. St. Mary's Hospital of Kankakee*, 273 Ill. App. 3d 555, 652 N.E.2d 1228 (1st Dist. 1995) (decided June 29, 1995; Hoffman, P.J., with Theis and S. O'Brien, JJ.) is correct. The quotation "Cook County has an interest because the decedent died here" is on Bradbury PDF p. 4, confirmed in the pinned PDF. The motion quotation is accurate and on PDF p. 5, Part V.A, confirmed in the pinned PDF. The criterion 25 details (Opus: "the wrong statistic," `opus55-low/response.md` line 31; Luna: under "What is strong for Defendants," `gpt6luna-xhigh/response.md` line 76; 3/3 in both runs) are accurate.

## Suggested revision of the note

> Arguable. The rubric penalizes a memo that "Mentions . . . that Cook County lacks interest in adjudicating a controversy over care rendered by its DuPage based providers" (−30 of 128). The theory behind the penalty is sound: the supplied *Bradbury v. St. Mary's Hospital of Kankakee*, 273 Ill. App. 3d 555, 652 N.E.2d 1228 (1st Dist. 1995), holds that "Cook County has an interest because the decedent died here" (Bradbury v. St. Mary's Hospital.pdf, PDF p. 4), and Baby Davis died in Cook County. The problem is the verb. The prompt asks what is strong about each of the defendants' arguments, and the motion argues that "Cook County's only connection to this case" is the later treatment and death, which "unlike in Bradbury, did not result from any Cook County conduct by these Defendants" (Defendants Motion to Transfer.pdf, PDF p. 5, Part V.A). That distinction has some record support (in *Bradbury* the defendants arranged the transfer, PDF p. 4; here the family did). So a careful memo may credit the argument before answering it with *Bradbury*, and read literally, "Mentions" could penalize it. I rate this Arguable rather than Defective for three reasons. First, the motion never says Cook "lacks interest"; it concedes *Bradbury*'s holding and argues only that Cook's tie is thin, so a faithful summary does not trigger the literal text. Second, "mentions that [a legal conclusion]" ordinarily reads as asserting it. Third, in the native runs all six judge votes read it that way: they cleared Opus, which restated "Cook County's only tie" before rebutting it, and Luna, which said under "What is strong for Defendants" that DuPage's interest "gives force to the argument that Cook jurors should not be burdened with a dispute wholly unconnected to their county." The same "Mentions" wording did misfire on this assignment's criterion 25 (Cook's 60.5% overall clearance rate, 3/3 against both runs), but that penalty targets a number any summary of motion Part V.C reproduces, not a proposition. The fix is to penalize only a memo that concludes or concedes Cook has no interest.

(If John keeps Defective, the same note can stand with the last three sentences replaced by an explicit materiality argument: a −30 penalty that turns on a judge's charitable reading of "Mentions" misgrades often enough to matter, as criterion 25 shows the same panel reading "Mentions" literally.)
