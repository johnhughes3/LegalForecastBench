## Strongest objections

1. **The record never says that staff recommended *approval*.** The criterion assumes "the staff report recommending approval." The supplied documents say only that the Board considered "the recommendations of the staff" (Resolution.docx, fifth WHEREAS clause). The Hearing Examiner, not staff, is the one recorded as having "recommended APPROVAL of the request with conditions" (Resolution.docx, third WHEREAS clause). The Petition quotes the 2023 Staff Report twice ("SR23 p. 3" and "SR23 p. 3-4"; Petition.docx, Part I, after the Open House discussion), but neither excerpt contains a recommendation. A defender of Sol could say the criterion requires a factual characterization that the record does not state. *Answered by the note?* Only partly. "It is referenced in other documents" is true, but the note relies on the recital alone, and the recital does not say which way staff recommended. The inference is still strong and permissible. The Board "adopts the recommendation of the Hearing Examiner" (Resolution § E). It found that "[t]he recommended conditions are sufficient to protect the public interest" (§ E ¶ 4). The applicant's planner told the Hearing Examiner that the solid-waste use "is prohibited on our schedule of uses, as very clearly outlaid in the Staff conditions" (Petition.docx, quoting Tr1 p. 10, lines 13–22). Staff who draft conditions of approval for a County-filed application, which the Hearing Examiner then recommends approving with conditions, have recommended approval with conditions. The solver also acts as Assistant County Attorney for the applicant; the staff report is the client's own document, cited by page in the opposing petition. Nothing in assignment 2 forbids relying on it. This objection does not make the criterion Defective, but the note should state the inference.

2. **Second-tier review does not reach competent substantial evidence, and the Order did not rest on it.** Sol's real point (GPT-6 Sol F1) is that *Dusseau* "precluded [the district court] from assessing the record evidence." The Order quashed on three grounds: (I) the motion was based on "facts beyond the quasi-judicial record" (*Jennings*); (II) predetermination and bias; and (III) failure to consider FAA advisories that the Order treated as incorporated into the LDC (Orderpdf.docx, AI transcription pp. 2–6). It made no finding that the decision lacked competent substantial evidence. A careful brief could therefore omit any claim that a particular document "is" competent substantial evidence. *Answered by the note?* No; the note does not engage the second-tier framing. The answer is in the authorities. *Dusseau* approved the district court's holding that a circuit court that reweighs evidence or ignores supporting evidence applies the wrong law. It quashed only the district court's own search of the record. *Hialeah Gardens*, decided on second-tier review two years after *Dusseau*, granted certiorari because the circuit court "ignored" supporting evidence, and it identified the staff testimony and record materials as competent substantial evidence. The point also answers the Order's ground I: if the Resolution rests on the staff and Hearing Examiner recommendations and the record, a commissioner's reference to "four years" of board direction does not show that the decision rested on extra-record facts. Both saved model answers make that structural argument (Opus 5.5 low, "The Board acts through its resolution"; GPT-6 Luna xhigh, Argument I), which suggests the point is natural rather than idiosyncratic. Requiring one sentence identifying the staff recommendation as supporting evidence prefers a permissible point. It does not reject a correct approach.

3. **Inconsistency with the FNC items (14–20), where a missing document made the criterion Defective.** In those items John found the criteria Defective, with the environment Defective, because the physicians' and Sara Davis's affidavits were not supplied. *Answered by the note?* No, though the distinction is real. The FNC prompt said, "If a document is not uploaded, it does not exist for purposes of this analysis," and the criteria needed content that only the missing affidavits could supply. Here, assignment 2 has no such rule. Assignment 1's instruction to "[l]imit your review to these documents" (Prior assignment 1 prompt) is not repeated. Even if it carried over, the supplied documents themselves describe the staff report and its recommendations. The criterion asks for a characterization the record supports, not for the report's unsupplied contents.

4. **"In the second assignment" was misread by the native judges.** Several graders read "second assignment" as the brief's second argument or "assignment of error" (e.g., `anthropic/claude-sonnet-5` on both runs). That is judge misapplication of a drafting convention used in every criterion of this rubric, not a defect in this criterion. On the saved answers it likely did not change the outcome. Neither response says the staff report is competent substantial evidence. Luna mentions that the Board "gave full and complete consideration to staff recommendations" (response line 79); Opus quotes the same recital (lines 185, 379). Neither goes further. *Answered by the note?* Not raised; immaterial to the verdict.

5. **Is a written staff report, as opposed to staff testimony, competent substantial evidence?** *Hialeah Gardens* concerns "testifying staff members." Still, *Dusseau* recounts without disapproval the district court's reliance on "the recommendations of the Zoning and Planning Departments" as competent substantial evidence. In Florida practice, a professional staff report in the quasi-judicial record is routinely treated as such evidence. I did not locate a Florida appellate holding addressing a written staff report alone (see "What could not be verified"). This does not make the legal premise wrong, and a competent county lawyer would make the point.

## Stronger arguments for the call

- **The source case was itself second-tier.** *City of Hialeah Gardens v. Miami-Dade Charter Found., Inc.*, 857 So. 2d 202, 204–05 (Fla. 3d DCA 2003), granted the city's second-tier petition because the circuit court "applied the wrong legal standard," ignored the evidence supporting the city, and reweighed the evidence. It identified the staff members' fact-based testimony and the record materials as competent substantial evidence. That answers Sol's *Dusseau* objection directly: identifying the evidence that the first-tier court disregarded is how the "wrong law" argument is made.
- **Sol concedes the fact.** Sol's own finding says, "The resolution recites that staff and the Hearing Examiner recommended approval." The objection is to unverified *specifics*, which this criterion does not require. It asks only that the staff report recommending approval be identified as supporting evidence.
- **More record support than the note cites.** It includes the Staff Report pages quoted by Petitioners (SR23 pp. 3–4, describing staff's site-selection "due diligence"); the "Staff conditions" quote (Tr1 p. 10); Resolution § E's adoption of the Hearing Examiner's recommendation and its finding on "recommended conditions"; and the extensive conditions of approval in the Resolution, several of which assign approvals to "Development Services Staff."
- **The companion criteria confirm the design.** The 20-point *Dusseau* criterion (`states_in_the_second_assignment_that_the_circuit_court_erred_by`) and the parallel Hearing Examiner and expert-testimony items (item 7) make this a small (+5 of 150) supporting point in a coherent argument, not a stand-alone demand.
- **The reconciliation and Opus's blind pass reached the same result.** Opus reviewed the three CSE items and found no defect, and the reconciliation overturned Sol's flag. Those are arguments, not independent verification, but they line up with the record.
