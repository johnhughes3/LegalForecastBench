## Stronger arguments for the call

- **The supplied attempted-murder statute builds justification into the offense.** § 707.11(1) requires that the act be done "not under circumstances which would justify the person's actions." Treating the planned shooting as the § 704.6(1) forcible felony therefore assumes the conclusion. This argument is in the record, and it is cleaner than the "exception would swallow the rule" point.
- **There is Iowa authority on the same statutory phrase.** Iowa's felony-murder provision (§ 707.2(1)(b)) uses the same words, "participating in a forcible felony." In *State v. Heemstra*, 721 N.W.2d 549 (Iowa 2006), the Iowa Supreme Court held that when "the act causing willful injury is the same act that causes the victim's death, the former is merged into the murder." It therefore cannot serve as the predicate forcible felony. Iowa has thus already refused to let the very act in issue double as the "forcible felony" that changes its legal treatment. This is an analogy, not a § 704.6(1) holding, but it is Iowa law and stronger support than *Martinez* alone.
- **The statute's structure supports the independence reading.** § 704.6(3) lets an initial aggressor whose unlawful acts provoked the force regain justification by withdrawing, or when the response is grossly disproportionate. If the felonious assault an aggressor starts were itself a § 704.6(1) bar, subsection (3) would rarely matter for deadly-force cases.
- ***Ellison* n.1** (supplied) says that illegal activity alone does not remove the defense; it only imposes a duty to retreat. That is the doctrinal home for the conspiracy admission, and it matches how Mallory and the government actually used the facts.
- **On the supplied Facts, Nelson fired first** ("He opened fire as the Trailblazer approached," Facts.pdf, PDF p. 7). So the government needed a sound legal route that does not depend on who fired first. The criterion pushes a solver toward an unsound one.

## Errors in the note

1. **Overstated certainty.** "[T]hat statute only applies when the defendant commits a different forcible felony" is stated as settled Iowa law. No Iowa decision applying § 704.6(1) that way was found (my bounded CourtListener search of Iowa opinions found none either). It should be framed as the correct reading, supported by the text, *Heemstra* and *Martinez*.
2. **The burglar example is imprecise for Iowa.** Only "burglary in the first degree" is a forcible felony under § 702.11(1). A burglar who "merely intended to steal" without, for example, being armed while persons are present would usually commit second- or third-degree burglary, which is not a forcible felony. An armed burglar or a robber fits better.
3. **"[A]lmost always constitute a forcible felony"** is true of *deadly* force, but not of force generally, since simple assault is a misdemeanor. Saying "deadly force" fixes this.
4. **The link for *Martinez* points to a commercial blog** (lawofselfdefense.com). A court-record source is better: [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1107216/martinez-v-state/). The quoted phrase is accurate; CourtListener's text of *Martinez* (filed Feb. 21, 2008) contains "there must be an independent forcible felony other than the one which the defendant claims he or she committed in self-defense." *Martinez* also held that the error was not fundamental on its facts. That does not affect the proposition cited, but "holding that" should be read as the court's construction of Fla. Stat. § 776.041(1).
5. **"[N]ot public" is slightly off.** The government's Eighth Circuit brief and the district court's ruling (D. Ct. Doc. 460, cited in the brief in opposition at 6) are on PACER: public, but not freely available. The brief in opposition quotes the district court as finding that Patton "was engaged in illegal activity, including being a felon in possession of a firearm." The word "including" leaves open whether that court also mentioned the conspiracy. Even so, it was mentioned under the duty to retreat, not § 704.6(1).
6. The rest checks out. The plea cite (Count VIII, §§ 706.1 and 706.3, FECR338165, PDF pp. 4–5) matches the pinned scan. *Mallory*'s cites to § 704 are limited to §§ 704.1(3), 704.3 and 704.6(3) ("Cf."). The brief in opposition relies on § 704.1(3), *Ellison* and *Baltazar* (935 N.W.2d at 871). The quotation from the justification for criteria 28 and 29 is verbatim.

## Suggested revision of the note

> The rubric rewards a legal error. Patton, a gang member, shot at a rival he and others had chased from a mall; he later pleaded guilty in state court to conspiracy to commit a forcible felony "by planning with others to engage in a shooting" (Patton State Plea.pdf, PDF pp. 4–5, Count VIII). Criterion 21 wants the government's brief to say that this conspiracy precludes justification under Iowa Code § 704.6(1). But § 704.6(1) bars only "[o]ne who is participating in a forcible felony," § 702.11(1) does not list conspiracy, and *Ellison* says illegal activity "does not, on its own," remove the defense (Ellison.pdf, PDF p. 10 n.1). The only forcible felony the conspiracy reaches is the shooting itself, and that is the act Patton says was justified. Attempted murder is defined as acting "not under circumstances which would justify" the act (§ 707.11(1)), so using it to deny justification assumes the answer. The rubric itself rejects that move for the parallel intimidation theory: arguing that intimidation bars the instruction "is akin to arguing Patton can't get the justification instruction because he was engaged in the felonious assault of attempted murder" (criteria 28–29 justification). The forcible-felony bar is meant for a separate felony: the armed robber or burglar who cannot claim self-defense when the victim fights back. It is not meant for the assault claimed as self-defense. Otherwise the exception would swallow the rule, because deadly force used without justification is nearly always a forcible felony. I found no Iowa decision applying § 704.6(1) on this point. But the Iowa Supreme Court has refused to let the same act serve as the "forcible felony" under the identically worded felony-murder statute (*State v. Heemstra*, 721 N.W.2d 549 (Iowa 2006)), and Florida's high court requires "an independent forcible felony other than the one which the defendant claims he or she committed in self-defense" (*Martinez v. State*, 981 So. 2d 449 (Fla. 2008)).
>
> The environment is based on a real case. There, neither the Eighth Circuit (*United States v. Mallory*, 104 F.4th 15 (8th Cir. 2024), citing only §§ 704.1(3), 704.3 and 704.6(3)) nor the government's brief in opposition to certiorari (No. 24-5503, relying on § 704.1(3), *Ellison* and *Baltazar*) made this argument. They rested on the felon-in-possession duty to retreat and the preplanned drive-by, which other criteria reward. A careful brief that does the same loses 15 points here, while both native runs earned the points by asserting the categorical bar.
