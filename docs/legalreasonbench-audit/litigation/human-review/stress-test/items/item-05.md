## Strongest objections

**1. The criterion refers to a separate local development order, not the rezoning.** A defender would say "the development order issued for the project" means the executive, post-zoning local development order that authorized construction, not Resolution Z-23-035. The Resolution's conditions repeatedly contemplate one ("at the time of local development order approval," Resolution.docx condition 2; "prior to the issuance of the first development order," conditions on open space and buffers). The LCU Project Detail, p. 1, says construction started October 6, 2025, before the October 25, 2025 Order. The companion criterion `states_in_the_first_assignment_that_the_exclusive_method_of_chal` (+10) shows the author's theory: its source note says "[a] challenge to issuance of a development order, which is an executive function, is a separate cause of action from appeal of a quasi-judicial decision." On this reading, John's point that "the project at issue was timely challenged" answers a criterion about the rezoning, not about an unappealed downstream order. *Does the note answer it?* Only partly. The note's sentence "a court invalidated the approval for the specific project that was timely challenged" treats the rezoning as the development order and does not take on the separate-order reading. The objection still fails, for three reasons the note does not state. (i) Even an unappealed downstream order is protected only against "a subsequent judicial determination that such land development regulations ... are invalid because of a deficiency in the approval standards," § 163.3167(10)(a). The Order made no such determination: it quashed a quasi-judicial decision for going outside the record (*Jennings*), for prejudgment and bias, and for failing to apply LDC and FAA airport-compatibility requirements (Orderpdf.docx pp. 2–6). Those are defects in the decision, not in the approval standards. (ii) Subsection (10)(b) says the provision "does not preclude or affect the timely institution of any other remedy ... including a common law writ of certiorari proceeding," which is what the petitioners brought. (iii) The record identifies no issued local development order, its date, or its appeal status, so the criterion's premise ("the development order issued for the project," "the timeframe for filing an appeal ... has expired") is unsupported by the supplied record (basis (a), alongside basis (b)).

**2. A rezoning is itself a "land development regulation."** Section 163.3164(26) (2024) defines land development regulations to "include[] any local government zoning, rezoning" regulations. A defender could say the Order is therefore a "judicial determination that such land development regulations ... are invalid," which brings a later development order within (10)(a). *Does the note answer it?* No; the note does not address it. It fails anyway. The definition covers "ordinances enacted by governing bodies," and Z-23-035 is a site-specific quasi-judicial resolution. Even if it were an LDR, the Order did not find it invalid "because of a deficiency in the approval standards," the element the criterion omits. And under §§ 163.3164(15)–(16), the rezoning is itself a "development order" (an order on an application for a "development permit," which "includes any ... rezoning"). That order *was* "the subject of a pending appeal," so the (10)(a) predicate fails for the rezoning on the statute's own terms. The petition itself calls the rezoning "the challenged development order" (Petition.docx, Venue).

**3. Downstream permits may survive quashal on other grounds, so the criterion takes a defensible side.** One might argue the criterion is only a judgment call that the project can continue, which is the County's practical position and is consistent with the rubric's construction-related penalties (`..._county_must_stop_constru`, −20; `..._county_should_cease_cons`, −30). *Does the note answer it?* Implicitly. Whether a later local development order survives quashal of the zoning it depends on is a fair question (vested rights, equitable estoppel, and § 163.3167(5) are possible routes). But the criterion does not reward that argument. It requires the memo to attribute protection to § 163.3167(10), which objectively does not provide it. A memo that tells the Board its project is insulated by (10) gives wrong advice; a careful memo that warns that development orders resting on Z-23-035 are exposed (as the saved Opus 5.5 answer does, response.md, "Development orders and permits are exposed") loses 10 points. This is not one of several permissible approaches.

**4. Version.** The prompt directs analysis under law "in effect as of January 1, 2025," that is, the 2024 statutes, while the criterion and John's note cite the 2025 statute. The text of § 163.3167(10) is identical in the 2024 and 2025 editions (both checked), so nothing turns on it.

**5. Protocol rules.** The matter is a real case, not a hypothetical record, and no instruction directs the solver to rely on (10), so the hypothetical-record rule does not apply. The research universe is open, subject to the prompt's limit to research "necessary to identify applicable rules"; the statute is within it. The criterion is a reward criterion, and John correctly treats it as rewarding an incorrect statement and penalizing its omission.

## Stronger arguments for the call

- **The criterion drops the statute's operative limiter.** The source note quotes "because of a deficiency in the approval standards," but the criterion itself states the rule as "may not be invalidated by a subsequent judicial determination" without qualification. That is a misstatement of the statute's scope apart from any question about this record.
- **Statutory definitions make the rezoning the development order.** §§ 163.3164(15)–(16) (2024): a "development order" is an order on an application for a "development permit," and "development permit" includes "rezoning." The rezoning was timely appealed and quashed, so (10)(a)'s "not the subject of a pending appeal" predicate fails. The petition's Venue section calls the rezoning "the challenged development order."
- **(10)(b) names the remedy used here and § 163.3215 too.** The full text preserves "a common law writ of certiorari proceeding pursuant to Rule 9.190, Florida Rules of Appellate Procedure, or an original proceeding pursuant to s. 163.3215." It thus preserves both the certiorari route and the development-order challenge route on which the companion criterion relies.
- **Enactment history fits John's description.** The provision was enacted by CS/CS/SB 162 (2004), ch. 2004-37, as § 163.3167(13), under the Senate's description "limit[ing] the effect of judicial determinations concerning certain development orders pursuant to adopted land development regulations." It addresses later facial invalidation of regulations, not review of the approval itself.
- **The record cannot support the criterion's premise.** No supplied document identifies an issued local development order, its date, or whether it was appealed. The Resolution only anticipates future ones. The LCU printout reports that construction began, and it is a website capture printed March 16, 2026, after the memo's October 27, 2025 date.
- **Native grades.** Both saved answers scored 0/3 on this criterion, and the Opus 5.5 answer gave the correct contrary advice. That shows the criterion would penalize a competent response in practice.

## Errors in the note

- **Omits the statutory limiter.** The note describes the trigger as a later case that "invalidates the regulation." The statute requires invalidity "because of a deficiency in the approval standards." This is not wrong, but it leaves out the strongest textual point and the element the criterion drops.
- **"Typically involving a different development" is a gloss.** The statute does not require the later determination to arise in a different case. The note hedges with "typically," so this is acceptable, but it should not be read as a statutory element.
- **Conflates the rezoning with any downstream development order.** "Invalidated the approval for the specific project that was timely challenged" is accurate for Z-23-035. It does not address the separate local development order the criterion may mean (Objection 1).
- **Version.** The prompt's governing law is the version in force January 1, 2025, so the 2024 statute is the better cite. The text is identical, so this is harmless.
- **Quotations check.** The (10)(b) quotation is accurate but truncated before "pursuant to Rule 9.190 ... or an original proceeding pursuant to s. 163.3215, as applicable"; acceptable. "The rezoning is hereby **QUASHED**" matches Orderpdf.docx p. 1 (the Order's decretal paragraph on p. 6 says "REVERSED"; immaterial here). The flsenate.gov 2025 link resolves and supports the proposition.

## Suggested revision of the note

The criterion misreads Fla. Stat. § 163.3167(10) (2024; unchanged in 2025). Subsection (10)(a) protects an unappealed development order only from "a subsequent judicial determination that such land development regulations ... are invalid because of a deficiency in the approval standards." Its typical use is a later case striking the regulation itself, after which an earlier, unchallenged approval under that regulation stands. Here nothing of the kind happened. On a timely first-tier certiorari petition, the court quashed the project's own approval, the rezoning (Orderpdf.docx p. 1, "the rezoning is hereby **QUASHED**"), for going outside the record, prejudgment, and failure to apply LDC and FAA airport-compatibility requirements (pp. 2–6). It held no regulation invalid, let alone for deficient approval standards. Subsection (10)(b) says the provision "does not preclude or affect the timely institution of any other remedy ... including a common law writ of certiorari proceeding." The rezoning is itself a "development order" (§ 163.3164(15)–(16), "development permit" includes "rezoning"), and it was the subject of a pending appeal. If the criterion means a later local development order, the record identifies none, and (10) would not protect it from this Order anyway. The criterion rewards telling the Board its project is statutorily insulated and penalizes a memo that correctly warns that approvals resting on Z-23-035 are exposed.
