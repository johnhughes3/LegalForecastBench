# Item 25: lrb-vicar-trial-jury-instructions-assignment-2 — criterion_8

Bottom line: John's mechanism is real. Assignment 2 asks only for a supplement on *State v. Kuhse*, yet criteria 5–9 re-grade assignment 1 fact points, worth 65 of 135 points, on `response2.md`. For this criterion, though, the call fits Arguable better than Defective, for three reasons. First, both native solvers read the task as asking for a complete supplemented brief. Contrary to the note's framing, Luna's "Supplemental response" reproduces its entire assignment 1 brief and appends a *Kuhse* section. Second, "could have driven away" is not a pure repeat: *Kuhse*'s own justification instruction makes "[a]n alternative course of action was available" a disqualifying element, and Opus's *Kuhse* subsection used it for exactly this point. Third, Luna's 0/3 reflects a merits choice it also made in assignment 1, not the scope ambiguity. I recommend a substantive change, Defective → Arguable, with AI reasoning staying Correct, disclosed as a post-tally reviewer disagreement and not made as a silent edit. Confidence is medium, because John's reading of "supplement your brief" is one a careful litigator could hold.

## Strongest objections

1. **The native instruction points toward a complete deliverable, and protocol basis (c) requires an inference a competent lawyer "would not reasonably" draw.** The pinned instruction says: "Write the final complete legal deliverable for this assignment to `/app/response2.md`. The verifier reads only that file," and "your prior deliverable `/app/response1.md` is also on disk. Build on that analysis as the prompt directs" ([pinned instruction](https://huggingface.co/datasets/micro1-inc/LegalReasonBench/blob/1bfa904c8f255925baf2610c0f86aa7c05d94ed9/tasks/lrb-vicar-trial-jury-instructions/steps/assignment-2/instruction.md), lines 28 and 30). The prompt says to "supplement your brief," and on a natural reading the object of the work is the brief. The reconciliation treated this as decisive ("An insert-only supplement is therefore not the requested deliverable"). Claude Opus 5.5, the model that flagged the point, rated it only arguable because "[t]he role prompt's call for a 'final complete legal deliverable' cuts the other way." Under the README, a criterion that "requires something the instructions did not ask for" is Defective only if it is also something "a competent lawyer would not reasonably infer." A complete supplemented brief is a reasonable inference here.
   - *Does the note answer it?* Partly. The note says the model is "not clearly told" to produce a full brief and that it "might expect" to write only new material. That is the language of ambiguity, which the protocol assigns to Arguable ("[r]easonable litigators could disagree"). The note also leaves a good rejoinder unused (see "Stronger arguments" below).

2. **Neither native solver wrote an insert. Both delivered a complete supplemented brief.** Luna's assignment 1 answer is 1,090 words. Its assignment 2 answer (1,501 words) is that same brief, with a new title ("Supplemental response concerning Patton's requested justification instruction"), a *Kuhse* citation added to the standard paragraph, a few small edits, and a new *Kuhse* section of about 400 words appended. A `diff` of the two files shows every assignment 1 paragraph carried forward (`model-runs/lrb-vicar-trial-jury-instructions-assignment-1/gpt6luna-xhigh/response.md` against `.../assignment-2/gpt6luna-xhigh/response.md`, lines 3–19 and 21–28). Opus's assignment 2 answer is a full argument section of 6,417 words, up from 4,396 in assignment 1. Its drafting note 5 says the new *Kuhse* analysis "appears in Part II.B.4 ... and Part II.D" (`.../assignment-2/opus55-low/response.md`, line 16). So the insert-only response the note describes is hypothetical on the available evidence, and both solvers drew the inference objection 1 calls reasonable.
   - *Does the note answer it?* No. The note sets Opus's "full argument section" against Luna's "shorter 'Supplemental response,'" which implies that Luna wrote only the new material. It did not (see Errors).

3. **This criterion, unlike criteria 5–6, is part of a natural *Kuhse* application, not just a repeated fact.** *Kuhse* quotes the justification instructions given to its jury. Instruction No. 13 provides that the defendant "was not justified" if the State proved any one of five elements, including "2. An alternative course of action was available to the Defendant" (Kuhse.pdf, PDF p. 5; confirmed in the [pinned PDF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/micro1-inc/LegalReasonBench/blob/1bfa904c8f255925baf2610c0f86aa7c05d94ed9/tasks/lrb-vicar-trial-jury-instructions/steps/assignment-2/workdir/attachments/Kuhse.pdf)). Opus's *Kuhse* subsection used exactly that element: "'An alternative course of action was available': the group could have stopped the chase at any time. (Facts.pdf at 7.)" (`.../opus55-low/response.md`, Part II.B.4.c, line 134). An insert that applies *Kuhse* to this record and argues that Patton failed the threshold (criterion 10) could therefore reach the drive-away point on its own. The upstream justification for criterion 8 cites the *Kuhse* request, not the assignment 1 prompt. Windows rolled down (criterion 5) and Patton hanging out the window (criterion 6) are closer to pure repetition. Criterion 8 is the weakest member of the group for the sequential-dependency theory.
   - *Does the note answer it?* No. The note treats criteria 5–9 as an undifferentiated block of repeats.
   - *Limits of the objection.* *Kuhse* "did not decide" the content of those instructions (Opus says so itself at line 134), and the case is an ineffective-assistance appeal about a marshaling instruction. Its only rule that the rubric uses is the burden statement at PDF p. 9 (criteria 1, 2, 10). A competent insert could rest on that burden allocation alone and never discuss retreat. The objection makes the misgrade less likely; it does not eliminate it.

4. **The native 0/3 is not evidence of the scope defect.** Luna's submission carried forward the assignment 1 sentence that "the forcible-felony bar—not retreat alone—is dispositive here" (assignment 1 line 17; assignment 2 line 19). Luna missed the same point in assignment 1 (criterion 13, 0/3) with the same text. Its miss on criterion 8 follows from a merits choice, not from treating the task as insert-only. Opus earned credit (2/3) from passages in both the carried-forward retreat argument (line 80: "the OTB group could simply have stopped following Nelson") and the alternatives argument (line 100). The judge rationales quote both.
   - *Does the note answer it?* The note reports the scores accurately. But read alongside the Luna characterization, it implies a format effect the record does not show.

5. **Environment and origin.** The protocol's environment verdict turns on "objective errors in the supplied record, or task materials that direct work product a competent litigator should not put before a court." Its examples of environment defects are conflicting figures, faulty arithmetic, and fabricated authority. An under-specified deliverable scope is neither kind. "Defect arises from environment: Yes" likewise requires "an error in the supplied task materials rather than in the criterion alone." Here the instruction is at most ambiguous. What produces the misgrade risk is the rubric's choice to re-grade assignment 1 points against a supplementation prompt.
   - *Does the note answer it?* No. This does not change the environment verdict, however. The environment verdict is assessed per assignment, and John's item 23 records this assignment's environment as Defective on an independent ground: Facts.pdf PDF p. 7 says Nelson "opened fire as the Trailblazer approached," while the rubric assumes it is unclear who fired first. Items 23–25 should share that verdict.

## Stronger arguments for the call

- **The "final complete legal deliverable" sentence is harness boilerplate.** It appears verbatim in all 11 litigation assignment instructions, including every first assignment (`tasks/*/upstream-instruction.md`; 11 of 11). On that evidence, "complete" means finished and written to the file, not "a full brief." The follow-on sentence also defers to the prompt ("Build on that analysis *as the prompt directs*"), and the prompt directs a supplement: "Please supplement your brief with relevant legal analysis of State v. Kuhse." This is the best answer to the reconciliation, and the note could make it.
- **The rubric's own justification undercuts the repetition.** Every assignment 2 criterion, criterion 8 included, cites as its source "Please supplement your brief with relevant legal analysis of State v. Kuhse." *Kuhse* is a domestic-assault ineffective-assistance case. It says nothing about retreat by car, pursuit, or illegal activity. The duty-to-retreat law the point depends on comes from Ellison.pdf (PDF p. 8: "The usual duty to retreat generally still exists if a person is engaged in illegal activity") and Iowa Code § 704.1(3), both supplied in assignment 1. So the criterion is grading an assignment 1 argument under an assignment 2 label.
- **Practice supports the insert reading.** When a supervising attorney writes "I found [case], which I didn't see in your initial response. Please supplement your brief with relevant legal analysis," a common and competent reply is a new section or insert with a note on where it goes. That is especially so when the supervisor already holds the earlier draft. The email's framing (a single new case the associate missed) fits an insert.
- **The stakes are large.** Criteria 5–9 carry 65 of 135 positive points (48%). An insert that does everything the prompt asks could lose up to that much, and up to 15 on this criterion alone. If the risk is real, it is material.

These points show the concern is genuine. They do not show that this criterion *would* misgrade a competent response: both observed solvers resolved the ambiguity toward a complete brief, and the drive-away point is reachable through *Kuhse* itself. On balance, they fit the README's Arguable definition.

## Errors in the note

- **Luna's answer is mischaracterized by implication.** The note contrasts Opus, which "rewrote the full argument section," with Luna, which "wrote a shorter 'Supplemental response' (about 1,500 words)." Luna's assignment 2 file contains its entire assignment 1 brief (1,090 words), lightly edited, plus an appended *Kuhse* section. Both solvers produced complete supplemented deliverables. The word counts (6,417 and 1,501) are accurate.
- **The note's account of model expectations has no support in the runs.** The note says that "the model might expect that it should write only the new material." Neither native model did. The point can stand as a hypothesis, but not as something the runs illustrate.
- **The "Related items" line refers to itself.** Item 25's note says "items 24 and 25 share this defect." It should say "item 24 shares this defect," and it could add item 23 as the same assignment's other environment ground.
- **"Token inefficiency" mislabels the issue.** Restating key facts in a full brief section is not inefficiency in any legal sense. Opus's 6,417 words are within the 8,750-word brief limit in the preamble. The real issue is unrequested scope (basis (c)), which the note otherwise states correctly.
- **Accurate points.** Criteria 5–9 match assignment 1 criteria 10–14 word for word. The points (65 of 135 in assignment 2; 70 in assignment 1) are correct. Fresh conversations in one sandbox are confirmed (`model-runs/experiment.md`, "Native execution conditions"). Opus met the criterion 2/3 (votes 1, 1, 0) and Luna 0/3. In assignment 1, criterion 13 went 1/3 for Opus and 0/3 for Luna, so neither met it. The quotations from the preamble and the prompt match the pinned instruction.
