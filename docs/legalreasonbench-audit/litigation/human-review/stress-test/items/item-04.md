# Item 4: lrb-certiorari-quasi-judicial-land-use-assignment-1 — states_in_the_first_assignment_that_the_county_should_remand_the

**Bottom line.** John's call (Defective, professional judgment, not from the environment) holds up. The criterion is conjunctive by design: its source note says the "best recommendation" is remand *because* "an appeal can be pursued parallel to a remand." So a memo that recommends remand alone, which is what a careful county attorney could well advise, fails 20 points here on top of the 20 it loses on criterion 23 (item 3). Under the protocol's rule on criteria that require one of several permissible approaches, that is a defect, not a mere preference. The strongest counterargument is that this is the judgment call Sol and the reconciliation both called "arguable." The note should answer it directly, cite its sources, and soften "incorrect" to "not required" so the wording matches the recorded basis. I recommend no field change.

## Strongest objections

1. **This is the judgment call the protocol treats as Arguable or Not defective.** The protocol lists "a judgment call where the criterion takes a reasonable side" as Not defective, and says "Requiring one permissible option is not by itself a defect." Appeal plus concurrent remand is a reasonable strategy. The real county pursued it: the circuit order is dated October 25, 2025 (Orderpdf.docx p. 6 e-stamp), the county filed its second-tier petition in *Lee County v. Spahhn*, No. 6D2025-2926, on November 24, 2025, and the Board's February 18, 2026 zoning agenda lists "DCI2023-00011 Southeast Water Reclamation Facility (Remand)," remanded to the Hearing Examiner "for the limited purpose of addressing findings of the 20th Circuit Court in its order dated October 25, 2025" (as reported in search results; see "What could not be verified"). Both GPT-6 Sol (F7) and the reconciliation (R7) rated the issue arguable, not problematic. *Does the note answer it?* Partly. Item 3's note gives the substantive reason: the Order lets the County reach the same result after fixing its process, and the County's own Sixth District petition (§ VII at 45–47) concedes that compliance "might arguably render this specific dispute technically moot." That shows remand alone is an approach a careful litigator would adopt, which puts the item under the protocol's Defective branch ("It is Defective when it rejects an approach a careful litigator would adopt on this record"). But item 4's note says the appeal requirement is "incorrect," which sounds like a legal error, and never ties the reasoning to that protocol rule. The answer is there by cross-reference; it should be stated.

2. **"While an appeal is pending" might describe timing rather than require an appeal.** A defender could read the phrase as permissive ("remand, and you need not wait for the appeal"), so a remand-only memo could pass. *Answered?* Implicitly. The upstream source note forecloses that reading: "Because the Order contains reversible errors, and because an appeal can be pursued parallel to a remand, this is the best recommendation." Criterion 23 (+20), the companion criterion, separately requires a recommendation to "file a timely appeal with the 6th District Court of Appeals," and criterion 17 (+5) already credits listing remand as an option. Read together, criterion 24 rewards the combined strategy. The native judges also read the criterion literally, looking for a remand "while an appeal is pending" (all six judgments in `model-runs/.../reward-details.json`). The note could quote the source note to close this off.

3. **Double counting with item 3.** One underlying judgment (whether an appeal is required) now produces two Defective items. *Answered?* Not in the note, but the protocol answers it: the unit is the criterion, and "One underlying error ... can make several criteria defective." Worth one clause so a reader doesn't think it was overlooked.

4. **An appeal has a concrete benefit the note does not weigh: it can keep the original approval in force.** Fla. R. App. P. 9.310(b)(2) provides that "[t]he timely filing of a notice shall automatically operate as a stay pending review ... when the state, any public officer in an official capacity, board, commission, or other public body seeks review." If that stay reaches a county's second-tier certiorari petition, an appeal would hold the quashal in abeyance while remand proceeds, and construction (started October 6, 2025, LCU Project.pdf p. 1) could continue under Resolution Z-23-035. That is a strong practical reason a lawyer might insist on the combined strategy. *Answered?* No; item 3's note addresses mootness and the weak bias-finding rationale but not the stay. This does not defeat the call. The rule speaks of a "notice," its application to a second-tier petition was not verified here, and the court "may ... vacate the stay." The protocol also asks whether the rubric rejects a permissible approach, not whether the rubric's approach has advantages. But a defender would raise it, and the note would be more persuasive if it acknowledged that the appeal-plus-remand strategy is reasonable and that the defect is mandating it.

5. **Remand may not cure every ground of the Order.** Grounds I and II of the Order rest on the *Board's* departure from the record and the County's predetermination (Orderpdf.docx pp. 2–5); only ground III (FAA advisories) is plainly a Hearing Examiner record issue (pp. 5–6). The Order's decretal line also says "REVERSED" rather than quashed-and-remanded (p. 6). Someone could argue remand is not obviously "the standard approach," so the criterion's remand half is also contestable. *Answered?* John asserts remand is standard without citation. The assertion is defensible. Under *Broward County v. G.B.V. International, Ltd.*, 787 So. 2d 838 (Fla. 2001), quoting *Tamiami Trail Tours*, a quashal leaves the matter "pending before the tribunal ... as if no order or judgment had been entered." LDC § 34-83(a)(4)a.1.b) lets the Board "remand a case back to the Hearing Examiner for further review of specific issues." The real county did exactly that. The objection cuts *for* John's verdict anyway: if remand is contestable, the criterion is no less defective. It does not affect the outcome; the note just needs authority.

6. **Neither native run isolates the defect.** Both saved responses (GPT-6 Luna xhigh; Claude Opus 5.5 low) recommended second-tier certiorari and did not recommend remand, so they scored 0/3 for reasons unrelated to the appeal requirement. No saved response shows a remand-only memo failing. *Answered?* The note does not rely on the runs, which is right; the defect is in the criterion's text. Not material.

## Stronger arguments for the call

- **The rubric's own scheme shows the conjunction is deliberate.** Criterion 17 (+5) credits *identifying* remand as an option; criterion 24 (+20) is the *recommendation*, expressly conditioned on a pending appeal; criterion 23 (+20) requires the appeal. A remand-only memo earns 5 and loses 40 of 170 positive points. That is a large weight on one contestable strategy.
- **Concurrent remand and appeal are in tension.** The County's Sixth District petition (§ VII at 45–47, per item 3) concedes that complying with the circuit court's order "might arguably render this specific dispute technically moot." A strategy whose two halves may undercut each other is a reasonable bet, but not one a careful lawyer must recommend. The County fell back on mootness exceptions to keep the appeal alive, mainly to undo the bias finding. This is the best evidence that remand alone is a competent recommendation, and item 4's note should carry it directly rather than only by reference.
- **The prompt is open.** The instruction asks the memo to "4) recommend[] a legal course of action to the Board" (assignment-1 `instruction.md`). Nothing in it signals that the Board wants to pursue both an appeal and a remand.
- **The rubric's authority does not support the appeal half.** The criterion cites only LDC § 34-83(a)(6) (2025), which authorizes nothing about appeals. As the matter citation check found, the quoted remand sentence is actually in § 34-83(a)(4)a.1.b); (a)(6) is the six-month deemed-withdrawal rule. The "while an appeal is pending" condition rests only on the author's say-so ("this is the best recommendation").
- **One blind model (Opus) passed this criterion,** but its reason ("consistent with G.B.V.'s rule that a quashal leaves the matter pending below") addresses only the remand half and does not engage the appeal condition. So Opus's not-flag does not answer Sol's ground.

## Errors in the note

- **"hearing officer."** The criterion, the LDC and the record say "Hearing Examiner." Minor, but the note should use the record's term.
- **"which is incorrect."** Requiring an appeal is not a legal error; item 3's own note calls it "really a judgment call." "Incorrect" sits uneasily with the recorded basis (Professional judgment) and invites objection 1. Say "not required" or "a strategy many careful lawyers would advise against."
- **No source locator.** The protocol calls for "the document and passage or the authority relied on." The note relies entirely on the cross-reference to item 3 and cites nothing for the claim that remand is standard.
- **"Sol flagged it (as arguable) on this same ground"** is accurate. Sol F7 cites criterion 24's "while an appeal is pending" language and says requiring both recommendations "can fail that reasoned alternative." Sol's listed alternatives ("new proceedings, negotiated conditions, or a stay-first approach") include remand-type new proceedings; the reconciliation names "remand alone" explicitly.
- The cross-reference "criterion 23 (item 3)" is correct: `states_in_the_first_assignment_that_the_county_should_file_a_tim` is the 23rd criterion in `input.json` and is worksheet item 3.

No misquotation: "while an appeal is pending" matches the criterion text.
