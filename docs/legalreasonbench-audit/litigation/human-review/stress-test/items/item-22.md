# Item 22: lrb-vicar-trial-jury-instructions-assignment-1 — criterion_23

Bottom line: John's call holds. The criterion penalizes any response that "[m]entions that Patton pled guilty in state court to intimidation with a dangerous weapon" (−5 of 175). The judges see only that text, not the justification explaining that the concern is relevance. In the native runs it misfired on a competent response: Opus 5.5's accurate drafting note separating the April 13 Count XX petition from the May 10 Count VIII admission was penalized 2/3. Unlike item 13, this is a misgrade that actually happened, not just a possible one. The strongest objection is that a −5 penalty is "low-impact" and so Arguable, as the reconciliation said. The worksheet has consistently not treated point weight as materiality (items 14 and 17–20 are +3 of 73), and the protocol asks whether the criterion misgrades on the point it covers. The one weak spot is the note's second rationale: a competent government brief would not introduce an unrelated, later-adjudicated shooting as "background about who the defendant is." I recommend a note-only revision and no field change.

## Strongest objections

1. **Low impact makes this Arguable, not Defective.** The README says "borderline or low-impact concerns are Arguable." The penalty is −5 against 175 reward points (2.9%). The reconciliation (R4) downgraded Sol's "problematic" to "arguable" on exactly this ground: "At -5 of 175, however, the effect is small, and careful briefs will often omit an irrelevant prior shooting."
   - *Does the note answer it?* No, not expressly. But the answer is strong. (i) The protocol defines Defective by whether the criterion "would grade a competent response wrong on the point it covers," not by its share of the total score. (ii) The worksheet has applied that reading consistently: items 14 and 17–20 are +3 reward criteria out of 73 (4.1%), and all are recorded Defective. Treating item 22 differently because of its weight would be inconsistent. (iii) The impact was observed, not hypothetical. One of the two saved native runs was penalized (`model-runs/lrb-vicar-trial-jury-instructions-assignment-1/opus55-low/reward-details.json`, criterion_23: GPT-5.6 Terra and Claude Sonnet 5 voted yes, Gemini 3.8 Flash no). That is not an "unusual response" problem. The plea is a single two-count document in one case (FECR338165), Count XX fills PDF pp. 1–3, and both petitions recite the same agreement, "Defendant to plead guilty to Counts VIII and XX" (PDF pp. 2, 5). So any response that describes or pin-cites the plea carefully is likely to meet Count XX. The note should say this.

2. **A careful prosecutor would not mention the April 13 plea, so the penalty targets bad lawyering.** The rubric's justification is right that the April 13 shooting is irrelevant to whether Patton was entitled to a justification instruction for May 10. In a government appellate brief, raising an unrelated shooting looks like propensity argument, adds a fact the supervisor's Statement of the Case omits (Facts.pdf mentions no April 13 incident), and draws on a document whose place in the trial record is not shown. On this view the penalty enforces good judgment, and Opus's drafting note was surplus content in a file the verifier grades as the deliverable.
   - *Does the note answer it?* Half. For drafting notes and accurate distinctions, the answer is good: the note to the supervisor is what a careful junior lawyer should write. The cited document opens with three pages on a different shooting, the rubric itself rewards citing that document (criteria 19–21, 30 points), and the supervisor needs to know which pages are being relied on and which are not. Opus's note also asked the supervisor to confirm that the Count VIII petition is in the record, which is careful work. But the note's second rationale ("[a] competent lawyer could also mention the defendant's prior convictions as part of the factual background … or introduction to who the defendant is") is the weakest part of the call, and this objection lands against it. The facts section was already drafted (prompt: "I've already drafted the facts section of this brief"). The assignment was the argument section. Characterizing the defendant through an unrelated shooting is what a careful AUSA avoids. The verdict should rest on the drafting-note and accurate-distinction cases, not on this one.

3. **"Mentions … pled guilty" naturally reads as relying on the plea, and a fair judge would not fire on a disclaimer.** Gemini 3.8 Flash read it that way and cleared Opus: "Drafting Note 2 explicitly clarifies that Count XX covers a separate April 13, 2020 incident and properly limits the argument to Patton's Count VIII conspiracy admission."
   - *Does the note answer it?* Implicitly, through the observed 2/3 misfire. It could make the point sharper. The criterion's object is a fact, not a proposition, and Opus's note states that fact. The judges never see the justification, so they cannot narrow the text to its intended purpose. The judge wrapper reads only: "Answer Pass only if the response DOES commit this failure. … Failure criterion: Mentions that Patton pled guilty in state court to intimidation with a dangerous weapon" (`opus55-low/reward-details.json`). Claude Sonnet 5 said so directly: "Because the document does mention this fact, the failure criterion is triggered." The item 13 stress test drew the same distinction: a "Mentions" penalty aimed at a record fact misfires far more readily than one aimed at a legal conclusion.

4. **The second blind auditor found no problem.** Claude Opus 5.5's blind report checked criterion 23 against the rendered plea and said "[t]he plea therefore supports criteria 19, 20 and 23 as written," listing it among criteria "checked against the record and law with no defect found" (`tasks/lrb-vicar-trial-jury-instructions-assignment-1/claude-opus-5-5-audit.md`, Coverage).
   - *Does the note answer it?* Not needed. Opus checked whether the criterion's factual premise was accurate (it is), not whether its scope was too broad. One model's silence does not answer Sol's reason, and the protocol scores AI reasoning on whether any flagging model's reason holds.

5. **Is the penalty doing legitimate work that the note overlooks?** Arguably yes, in one respect. A response that cited the intimidation plea as the felony underlying Patton's felon status would be wrong: the Count XX petition is dated 11/18/20 (PDF p. 1), after the May 10, 2020 shooting. A response that used the plea as proof of a May 10 intimidation offense would collide with criteria 28–29. The criterion catches those errors. But the narrower fix Sol proposed (penalize misdating, or relying on the April 13 plea as proof about May 10) would catch them too, without penalizing accurate mentions. This supports the call: the defect is breadth, not purpose.

## Stronger arguments for the call

- **The judges cannot see the limiting justification.** This is the decisive structural point, and the note omits it. The justification ("Because that involved a different shooting, it's not relevant…") shows the author meant to penalize *reliance*. The judge panel, however, receives only the criterion text in a "DOES commit this failure" wrapper. Applied as written, which is the protocol's test, it penalizes any mention.
- **The plea document builds in the collision.** Both counts appear in one case, with one agreement and consecutive sentences, and the agreement naming both counts is printed on the Count VIII petition the rubric wants cited (PDF p. 5). So reproducing the plea accurately, or pin-citing pp. 4–6 while explaining why pp. 1–3 are excluded, triggers the penalty. Luna avoided it only by not discussing the document's structure, and it pin-cited the wrong pages ("Patton State Plea.pdf at 3–4" for the Count VIII admission, which is on PDF p. 5).
- **The flagged behavior is the skill the rubric says it is testing.** John's note says the rubric "seems intended to test the model's ability to distinguish the events." Opus's Drafting Note 2 does exactly that, and the penalty fires because it does. A criterion that penalizes the response for showing the competence it was written to reward is misgrading on the point it covers.
- **The companion penalties show the rubric could have drafted this narrowly.** Criteria 28 ("Mentions that Patton was committing the crime of intimidation … on May 10, 2020") and 29 ("States that Patton's commission of intimidation … precludes a justification instruction") are tied to the May 10 event and to reliance. Criterion 23 alone has no date and no reliance element.
- **The real appeal points the same way.** The Eighth Circuit's opinion in *United States v. Mallory*, 104 F.4th 15 (8th Cir. 2024), resolves the justification issue without mentioning either state plea. The point of the case is that the April 13 incident is irrelevant, not that mentioning it is error.

## Errors in the note

- **"Prior convictions" / "priors" is imprecise.** The Count XX petition was entered November 18, 2020 (PDF p. 1, handwritten "11/18/20"; e-file stamp 2020 NOV 18), after the May 10, 2020 shooting. It was not a prior conviction when the shooting occurred, and the record does not show that it was the predicate for Patton's felon-in-possession plea (Facts.pdf p. 10, Count 6). The record also contains petitions to plead guilty, not a judgment. "Separate state guilty plea" is accurate; "priors" is not.
- **The "factual background" rationale does not fit the assignment.** The supervisor had already drafted the facts section (prompt), and Facts.pdf does not mention April 13. This is a judgment point rather than a factual error, but the note should drop it or narrow it (see objection 2).
- **Paraphrase of Opus's note.** The note says Opus decided not to rely on the other incident "because it was unrelated." Opus wrote that Count XX "covers a *different* event, a shooting on April 13, 2020. I rely only on the Count VIII admission." The inference is fair but not a quotation. The sources line quotes it correctly.
- **Missing locator.** The Facts.pdf quotation ("on May 10, 2020, two more shootings occurred") is accurate but has no page. It is at PDF p. 5 (printed p. 5), and the quoted sentence begins "Four days later, …".
- **Verified as accurate:** Count XX caption, "Intimidation with a Dangerous Weapon," § 708.6 (PDF p. 1); the April 13, 2020 admission (PDF p. 2); the May 10, 2020 Count VIII admission (PDF p. 5). ## Defect basis and environment origin

**Basis: Professional judgment.** The criterion's factual premise is true: Patton did petition to plead guilty to intimidation with a dangerous weapon (PDF pp. 1–3). The defect is that it treats an accurate, sometimes useful statement as a failure. Whether a given mention is useful or gratuitous is a lawyer's judgment, not an objective contradiction of the record or law. Of the protocol's grounds, the closest is (c): in effect, the criterion requires the solver to omit an accurate record fact, which the instructions never ask and a competent lawyer would not infer as a rule.

**Origin: No.** The scanned plea is legible and internally consistent: one case number, one agreement, two dated admissions. Nothing in the supplied materials causes the misgrade.

**Environment: Correct.** I found no objective error in this assignment's materials that bears on the criterion. Small issues do not rise to the protocol's level. The prompt's "Defendant Raekwon's Patton's" is a typo. The statute printouts labeled "(2020)" are 2026 prints, with the cited provisions unchanged (citation check, limits). The scanned plea has no text layer, but rendering it is something the role prompt anticipates. Item 21 (same assignment) also records Correct.

## Suggested revision of the note

> The rubric penalizes (−5) any response that "[m]entions that Patton pled guilty in state court to intimidation with a dangerous weapon." Its justification shows the real concern: the intimidation plea "involved a different shooting" (April 13, 2020) and is "not relevant" to the May 10, 2020 shooting on appeal. That is right, and penalizing a brief that *relies* on the April 13 plea would be fine. But the criterion penalizes any mention, and the judges see only the criterion text ("Answer Pass only if the response DOES commit this failure"), not the justification. The plea makes a mention likely in careful work. It is one two-count document in Polk County No. FECR338165: Count XX (intimidation, April 13) on PDF pp. 1–3 and Count VIII (the May 10 conspiracy the rubric rewards citing, criteria 19–21) on pp. 4–6, with one agreement "to plead guilty to Counts VIII and XX" (PDF pp. 2, 5). Opus was penalized 2/3 for doing exactly what the rubric seems meant to test: Drafting Note 2 told the supervisor that Count XX "covers a *different* event, a shooting on April 13, 2020. I rely only on the Count VIII admission," and asked her to confirm Count VIII is in the record. A careful associate should flag that, and so should a brief that pin-cites pp. 4–6 and explains why pp. 1–3 are excluded. The low point value does not change the verdict: the criterion misgraded a competent response on the point it covers, and I have treated small criteria the same way elsewhere (items 14, 17–20). The narrower companion penalties (criteria 28–29, tied to May 10 and to reliance) show the fix: penalize relying on the April 13 plea, not mentioning it.
