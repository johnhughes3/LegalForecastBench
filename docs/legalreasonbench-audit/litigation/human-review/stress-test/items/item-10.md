## Strongest objections

**1. Stipulation (a) and the "prove" framing make the criterion a deliberate, defensible point about the scope of review, and the note does not address them.** The assignment 2 prompt tells the solver to "[t]reat the following as true ...: (a) reasonable minds can disagree as to how comparable the four comparator cases are to Braddock's case." Question 1 puts "prove" in quotation marks ("the arbitrator's requirement that MPD 'prove' the comparability"). Under *Douglas* the reviewing tribunal asks whether the agency "did conscientiously consider the relevant factors and did strike a responsible balance within tolerable limits of reasonableness" (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf, printed pp. 332–33). The principle of "like penalties for like offenses" does not require "mathematical rigidity or perfect consistency" (p. 333; see also p. 346 n.84). Read together, the stipulation and *Douglas* say that MPD's comparability conclusion was one reasonable minds could reach. That is why the arbitrator's demand for *proof* of comparability (Award ¶ 7: "failed to carry its burden to prove comparability") went beyond reasonableness review. The rubric applies the same theory across the matter: criterion 1 (*Douglas* reasonableness standard), penalty criterion 7 (an arbitrator free to choose a penalty without deference), and assignment 5's criterion 8 ("the arbitrator's demand that MPD prove comparator comparability applied Douglas beyond the tolerable limits scope of review"). On that reading, criterion 4 is a judgment call where the rubric takes a reasonable side, which the protocol treats as Not defective. **Answered by the note?** No. The note never mentions stipulation (a), the reasonableness standard, or the companion criteria.

**2. John's own concessions undercut "Unambiguous objective error."** The note says the proposition "is perhaps narrowly correct as a general proposition of law" and that "*Douglas* does not expressly require an agency to prove comparable cases every time." Its affirmative claim is practical: failure to prove the comparators "leaves MPD highly vulnerable." A criterion that is literally true but incomplete or misleading on these facts is a professional-judgment problem. Neither the record, the law, nor arithmetic contradicts it. Both blind models and the reconciliation called it arguable because the narrower reading, that no upload makes strict comparability a threshold element, is defensible (reconciliation R2). **Answered?** Partly. The note acknowledges the narrow reading and says that in context it becomes "objectively inaccurate." That reasoning supports professional judgment, not objective error.

**3. How the comparators were handled is undisputed: all four were terminated (comparator_discipline_cases.pdf ¶¶ 1–5), and the arbitrator does not question that (Award ¶ 7). The dispute is whether those officers were *similarly situated*, and stipulation (a) says reasonable minds can differ on that. MPD did produce "evidence of the facts on which selection of the penalty was based" (*Douglas*, p. 334): the Britt memorandum. What remained was a contestable judgment. **Answered?** No.

## Stronger arguments for the call

- **Douglas says more than the note quotes.** On printed p. 334, the agency's decision can be sustained "only if the agency establishes the facts on which that decision rests by the requisite standard of proof." Deference to the agency "cannot have the effect of shifting to the appellant the burden," because "it is the agency's obligation to present all evidence necessary to support each element of its decision." Penalty selection "is a distinct element ... properly within its burden of persuasion." The Final Notice's factor-six finding rests on a factual premise, that the four were "similarly situated members" (¶ 19). That is the strongest textual basis for John's position.
- **The penalty was contested, which triggers the heavier burden.** *Douglas* says a prima facie case suffices only "[w]hen no issue has been raised concerning the penalty." When "the appellant challenges the severity of the penalty," the agency must "present such further evidence" (p. 334). The stipulated arbitral issue was "is the penalty of termination appropriate?" (Award ¶ 2).
- **Douglas says equity is preserved "in relation to genuinely similar cases"** (p. 333). Its notice passage treats "aggravating factors on which the agency intends to rely for imposition of an enhanced penalty" as matters the agency must disclose and explain (p. 331). Both passages support the view that an aggravating comparator rationale is the agency's to support.
- **The regulation points the same way.** § 1606.2 requires managers to "be prepared to demonstrate" consideration of factor (f), and § 1606.4 requires the factor analysis in the decision.
- **The prompt asks for the weakness.** Question 2 asks for "the weakest point in [MPD's] position." The agency's *Douglas* burden on a comparator rationale it chose to rely on is the obvious candidate. A response that develops it fully is doing what the prompt asked and risks losing 26 of 88 points.
- **The native run shows the misgrade happening.** GPT-6 Luna's answer said the arbitrator's "prove" language "can be defended as applying the agency's ultimate persuasion burden," while also criticizing the rigid comparator-identity test. `anthropic/claude-sonnet-5` failed it on criterion 4 for that concession ("implying that some burden of proof regarding comparators does exist under Douglas"). The answer still met on a 2/3 vote, but the split shows that an accurate *Douglas* analysis can lose the criterion's points.
- **John's distinction of the real case is sound.** In *Thomas*, the panel "had not cited comparable cases" (2022 slip op. at 5). Here, MPD made comparators an affirmative aggravating ground. The real-world rule that the agency's burden is "triggered by the appellant's initial showing" is about an employee's disparate-penalty claim, not an agency's own aggravating rationale.

Taken together, these points make a strong case that the criterion can misgrade a competent response. They do not show that its literal proposition is false. That is why Arguable, or Defective on a professional-judgment basis, fits better than an objective error.

## Defect basis and environment origin

**Basis.** If the item stays Defective, the basis is Professional judgment. The criterion's sentence is literally defensible. *Douglas* places the ultimate burden on "the appropriateness of the penalty," not on comparability as such, and stipulation (a) makes comparability a matter of reasonable disagreement. The defect is that the criterion is categorical and heavily weighted (26 of 88) on a point where a careful litigator could reasonably say that MPD, having made the comparators an aggravating ground, had to substantiate them under *Douglas* p. 334. That is "rejecting an approach a careful litigator would adopt on this record," which is a professional-judgment defect. The record, the law, and the arithmetic do not contradict the criterion.

**Origin.** No. The supplied materials are not wrong on this point. The task author changed the real record on purpose, putting comparators in MPD's Final Notice, and the criterion does not account for that change.

## Suggested revision of the note

Written for the recorded Defective verdict, with the basis amended to Professional judgment. If the disclosed disagreement is adopted, the final sentence is the one to change.

> The rubric wants the burden analysis to state that "no uploaded authority requires MPD to prove the comparability of its comparator cases." Read narrowly, that is defensible: *Douglas v. Veterans Administration*, 5 M.S.P.B. 313, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981), reviews an agency's penalty only for whether it struck "a responsible balance within tolerable limits of reasonableness," says "like penalties for like offenses" does not require "mathematical rigidity" (5 M.S.P.B. at 332–33), and the prompt stipulates that "reasonable minds can disagree" about how comparable the four cases are. But the same uploaded opinion puts "the ultimate burden ... upon the agency to persuade the Board of the appropriateness of the penalty imposed" (*id.* at 333), sustains the decision only if "the agency establishes the facts on which that decision rests," and requires "further evidence" once "the appellant challenges the severity of the penalty" (*id.* at 334), as Braddock did (Award ¶ 2). And on this record MPD itself put the comparators at issue: the Final Notice found factor six aggravating because the four officers were "similarly situated members" who were terminated (Final Notice ¶ 19). A careful associate could therefore conclude that MPD had to substantiate the similarity of the comparators it chose to rely on, and that this is the weakest point in MPD's position the prompt asks for. The criterion, worth 26 of 88 points and stated categorically, fails that answer; one native judge failed GPT-6 Luna for conceding that the arbitrator's "prove" language "can be defended as applying the agency's ultimate persuasion burden." The criterion appears modeled on the real *Thomas* case (*MPD v. PERB*, 282 A.3d 598 (D.C. 2022); No. 24-CV-0573 (D.C. May 21, 2026)), where MPD argued "no such proof" was required because its panel "had not cited comparable cases" and the union, not MPD, produced the comparators. The task changed that fact, and the criterion does not account for the change. Because the criterion's sentence is literally defensible and the defect lies in rejecting a reasonable reading of *Douglas* on these facts, the basis is professional judgment, not an unambiguous objective error.
