## Bottom line

1. **Public policy exception: not viable.** The record contains no positive law or legal precedent establishing a "well defined and dominant" policy that requires an officer to be *discharged* rather than suspended. Misco squarely forecloses a court from overturning an award because the arbitrator imposed discipline short of discharge.
2. **Contrary-to-law exception: a real argument, but a long shot.** The argument is that Arbitrator Larrabee expressly adopted Douglas and 6-B DCMR § 1606.2, then disregarded Douglas's express limit on when a reviewer may substitute a penalty. FOP/DOC recognizes that theory. Its weaknesses are serious:
   - the arbitrator recited the correct standard;
   - Douglas itself says penalty selection "is a matter of judgment and discretion, not a question of law per se";
   - the DCCA reviews PERB's decision only for whether it is "rationally indefensible."
3. **The supplemental comparator (Sgt. Kucera) cannot be used by either side on review.** In practical terms, though, it weakens MPD's equities, its public-policy narrative, and the value of a remand.
4. **Recommendation: MPD should not petition for review.** If MPD nonetheless decides to file for institutional reasons, the petition should be limited to the contrary-to-law argument, framed as described in Part IV.

## II. The public policy exception

### A. Question presented

Under D.C. Code § 1-605.02(6), is the Award "on its face . . . contrary to . . . public policy" because it reinstates to an armed position an officer whom the arbitrator found used deadly force against an unarmed man without an objectively reasonable belief of an imminent threat? And would the D.C. Court of Appeals hold that PERB was "rationally indefensible" in concluding otherwise?

### B. Governing standard

1. **The statutory text.** PERB may "modif[y] or set aside or remand[]" an award "only if" one of the following applies:
   - the arbitrator lacked or exceeded jurisdiction;
   - "the award on its face is contrary to law and public policy"; or
   - the award was procured by fraud or collusion.

   § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6). This paragraph is "the exclusive method" for reviewing an arbitrator's decision. *Id.*
2. **How the DCCA reviews PERB.** The court "will not easily disturb a decision of the PERB." Unless "rationally indefensible," a PERB decision "must stand." Fraternal Ord. of Police/Dep't of Corr. Lab. Comm. v. D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd., 973 A.2d 174 (D.C. 2009) (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf, p. 3, ¶ 1). Absent a "clear violation of law — one evident on the face" of the award, PERB lacks "authority to substitute its judgment for the arbitrator's." *Id.* at p. 3, ¶ 2 (quoting 901 A.2d at 789).
3. **Where the policy must come from.** The policy must be "ascertained by reference to the laws and legal precedents and not from general considerations of supposed public interests." *Id.* at p. 5, ¶ 2 (quoting W.R. Grace & Co. v. Local Union 759, 461 U.S. 757, 766 (1983)). An award "contrary to a specific law ipso facto may be said to be contrary to the public policy that the law embodies." *Id.*
4. **What the policy must look like.** It must be "explicit," "well defined and dominant." W.R. Grace & Co. v. Local Union 759, 461 U.S. 757, 766 (1983) (wr_grace_v_rubber_workers.pdf, p. 766, ¶ 2). Even an "important public policy" does not defeat an award unless enforcing the award actually "compromise[s]" that policy. *Id.* at 766–67 (wr_grace_v_rubber_workers.pdf, p. 766, ¶ 3; p. 767, ¶ 1).
5. **The Misco refinements.**
   - The policy "must be properly framed," and "the violation of such a policy must be clearly shown." United Paperworkers Int'l Union v. Misco, Inc., 484 U.S. 29, 43 (1987) (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf, p. 43, ¶ 1).
   - A refusal to enforce "must rest on more than speculation or assumption." *Id.* at 44 (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf, p. 44, ¶ 2).
   - A reviewing court may not do its own "factfinding." *Id.* at 44–45 (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf, p. 44, ¶ 3; p. 45, ¶ 1).
   - Most important here: where an arbitrator "imposed discipline short of discharge because he found as a factual matter that [the employee] could be trusted," the court "could not upset the award because of its own view that public policy . . . was threatened." *Id.* at 45 (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf, p. 45, ¶ 1).
   - Reinstatement "in his old job or in an equivalent one" weighs against vacatur where it is "by no means clear" that the employee "would pose a serious threat to the asserted public policy in every job for which he was qualified." *Id.*
6. **PERB's own view.** PERB treats this exception as "extremely narrow." PERB Decision, p. 2, ¶ 5.

### C. Application to the record

**1. Stating the policy.** The strongest framing is that the District has an explicit, well-defined, and dominant policy against police use of deadly force without an objectively reasonable belief of imminent threat. Under that framing, an award returning an officer to armed duty after an express finding that he used such force violates the policy.

The record supports the *factual* half of that framing on the face of the Award:

- no reasonable officer "would have perceived an imminent threat," Award, p. 1, ¶ 4;
- Braddock "never saw a weapon," Award, p. 1, ¶ 3;
- reinstatement is "to his position of record," Award, p. 2, ¶ 10.

**2. Source of the policy: the decisive gap.**

- The only articulation of the deadly-force standard in the record is General Order 120.21, Attachment A, Part A-12, an internal MPD directive. Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 12.
- None of the uploaded materials contains a statute, regulation, or judicial decision establishing that standard as District public policy.
- W.R. Grace and FOP/DOC require the policy to come from "laws and legal precedents." W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766 (p. 766, ¶ 2); FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2.
- Misco rejected a policy that was "firmly rooted in common sense" because the court below "made no attempt to review existing laws and legal precedents." Misco, 484 U.S. at 44 (p. 44, ¶ 1).
- On this record, MPD cannot meet that requirement. An employer's own work rule is the kind of contractual or managerial norm that the parties assigned to the arbitrator. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4.

**3. Even a properly sourced policy would not require discharge.**

- The Award does not condone the shooting. It sustains cause, affirms the finding, and imposes a 45-day unpaid suspension and a permanent disciplinary record. Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 8, 10.
- A policy *against* unjustified deadly force does not, without more, require *termination* as the only lawful response. Nothing in the record says it does.
- This is the scenario Misco addressed: the arbitrator found misconduct "yet imposed discipline short of discharge." A reviewing court "could not upset the award because of its own view that public policy . . . was threatened." Misco, 484 U.S. at 45 (p. 45, ¶ 1).
- The arbitrator's prediction that a suspension "would likely have sufficed to deter future misconduct by the Grievant" is a factual finding, however debatable. Award, p. 2, ¶ 8. Misco forbids a reviewing body from replacing it with a contrary inference. Misco, 484 U.S. at 44–45 (p. 44, ¶ 3; p. 45, ¶ 1).

**4. "Clearly shown" violation and "every job" analysis.**

- **The record supports MPD:**
  - the deciding official found MPD "cannot deploy an officer whose judgment in the use of deadly force has failed this gravely," Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 18;
  - the arbitrator upheld that factor-5 finding, Award, p. 2, ¶ 6.
- **What MPD still cannot show:**
  - The record does not show that reinstatement "to his position of record" necessarily means armed patrol duty.
  - It does not show that Braddock would pose a serious threat in *every* position for which he is qualified. Misco, 484 U.S. at 45 (p. 45, ¶ 1).
  - MPD retains its ordinary authority over assignments. Any claim that reinstatement will inevitably produce another unjustified shooting rests on "speculation or assumption." Misco, 484 U.S. at 44 (p. 44, ¶ 2).

**5. Preservation.** MPD did raise public policy before PERB. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2. So the bar against unpreserved objections in § 1-617.13(b), FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2, does not apply. But the DCCA will review only the policy argument as MPD framed it below.

**6. Deference to PERB.** PERB held that MPD identified no "clear violation of public policy on the face of the Award." PERB Decision, p. 2, ¶ 5. Given the missing positive-law source and Misco's holding about discipline short of discharge, the DCCA will not find that conclusion "rationally indefensible." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1.

### D. Effect of the supplemental comparator

- **Formally: none.** Review is confined to the Award "on its face." § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6); FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 2.
  - The DCCA will not consider material PERB did not rely on, even when it *was* in the agency record. FOP/DOC, pp. 5–6, n. 2.
  - Kucera was never in either record. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 4.
  - A negotiated settlement is not a "law[] [or] legal precedent[]," so it neither supplies nor defeats a policy source. W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766 (p. 766, ¶ 2).
- **Practically: damaging.** MPD itself kept on duty, under a suspension and probation, a supervisor whose off-duty discharge at an unarmed man was found outside policy. It proposed only 60 days. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶¶ 2–3.
  - That is very hard to reconcile with a position that the "dominant" public policy *requires* removal for an outside-policy off-duty discharge.
  - If the case surfaces through a remand, a later proceeding, or FOP's own sources, MPD's public-policy position would look inconsistent with its own conduct. That would damage its credibility on the contrary-to-law argument as well.
- **Limited distinctions remain.** They do not cure the problem:
  - Kucera faced an actual crime in progress; Mr. Pryor "committed no crime." Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 2; Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 24.
  - Her round missed; Braddock's two rounds nearly killed Mr. Pryor. Final Notice, p. 2, ¶¶ 9–10.
  - The Bell memo does not say she was charged under Part A-12 or that anyone made an objective-reasonableness finding. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 3.

### E. Principal weakness

**The record contains no positive law or legal precedent establishing the asserted policy.** Even if MPD supplied one, Misco holds that an award imposing discipline short of discharge on the arbitrator's factual assessment of the employee cannot be set aside because the reviewer believes the policy is threatened. Misco, 484 U.S. at 43–45 (p. 43, ¶ 1; p. 44, ¶¶ 1–2; p. 45, ¶ 1); W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766 (p. 766, ¶ 2). The Kucera settlement compounds this problem in practice.

**Assessment: not viable.** I would not include this ground in any petition. At most, it could appear as a short, preserved alternative argument confined to the Award's own findings. Even then, it risks diluting the stronger ground.

## III. The contrary-to-law exception

### A. Question presented

Is the Award "on its face . . . contrary to law" under D.C. Code § 1-605.02(6) because the arbitrator expressly adopted Douglas and 6-B DCMR § 1606.2 as the governing standard, then substituted his own penalty without finding that MPD failed to weigh the relevant factors or clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness? Specifically, did he do so by (i) requiring MPD to "prove" comparability on factor 6 and (ii) requiring "evidence" beyond the deciding official's reasoned judgment on factor 12? And was PERB's contrary conclusion "rationally indefensible"?

### B. Governing standard

1. **Facial error and deference.** An award may be set aside only if it is contrary to law "on its face." § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6). PERB has no authority to substitute its judgment "[a]bsent a clear violation of law — one evident on the face" of the award. FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 2. The DCCA defers to PERB's interpretation of the CMPA unless it is "plainly erroneous," and affirms unless PERB's decision is "rationally indefensible." *Id.* at p. 3, ¶ 1.
2. **The recognized theory.** The DCCA upheld, as a permissible reading of § 1-605.02(6), PERB's view that an award is facially contrary to law "where, in arriving at the award, the arbitrator looks to an external law for guidance and purports to apply that law, but overlooks or ignores the law's express provisions." *Id.* at p. 3, ¶ 4. The statute also reaches "a misinterpretation of law by the arbitrator that was apparent 'on its face.'" *Id.* at p. 4, ¶ 2 (quoting 901 A.2d at 787–88).
   - In FOP/DOC, no "interpretation" or "comprehensive analysis" was needed to see the conflict. The arbitrator "overlooked th[e] provision of the very law that he relied upon." *Id.* at p. 4, ¶ 4; p. 5, ¶ 1.
3. **The limit on that theory.** Where an arbitrator "is called upon actually to interpret the law, his interpretation merits deference, because it is the interpretation that the parties 'bargained for.'" *Id.* at p. 6, n. 3. PERB applied the same principle here. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4. Misco adds that a reviewer's conviction that the arbitrator "committed serious error does not suffice." Misco, 484 U.S. at 38 (p. 38, ¶ 1).
4. **Preservation.** Only objections "first urged before the Board" may be considered. FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2 (quoting D.C. Code § 1-617.13(b)).
5. **The external law the arbitrator adopted.**
   - **Douglas's limit on substitution.** A reviewer's role "is not to insist that the balance be struck precisely where [it] would choose to strike it." Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2). Instead, it asks whether the agency "did strike a responsible balance within tolerable limits of reasonableness." "**Only if** the [reviewer] finds that the agency failed to weigh the relevant factors, or that the agency's judgment clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness, is it appropriate . . . to specify how the agency's decision should be corrected." *Id.* at p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1 (emphasis added).
   - **Douglas on consistency.** "[L]ike penalties for like offenses" does not require "mathematical rigidity or perfect consistency." It is to be applied "with practical realism," and "surface consistency should be avoided." *Id.* at p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2.
   - **Douglas on the agency's prima facie case.** The agency makes out a prima facie case with "a concise statement of its reasoning" that is "not . . . inherently irrational." "When no issue has been raised concerning the penalty, such a prima facie case will normally suffice." *Id.* at p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
   - **DCMR § 1606.2.** It requires managers to "demonstrate that the [twelve] factors were considered," including "[c]onsistency of the penalty" (f), "[p]otential for . . . rehabilitation" (j), and "adequacy and effectiveness of alternative sanctions" (l). DCMR, p. 1, § 1606.2(f), (j), (l). Section 1606.3 requires that the factors be "considered and balanced." DCMR, p. 1, § 1606.3.

### C. Application to the record

**1. The arbitrator adopted Douglas and § 1606.2 as the governing law.** This is the FOP/DOC predicate: an external law the arbitrator "looks to . . . for guidance and purports to apply." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 4.

- He stated that review was "not de novo." He adopted the "tolerable limits of reasonableness" test and cited both authorities. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5.
- That brings the Award within the FOP/DOC theory. The question becomes whether he "overlook[ed] or ignore[d]" Douglas's express provisions. FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 4.

**2. The "[o]nly if" limit: the lead argument.**

- **What the arbitrator found.** MPD weighed ten of twelve factors "within tolerable limits of reasonableness." Award, p. 2, ¶ 6. The deciding official addressed all twelve. Final Notice, pp. 2–4, ¶¶ 13–26.
- **Why that triggers the limit.** MPD therefore did not "fail[] to weigh the relevant factors." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1. The only remaining route to substitution was a finding that MPD's *overall* judgment "clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness." *Id.*
- **What the arbitrator did instead.** He held that because two factor-level conclusions were outside "tolerable limits," the "penalty of termination cannot stand." He then struck his own balance: "[w]eighing the sustained findings on the remaining ten factors against the failures on factors six and twelve, I conclude that a 45-day suspension . . . is the appropriate penalty." Award, p. 2, ¶ 9.
- **Why that is error on the face of the Award.** That is precisely the re-balancing Douglas forbids, "where the [reviewer] would choose to strike it." Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2. The arbitrator never found that termination, on the ten factors he upheld (all aggravating, including the "utmost seriousness" of the offense), clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness. Award, p. 2, ¶ 6; Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 14.
  - His own framing, that a single deficient factor means the penalty "cannot stand as issued," is a rule found nowhere in Douglas. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5.
  - This parallels FOP/DOC: the arbitrator invoked the governing law but overlooked the provision that controlled the remedy. FOP/DOC, p. 4, ¶ 4; p. 5, ¶ 1.

**3. Factor 6: a proof standard applied to a reasonableness judgment.**

- **The standard is internally inconsistent.** The arbitrator demanded that MPD "prove" the comparators were similarly situated and faulted it for failing "to carry its burden to prove comparability." Award, p. 2, ¶ 7. Under assumption (a), reasonable minds can disagree about comparability. A judgment on which reasonable minds can disagree is, by definition, within "tolerable limits of reasonableness," the test the arbitrator himself adopted. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5. The arbitrator could reach the opposite conclusion only by applying a proof standard rather than his own stated standard. That inconsistency appears on the face of the Award.
- **Douglas's consistency provision was ignored.** The arbitrator disqualified each comparator on a single surface feature (on duty, fatality, prior complaints). Award, p. 2, ¶ 7. Douglas calls for "practical realism" and warns against "surface consistency." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2.
  - All four comparators involved sworn members who used unjustified force involving a firearm or serious bodily injury, and all four were terminated. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 1–5.
  - Two of them, Sadler (no prior discipline, no fatality) and Voss (one unrelated prior complaint, no fatality), are not touched by the fatality or force-history distinctions. The Award does not address them individually.
  - The Award also miscounts the on-duty comparators: it says two, but there were three. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7; Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 2, 3, 5.
- **No disparity claim was raised.** Under assumption (b), neither Braddock nor the union alleged disparate treatment before the hearing. Under Douglas, when "no issue has been raised concerning the penalty," the agency's prima facie case "will normally suffice." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2. Under assumption (c), Braddock knew of the four comparators before the decision and had a "fair opportunity" to contest them. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), ¶ 2. The arbitrator raised the comparability issue himself and then put a proof burden on MPD to answer it.
- **A necessary qualification.** Douglas places on the agency the burden of establishing the *facts* behind its penalty, expressly including "consistency with other agency actions." Douglas, p. 12 (printed 324), ¶ 4. It also places the "ultimate burden" of persuasion on the agency. *Id.* at p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 3. And it allows the reviewer to raise "genuine issues of justice or equity" on its own initiative. *Id.* at p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
  - MPD must therefore argue that it **proved the facts** (the four cases existed and ended in termination, which is undisputed). The arbitrator's error was converting the *evaluative judgment* of comparability into a fact MPD had to "prove."
  - MPD must **not** argue that it bore no burden on factor 6.

**4. Factor 12: demanding "evidence" for a reasoned predictive judgment.**

- **The arbitrator demanded evidence where Douglas requires reasoning.** He rejected the deciding official's conclusion because MPD "offered no evidence . . . beyond the deciding official's assertion." Award, p. 2, ¶ 8. Douglas requires only "a concise statement of [the agency's] reasoning" that is not "inherently irrational." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2. The deciding official considered "a suspension of significant length" and explained why it would not deter. Final Notice, p. 4, ¶ 25. That is a reasoned statement, not a bare assertion.
- **The finding conflicts with his own sustained findings.** He upheld factor 5 (supervisors' confidence "lost") and factor 10 (Braddock "accepts no responsibility"; rehabilitation potential "poor"). Award, p. 2, ¶ 6; Final Notice, p. 3, ¶¶ 18, 23. A finding that a suspension "would likely have sufficed to deter future misconduct by the Grievant," Award, p. 2, ¶ 8, cannot easily be squared with an upheld finding that the Grievant accepts no responsibility and is unlikely to be rehabilitated.
- **He substituted his own prediction.** His statement that a suspension "would likely have sufficed" is his own prediction offered in place of the agency's. Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2.

**5. The Award is not saved by § 1600.2(g), but that provision must be addressed.**

- Chapter 16 of 6-B DCMR, by its terms, does not apply to "[s]worn members of the Metropolitan Police Department." DCMR, p. 1, § 1600.2(g).
- FOP may argue that § 1606.2 therefore is not "law" governing Braddock.
- **Response.** The FOP/DOC theory does not depend on whether the external law independently binds the parties. It turns on the arbitrator having "look[ed] to" that law and "purport[ed] to apply" it. FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 4. In FOP/DOC itself, it was disputed whether the federal Back Pay Act applied to District employees at all. *Id.* at p. 6, n. 4. Both the Final Notice and the Award expressly applied § 1606.2 and Douglas. Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 13; Award, p. 2, ¶ 5.

**6. PERB's decision is unreasoned.**

- PERB disposed of the Douglas arguments in one paragraph, characterizing them as "[m]ere disagreement." It did not engage with the "[o]nly if" limit. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4.
- It misstated the penalty as a 40-day suspension. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 1.
- It added that the Award "draws its essence from the parties' agreement." PERB Decision, p. 2, ¶ 6. That is not the § 1-605.02(6) test for facial contrariety to law.
- The DCCA reviews PERB on the grounds PERB actually gave. FOP/DOC, pp. 5–6, n. 2. These defects support a request for remand to PERB for a reasoned application of the FOP/DOC standard.
- **Caution:** the misstatement of the penalty is very likely harmless, and the court may see the decision as adequate given PERB's narrow review.

**7. Preservation.** MPD argued factor 6 and factor 12 below. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2.

- The "[o]nly if" argument is closely related to MPD's factor-12 "substitution" argument. Still, FOP will contend that the specific argument about overall re-balancing in ¶ 9 of the Award was not "first urged before the Board." FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2.
- MPD's PERB filing is not in our file. Before committing to this argument, we must review it to confirm how the argument was presented.

### D. Effect of the supplemental comparator

- **Formally: none.** Kucera cannot be considered.
  - The question is whether the Award is contrary to law "on its face." § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6); FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 2.
  - The case was never before the arbitrator or PERB. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 4.
  - The DCCA will not look beyond the grounds and record PERB relied on. FOP/DOC, pp. 5–6, n. 2.
  - Weighing Kucera against Braddock would be "an exercise in factfinding" reserved to the arbitrator. Misco, 484 U.S. at 44–45 (p. 44, ¶ 3; p. 45, ¶ 1).
  - The lead "[o]nly if" argument and the "prove" standard argument both turn on the *method* shown in the Award's text, not on the comparators, so they are formally unaffected.
- **Practically: three significant harms.**
  - **Equities.** Kucera is the closest known comparator. Like Braddock, she was off duty, at home, out of jurisdiction, caused no fatality, and fired at an unarmed man without calling 9-1-1. MPD proposed 60 days, and she received 40. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶¶ 2–3. The arbitrator's 45 days sits squarely within that range. Award, p. 2, ¶ 9. A reviewing body that senses the outcome was fair is more likely to label any error "misapplication" rather than a facial violation of law, which is the label PERB has already used. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4.
  - **Remand risk.** The realistic best outcome is a remand. If the matter returns to the arbitrator, he may invite "additional information relating to possible alternative penalties," Douglas, p. 12 (printed 324), ¶ 1, and raise "genuine issues of justice or equity" on his own, *id.* at p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2. Misco contemplates that further facts will be found by the arbitrator. Misco, 484 U.S. at 45 (p. 45, ¶ 1). Kucera would then become FOP's best exhibit. A "win" could end with the same penalty, this time resting on a sounder record.
  - **Factor 12 rhetoric.** MPD's general-deterrence rationale, that a lesser penalty would signal the conduct is "survivable," Final Notice, p. 4, ¶ 25, is undercut by MPD's own suspension of Kucera seven months before Braddock's shooting. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 3.
- **What still distinguishes Kucera.** None of these points can be argued on review, but they matter on any remand:
  - Mr. Pryor "committed no crime," while Kucera faced a crime in progress. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 24; Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 2.
  - Braddock caused near-fatal injuries, while Kucera's round missed. Final Notice, p. 2, ¶¶ 9–10.
  - Braddock has a prior reprimand; Kucera had no prior discipline. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 16; Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 3.
  - Braddock refuses to accept responsibility. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 23.
  - Kucera's outcome was a negotiated settlement, not an adjudicated penalty. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 3.
  - **Against MPD:** Kucera's supervisory rank is aggravating under Douglas. DCMR, p. 1, § 1606.2(b).
- **No waiver argument reaches Kucera.** Assumption (c) covers only the four comparators. Nothing suggests Braddock knew of a settlement that MPD's own search missed.
- **Drafting constraints for any filing.**
  - Do not describe the four comparators as MPD's complete practice.
  - Do not argue that MPD uniformly terminates in such cases.
  - Before filing, obtain separate advice, outside this record, on any disclosure obligations concerning Kucera.

### E. Principal weakness

The arbitrator **recited the correct Douglas standard** ("not de novo," "tolerable limits of reasonableness"). Award, p. 2, ¶ 5. Douglas itself states that "[t]he appropriateness of a penalty is a matter of judgment and discretion, not a question of law per se." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), n. 72.

- **The obstacle.** That combination lets PERB, and the DCCA deferring to PERB, treat every error MPD identifies as a misapplication of a correctly stated standard to the evidence. That is "[m]ere disagreement with the Arbitrator's interpretation of Douglas or with his evaluation of the evidence," which the parties "bargained for." PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4; FOP/DOC, p. 6, n. 3; Misco, 484 U.S. at 38 (p. 38, ¶ 1).
- **Why FOP/DOC is distinguishable.** There, the law's command (offset interim earnings) was unambiguous and required no "interpretation" or "comprehensive analysis." FOP/DOC, p. 4, ¶ 4. Douglas's "clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness" is itself an evaluative standard. *Douglas*, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.
- **The added burden-allocation problem.** Douglas puts on the agency both the factual burden on "consistency with other agency actions" and the ultimate burden of persuasion. Douglas, p. 12 (printed 324), ¶ 4; p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 3. FOP will argue that the arbitrator's "burden to prove" language simply tracks Douglas.
- **Compounding factors.** MPD must show that PERB's conclusion was "rationally indefensible," not merely wrong. FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1. And the Kucera case makes the Award's result look fair.

**Assessment: colorable but unlikely to succeed.** The "[o]nly if" argument is the strongest available point and is grounded in the text of the Award. I estimate the chance of obtaining vacatur or a remand at low, and the chance of ultimately restoring the termination as lower still.

## IV. Recommendation

**MPD should not petition for review of PERB Opinion No. 1904.** My reasons:

1. **The public policy ground is not viable.**
   - There is no positive-law source for the policy in the record.
   - Misco bars vacatur of discipline short of discharge on these facts.
   - Kucera undermines the claim that MPD's own policy demands removal.

   Misco, 484 U.S. at 43–45; W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766; Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶¶ 2–3.
2. **The contrary-to-law ground is colorable but unlikely to prevail.**
   - The DCCA asks only whether PERB was "rationally indefensible." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1.
   - The arbitrator recited the correct standard, and Douglas characterizes penalty selection as judgment rather than law. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5; Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), n. 72.
3. **Even success carries a serious downside.** The best realistic outcome is a remand. A remand could reopen the record and bring in the Kucera case, which supports a penalty in the 40–60-day range, close to the 45 days already imposed. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 3; Award, p. 2, ¶ 9.
4. **Litigation risk.** A petition that implicitly presents MPD's comparator practice as uniform, while MPD knows of Kucera, creates candor and credibility risk that outweighs the modest chance of success.

**Better uses of MPD's resources:**

- Comply with the Award. Exercise MPD's ordinary management authority over Braddock's assignment, consistent with the Award's reinstatement "to his position of record." Award, p. 2, ¶ 10.
- Fix the comparator process that produced this record:
  - search both the adverse-action and settlement databases;
  - use a current lookback window rather than one ending more than two years before the decision. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 1; Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 1.
- In future Final Notices, address each Douglas factor with articulated reasons and record support, especially factors 6 and 12. That will limit an arbitrator's ability to call the agency's conclusions unsupported "assertion[s]." Award, p. 2, ¶ 8.

**If MPD nonetheless decides to petition for institutional reasons**, it should do the following:

- Rest the petition solely on the contrary-to-law ground, led by the "[o]nly if" argument. Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 6, 9; Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1; FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 4.
- Frame factor 6 as an error in the standard applied (proof versus reasonableness), consistent with assumptions (a)–(c), not as a claim that MPD's practice is uniform.
- Frame factor 12 around the arbitrator's inconsistency with his own factor-10 finding.
- Seek remand to PERB for a reasoned decision.
- Omit the public-policy argument, or reduce it to a short preserved alternative.
- Before filing:
  - confirm the filing deadline and any procedural requirements from sources outside this record;
  - review MPD's PERB filing for preservation;
  - convert the FOP/DOC citations to official A.2d pincites;
  - resolve the April 11 / April 17 memo date discrepancy;
  - resolve any disclosure obligations concerning Kucera.
