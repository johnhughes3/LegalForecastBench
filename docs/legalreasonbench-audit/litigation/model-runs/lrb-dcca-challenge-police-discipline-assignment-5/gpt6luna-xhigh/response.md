# Memorandum

**To:** District of Columbia Metropolitan Police Department  
**From:** Senior Legal Analyst  
**Date:** March 2, 2026  
**Re:** Viability of D.C. Court of Appeals challenge to PERB’s Braddock decision

## Executive conclusion

MPD should **not petition for review**. The public-policy theory is weak: the supplied authorities require an explicit, well-defined, dominant policy grounded in law or precedent and a clear conflict on the face of the award; the attached materials identify no law requiring Officer Braddock’s termination or showing that reinstatement itself compels unlawful conduct. The contrary-to-law theory is colorable, especially as to the arbitrator’s factor-twelve assessment, but the award states the deferential standard it applied, and its result can be characterized as an evidentiary judgment within the expressly submitted penalty issue. Factor six is still less promising. The newly identified Kucera settlement materially weakens MPD’s position on the broader merits but was not before the arbitrator or PERB and cannot establish a facial defect in the award on this review.

## Record posture and citation convention

The arbitration issue expressly included whether termination was appropriate, and the arbitrator sustained cause while reducing termination to a **45-day** suspension. The PERB decision’s opening paragraph describes the suspension as 40 days, but the award itself specifies 45 days and PERB affirmed the award “in all respects.” The discrepancy is not identified as a basis for PERB’s decision and does not appear to affect its analysis. ## 1. Public-policy exception

### Question presented

Whether reinstating Braddock, subject to a 45-day suspension, after the arbitrator sustained the finding that he used unjustified deadly force violates an explicit, well-defined, dominant public policy so clearly that the conflict appears on the face of the award.

### Governing standard

The statute permits PERB to modify, set aside, or remand an arbitration award only in specified circumstances, including when “the award on its face is contrary to law and public policy.” D.C. Code § 1-605.02(6) (2016) (dc_code_1-605.02.pdf), PDF p. 1, subsection (6). Under *W.R. Grace*, the policy must be explicit, well-defined, and dominant, and must be ascertained from laws and legal precedents—not general considerations of supposed public interests. *W.R. Grace & Co. v. Local Union 759*, 461 U.S. 757, 766 (1983) (wr_grace_v_rubber_workers.pdf, reporter p. 766 ¶ 1 beginning “As with any contract”). *Misco* requires both proper formulation of the policy and a clearly shown violation; speculation is insufficient, and the reviewing body may not perform the arbitrator’s factfinding. *United Paperworkers Int’l Union, AFL-CIO v. Misco, Inc.*, 484 U.S. 29, 43–45 (1987) (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf, reporter p. 43 ¶ 1 beginning “In W.R. Grace”; p. 44 ¶¶ 1–3 beginning “As we see it,” “Even if,” and “In any event,” with the last paragraph continuing onto p. 45).

PERB likewise described the public-policy exception as “extremely narrow” and found no clear violation on the face of this award. *Opinion No. 1904*, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd. Nov. 6, 2025), PDF p. 2 ¶ 5 (perb_decision_braddock.pdf).

### Application

MPD’s strongest formulation is a policy against an officer’s unjustified use of deadly force. The award’s findings make the underlying conduct serious: Braddock confronted an unarmed, dazed man, never saw a weapon, and fired twice, striking and gravely injuring him; the arbitrator sustained the charge and found no objectively reasonable perception of imminent death or serious bodily injury. The Final Notice identifies the charged conduct as a violation of General Order 120.21, Attachment A, Part A-12. (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4; D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 9–12.)

Those findings establish the seriousness of the misconduct, but not the necessary conflict between the award and a dominant public policy. The supplied materials identify the Department’s conduct rule; they do not supply an underlying statute, regulation, or legal precedent requiring removal for its violation. Nor does the award direct or authorize future unjustified force. It orders reinstatement after a substantial suspension. The record does not establish what assignment Braddock would receive or that reinstatement would compel a future violation. Under *Misco*, a reviewing court cannot fill that gap by assuming future misconduct or deciding for itself that the penalty is inadequate. (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF pp. 2–3 ¶¶ 8–10; final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf, PDF p. 2 ¶ 12; paperworkers_v_misco.pdf, reporter pp. 484 U.S. 44–45, p. 44 ¶¶ 2–3.)

The supplemental Kucera disclosure further counsels against framing policy as a categorical rule that an officer who discharges a service weapon off duty in violation of policy must be removed. Kucera was also off duty, used her service pistol against an unarmed person, and received a 40-day suspension plus supervisory probation after the Department found the discharge outside policy. Her circumstances were materially different: she fired once and missed as a man turned toward her after reaching into her vehicle; Braddock fired twice and caused grave injuries to a dazed man who had committed no offense, and Braddock had a prior reprimand. The settlement is not a merits ruling and does not dictate Braddock’s penalty. Most importantly for review, Bell confirms it was not before the arbitrator or PERB. It therefore cannot be used as record evidence to establish or defeat a facial public-policy violation. It does, however, make a categorical-removal position less persuasive in assessing the practical strength of a petition. (Memorandum from Marcus T. Bell, Labor Relations Liaison, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, to Outside Counsel, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (Braddock), re: Supplemental Comparator Case Identified After Close of Arbitration Record (Feb. 24, 2026) (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 2–4; final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf, PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 9–10, PDF p. 3 ¶ 16.)

**Principal weakness:** The attached materials do not establish that the policy against unjustified force requires termination, and the award does not compel future unlawful conduct. The factual gravity of Braddock’s shooting cannot substitute for the required legal source and clearly shown conflict. The out-of-record Kucera matter cannot cure that defect.

## 2. Contrary-to-law exception

### Question presented

Whether the award is facially contrary to law because the arbitrator misapplied the Douglas penalty framework—particularly by substituting his own factor-twelve deterrence judgment for the deciding official’s—or relied on a regulation that excludes sworn MPD members.

### Governing standard

The statutory test is facial and narrow. In *Fraternal Order of Police/Department of Corrections Labor Committee*, the D.C. Court of Appeals explained that PERB may not substitute its judgment for an arbitrator’s absent a clear legal violation evident on the face of the award. The court upheld PERB’s contrary-to-law determination where the arbitrator expressly relied on a specific statute but failed to follow that statute’s express requirement. *Fraternal Ord. of Police/Dep’t of Corr. Lab. Comm. v. D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd.*, 973 A.2d 174, 178–81 (D.C. 2009) (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf, PDF pp. 3–4, § II ¶¶ 1–2 and § III; reporter pp. 178–81). On judicial review, PERB’s factual findings receive substantial-evidence deference, and its interpretation of the CMPA stands unless unreasonable, inconsistent with the statute, plainly erroneous, or rationally indefensible. *Id.* at 177–79 (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf, PDF p. 3, § II ¶¶ 1–2). The Board applied the same narrow approach here, stating that disagreement with the arbitrator’s interpretation of Douglas or evaluation of evidence does not permit substitution of judgment. *Opinion No. 1904*, PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4 (perb_decision_braddock.pdf).

The Award invoked Douglas’s factors and its “tolerable limits of reasonableness” approach. Douglas requires a responsible balance of relevant factors while preserving primary agency discretion; the reviewer is not to strike the balance anew. It also places the ultimate burden to justify the penalty on the agency, and says that when a penalty is challenged or the reviewer perceives a genuine equity issue, further supporting evidence may be required. *Douglas v. Veterans Administration*, 5 M.S.P.R. 280, 332–34 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf, printed pp. 332–34, PDF pp. 20–22, including p. 332 ¶¶ 1–2, p. 333 ¶¶ 2–3, and p. 334 ¶¶ 1–2).

### Application

**Factor six.** The arbitrator found that MPD had not proved that its four comparator officers were similarly situated, identifying that two cases involved on-duty conduct, one involved a fatality, and one involved two prior sustained excessive-force complaints; he contrasted those matters with Braddock’s off-duty, nonfatal conduct and one unrelated reprimand. The four-case memorandum confirms each comparator was terminated but also describes materially different conduct and records. (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF p. 2 ¶ 7; Memorandum from Yolanda Britt, Director, Disciplinary Review Division, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, to Sandra M. Delgado, Assistant Chief, Professional Development Bureau, re: Comparator Discipline Cases, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841 (Braddock) (Apr. 11, 2024) (comparator_discipline_cases.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 1–5; final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf, PDF p. 3 ¶ 19.)

Douglas places the ultimate burden of persuasion on MPD and rejects mathematical rigidity in comparing penalties. The arbitrator’s statement that MPD failed to “prove” comparability does not itself shift the burden to Braddock; it is consistent with MPD’s burden to support its own factor-six conclusion. Nor did the award demand exact identity: it identified differences it considered material. The stipulated fact that reasonable minds can disagree about comparability makes the result an evidentiary judgment, not a clear legal error. (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf, printed pp. 332–34, PDF pp. 20–22, p. 333 ¶¶ 2–3 and p. 334 ¶ 2; braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF p. 2 ¶ 7.)

Braddock’s and the union’s failure to allege disparate treatment before the arbitration hearing, together with Braddock’s knowledge of MPD’s four comparators before the Department’s guilt decision, helps MPD show there was no earlier specific comparator objection or surprise about the cases. But the stipulated arbitration issue expressly asked whether termination was appropriate. That put the penalty in issue; the absence of a pre-hearing comparator allegation does not erase MPD’s ultimate burden or bar the arbitrator from considering factor six. (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF p. 1 ¶ 2; comparator_discipline_cases.pdf, PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 1, 6; douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf, printed p. 334, PDF p. 22, ¶ 2 beginning “In many cases.”)

The supplemental Kucera case is a closer comparator than the four considered by the arbitrator and materially weakens MPD’s position on the merits: it involved an off-duty, out-of-policy service-weapon discharge at an unarmed person and resulted in a non-removal settlement. Braddock’s more serious conduct and prior reprimand remain substantial distinctions. Because Kucera was not before either decision maker, however, it cannot show that the arbitrator’s four-case analysis was legally defective on the record then before him. (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf, PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 2–4; braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF p. 1 ¶ 1; Opinion No. 1904, PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 1–2.)

**Factor twelve.** This is MPD’s strongest preserved contrary-to-law argument. The deciding official explained that no lesser sanction would deter similar misconduct and that discipline short of removal would signal that shooting an unarmed, non-threatening person was survivable. The arbitrator nevertheless found no support beyond that assertion, predicted that a lengthy suspension would likely deter Braddock and other officers, and used that conclusion to reduce the penalty. MPD can argue that the arbitrator announced deferential review but replaced the deciding official’s deterrence judgment with his own. (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf, PDF p. 4 ¶¶ 25–26; braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF p. 2 ¶ 8, PDF p. 3 ¶ 10.)

The argument is not strong enough to make reversal likely. The award expressly disclaimed de novo review and used the tolerable-limits standard. Douglas both requires the agency to carry the burden when penalty appropriateness is contested and allows the reviewer to require further support when an equity issue arises. The arbitrator could therefore characterize his conclusion as finding MPD’s unsupported prediction insufficient—not as adopting a rule that deterrence requires empirical studies or as rejecting the official’s management authority. The final notice supplies a qualitative rationale, but it does not include evidence that the asserted deterrent effect was established. Which inference is better is precisely the kind of evidence-weighting disagreement PERB said could not justify setting aside the award. (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 5, 8–9; douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf, printed pp. 332–34, PDF pp. 20–22, p. 332 ¶¶ 1–2, p. 333 ¶ 3, p. 334 ¶ 2; Opinion No. 1904, PDF p. 1 ¶ 4.)

Kucera does not change that analysis. The 40-day suspension and probation show a prior non-removal outcome after an out-of-policy off-duty discharge, but Bell provides no evidence that the outcome deterred Kucera or others. The settlement was also outside the arbitration and PERB records. It therefore cannot answer the arbitrator’s factor-twelve question or supply an appellate factual basis. (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf, PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4.)

**Section 1600.2(g) issue.** The supplied regulation excludes sworn MPD members from Chapter 16, while § 1606.2 lists the Douglas-type factors. The award states that it reviewed the deciding official’s Douglas factors “pursuant to” § 1606.2, and the Final Notice likewise invokes that section. MPD could characterize reliance on § 1606.2 as application of a regulation that does not cover Braddock. (D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, §§ 1600.2(g), 1606.2; braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF p. 2 ¶ 5; final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf, PDF p. 2 ¶ 13.)

This is not a sound petition ground on the supplied record. PERB’s account of MPD’s arguments does not identify the sworn-member exclusion as an objection raised before the Board. *FOP/Department of Corrections* states that an objection not first urged before PERB may not be considered on judicial review. *Fraternal Ord. of Police/Dep’t of Corr. Lab. Comm.*, 973 A.2d at 181 n.8 (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf, PDF p. 5, footnote 8; Opinion No. 1904, PDF p. 1 ¶ 2). And the regulation’s exclusion does not itself establish that the arbitrator could not use the same factors as a benchmark under the grievance the parties submitted; the supplied materials do not include the collective-bargaining-agreement text. MPD’s own Final Notice also invoked § 1606.2. That makes this an uncertain, apparently unpreserved theory rather than a reliable facial defect.

**Principal weakness:** The strongest preserved argument (factor twelve) challenges the arbitrator’s evaluation of the deciding official’s explanation, not a facial command of law the award expressly ignored. The award states the correct deferential framework, and Douglas left the penalty burden with MPD once appropriateness was contested. Factor six is even less viable because the arbitrator applied that burden to a reasoned comparison, and the stipulated comparability disagreement forecloses portraying the conclusion as a clear legal mistake. The regulation-exclusion theory was not identified among MPD’s PERB arguments and is vulnerable to the preservation rule described in *FOP/Department of Corrections*.

## Recommendation

MPD should **not petition for review**. The public-policy ground lacks the required legal source and clear conflict; the factor-six ground is an evidentiary disagreement; and the factor-twelve ground, though colorable and the best preserved theory, is unlikely to overcome the facial-error threshold. Kucera’s settlement makes the broader comparator merits less favorable to MPD, but its absence from the arbitration and PERB records means it should not be offered as evidence in the petition. The possible 40/45-day description discrepancy in PERB’s opening paragraph does not change that assessment: the award specifies 45 days, and PERB’s order affirmed it in all respects without tying its reasoning to the mistaken figure. (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf, PDF p. 3 ¶ 10; perb_decision_braddock.pdf, PDF p. 1 ¶ 1, PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 6–7.)
