## 1. JURISDICTION AND TIMELINESS

This Court has certiorari jurisdiction to review a final order of a circuit court acting in its appellate capacity. Fla. Const. art. V, § 4(b)(3); Fla. R. App. P. 9.030(b)(2)(B). The challenged order arose from the Twentieth Judicial Circuit, which is assigned to the Sixth Appellate District. Fla. Stat. § 35.044 (2024). The circuit court’s October 25, 2025 order granted the residents’ first-tier petition and quashed the County’s rezoning approval. The order is final and disposes of the first-tier proceeding. Lee County files this petition on November 10, 2025, within 30 days after rendition. Fla. R. App. P. 9.020(h), 9.100(c)(1).

The order is reviewable only by second-tier certiorari. No appeal as of right lies from a circuit court’s final order entered in its appellate capacity. The order nullifies the County’s final approval of this application, and no ordinary appeal can remedy the circuit court’s application of an incorrect standard. The petition therefore satisfies the material-injury and inadequate-remedy threshold in Florida Rule of Appellate Procedure 9.100(h). It is accompanied by an appendix index identifying the order and essential record materials. See Fla. R. App. P. 9.100(g), 9.220.

## 2. QUESTIONS PRESENTED

1. Did the circuit court depart from clearly established law when it treated a statement made during the public hearing and the County’s earlier public actions as the Board’s dispositive decision, without analyzing the Board’s adopted written resolution and its findings?

2. Did the circuit court misapply *Jennings v. Dade Cnty.* by finding a due-process violation based on prior public County proceedings and a public settlement, without identifying an ex parte communication, a decisionmaker who had prejudged this application, or other conduct denying a fair hearing?

3. Did the circuit court depart from the essential requirements of law by treating the Federal Aviation Administration’s (FAA’s) five-mile recommendation and its “not fully considered” observation as a dispositive legal violation, without identifying (1) an enforceable County provision imposing that requirement, (2) how section 34-1102 makes the recommendation mandatory, or (3) an actual conflict under section 333.04(2)?

## 3. STATEMENT OF THE CASE AND FACTS

### A. The Board approved a planned wastewater facility after a public quasi-judicial process.

Lee County Utilities applied to rezone approximately 112.21 acres from Agricultural (AG-2) to Community Facilities Planned Development (CFPD) for the Southeast Advanced Water Reclamation Facility. The application proposed an essential-services wastewater treatment facility with up to 25,000 square feet of office, maintenance, and administrative buildings and treatment capacity of up to 10 million gallons per day. The Lee County Zoning Hearing Examiner held a public hearing on October 19, 2023, considered the evidence, and recommended approval subject to conditions. The Board held its public hearing on January 17, 2024, and unanimously approved the request subject to conditions and deviations. *See* Lee Cnty. Bd. of Cnty. Comm’rs, Resolution No. Z-23-035, §§ A–E (Jan. 17, 2024) (Pet. App. Tab 2) [hereinafter *Resolution*].

The Resolution records that the Board gave full and complete consideration to staff recommendations, the Hearing Examiner’s recommendation, the documents in the record, and testimony from interested persons. It adopts the Hearing Examiner’s recommendation and findings. Those findings conclude that the planned development, as conditioned, complies with the Lee Plan and Land Development Code, is compatible with existing and planned uses, will not adversely affect environmentally critical areas and natural resources, and satisfies the applicable planned-development criteria. *Id.* §§ A, E. The Resolution also approves specified deviations subject to conditions, including a four-to-one lake-bank slope with enhanced slope protection and no planted littoral shelf. *Id.* § C.

During the public hearing, Commissioner Pendergrass moved to adopt the Resolution, Commissioner Sandelli seconded, and the Board adopted it by unanimous consent. The motion included the words “based on the direction of the board over the last four years.” The circuit court quoted the statement and cited the hearing record. *Order Granting Petition for Writ of Certiorari* at 2–3, *Spahn v. Lee County*, No. 24-CA-1479 (Fla. 20th Cir. Ct. Oct. 25, 2025) (Pet. App. Tab 1 at 2–3) [hereinafter *Order*]; R. App. vol. 1, Bates 314–15 (Pet. App. Tab 3).

### B. The first-tier order relied on the motion, prior public County actions, and airport guidance.

The residents sought first-tier certiorari review. The circuit court found standing based on the proximity of their homes and the scale of the proposed facility. *Order* at 2. It granted the petition on three grounds. First, it concluded that the motion’s reference to the Board’s “direction” over four years relied on matters beyond the quasi-judicial record and violated *Jennings*. *Id.* at 2–3. Second, it concluded that the County’s earlier public actions—including a 2019 rezoning process that ended in remand and a 2022 settlement depicting sewer infrastructure to the proposed site—prejudiced the 2024 proceeding or demonstrated bias. *Id.* at 3–5. Third, it concluded that the County Code and chapter 333 required consideration of updated FAA airport-compatibility guidance, including its recommendation concerning wastewater treatment facilities, and that the current guidance had not been “fully considered.” *Id.* at 5–6.

The airport issue arose in the record before the Hearing Examiner. The applicant’s planner described the Lee County Code’s 10,000-foot Airport Wildlife Hazard Protection Zone, testified that the site would be outside that zone after the planned runway expansion, and described design choices made with airport compatibility in mind. The planner also reported that the Lee County Port Authority (Port Authority) had no objection. Hearing Tr. vol. 1 at 226–27 (Pet. App. Tab 4). The Lee County Code defines the Airport Wildlife Hazard Protection Zone by reference to 10,000 feet from the nearest point of a runway and sets specific surface-water standards for development within that zone. Lee Cnty., Fla., Land Development Code §§ 10-418(5), 34-1103 (as in effect Jan. 1, 2025).

The order’s first page identifies Resolution Z-23-022, but the application, project description, and Board action at issue concern Resolution Z-23-035. The order’s concluding paragraph states that the decision below is “REVERSED,” although its opening paragraph states that the rezoning is “QUASHED.” *Order* at 1, 6. The order does not specify a remand procedure or separately identify the operative reach of its relief.

## 4. STANDARD OF REVIEW

First-tier certiorari review asks whether the local government afforded procedural due process, observed the essential requirements of law, and based its decision on competent substantial evidence. *City of Deerfield Beach v. Vaillant*, 419 So. 2d 624, 626 (Fla. 1982); *Bd. of Cnty. Comm’rs of Brevard Cnty. v. Snyder*, 627 So. 2d 469, 476 (Fla. 1993). A circuit court may not reweigh conflicting evidence or substitute its judgment for the local government’s. *Dusseau v. Metro. Dade Cnty. Bd. of Cnty. Comm’rs*, 794 So. 2d 1270, 1274–75 (Fla. 2001).

Second-tier review is narrower. It asks only whether the circuit court afforded procedural due process and applied the correct law. *Miami-Dade Cnty. v. Omnipoint Holdings, Inc.*, 863 So. 2d 195, 198–99 (Fla. 2003); *Broward Cnty. v. G.B.V. Int’l, Ltd.*, 787 So. 2d 838, 843 (Fla. 2001). It does not permit another review of the Board’s evidentiary record. A departure from the essential requirements of law requires a violation of a clearly established legal principle resulting in a miscarriage of justice. *Haines City Cmty. Dev. v. Heggs*, 658 So. 2d 523, 530 (Fla. 1995); *Custer Med. Ctr. v. United Auto. Ins. Co.*, 62 So. 3d 1086, 1092 (Fla. 2010). The County therefore does not ask this Court to reassess the weight of evidence; it asks the Court to correct the legal standards the circuit court applied.

## 5. SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

The circuit order cannot stand because it attributes dispositive significance to the oral motion and earlier County acts without addressing the Board’s written Resolution—the Board’s final action. The Resolution adopts the Hearing Examiner’s recommendation and findings and expressly states that the Board considered the record and testimony. In a closely analogous second-tier rezoning case, the Second District held that a circuit court departs from the essential requirements of law when it treats a commissioner’s hearing comment as a Board finding that the Board did not adopt. *Hillsborough Cnty. v. G.L. Acquisitions Corp., Inc.*, 415 So. 3d 1205, 1207–09 (Fla. 2d DCA 2025). The governing principle is that a county commission speaks through its adopted resolution, not through an inference drawn from an isolated oral statement. *Metro. Dade Cnty. v. Blumenthal*, 675 So. 2d 598, 604 (Fla. 3d DCA 1995) (en banc).

The due-process ruling likewise extends *Jennings v. Dade County*, 589 So. 2d 1337, 1341–42 (Fla. 3d DCA 1991), beyond its holding. *Jennings* concerns proof of an ex parte contact with a quasi-judicial decisionmaker and the rebuttable presumption that follows such proof. The order identifies no such contact. It relies instead on public County proceedings and a recorded settlement, together with a statement made in the open hearing. Those circumstances do not, without a finding of individual prejudgment or a denial of a fair opportunity to be heard, establish the *Jennings* violation the order found. The County’s prior public involvement in a County-owned property’s rezoning is not itself disqualifying. *See* Op. Att’y Gen. Fla. 94-71 (1994).

Finally, chapter 333 does not impose a five-mile wastewater-facility setback. The chapter specifies minimum airport-compatibility zoning subjects, including landfills, noise, and runway protection zones; it does not identify wastewater treatment facilities as a prohibited use. Fla. Stat. § 333.03(2) (2024). It allows local governments to adopt more restrictive rules, but does not itself adopt them. *Id.* § 333.03(6). Section 333.04(2)’s “more stringent” rule applies when enacted regulations actually conflict; it does not transform FAA recommendations into regulations. *Id.* § 333.04(2). Lee County’s Code references the current FAA circular as guidance, but its specific wildlife-hazard zone is 10,000 feet and its specific standards apply within that zone. The FAA circular itself says it is not mandatory or legally binding and “recommends against” certain facilities, while directing discussion, site review, and mitigation if a facility is proposed within the separation criteria. The order identifies no enacted five-mile prohibition and no actual regulatory conflict. It thus treats the FAA recommendation as dispositive without identifying an operative local criterion that makes it so.

These are errors of law, not disputes about how much weight particular testimony deserved. The Court should grant the petition, quash the order, and remand for first-tier review under the correct legal standards.

## 6. ARGUMENT

### I. The circuit court did not review the Board’s final written resolution under the governing law.

The Resolution is the Board’s final action. It adopts the Hearing Examiner’s recommendation and findings, recites that the Board considered the staff recommendations, the record, and testimony, and makes findings under the Lee Plan and Land Development Code. *Resolution* §§ A, E. The circuit court nevertheless treated the motion’s reference to “direction” over four years as establishing that the Board acted on extra-record matters and as proof that the outcome had been predetermined. *Order* at 2–5. It did not analyze the Resolution’s adopted findings or explain why the Resolution failed to express the Board’s decision.

That approach conflicts with the established rule that a county commission speaks through its written resolution. *Metro. Dade Cnty.*, 675 So. 2d at 604. The recent second-tier decision in *G.L. Acquisitions* applies that rule in the zoning context. There, the circuit court relied on a commissioner’s hearing comment that did not appear in the Board’s adopted resolution. The Second District held that the circuit court departed from the essential requirements of law by attributing the comment to the Board and reviewing a finding the Board had not made. 415 So. 3d at 1207–09. The decision is particularly persuasive here because it addresses the same procedural error: treating a commissioner’s oral statement as the Board’s operative decision while omitting the adopted written action from the analysis.

The County recognizes that the motion here was an official motion to adopt the Resolution, not merely a commissioner’s unrelated remark. But that distinction does not make the motion proof of an ex parte contact, a secret factual submission, or a formal Board finding that supersedes the Resolution. The motion was made in the public hearing, after public participation, and was included in the record. The Board’s formal resolution then adopted the Hearing Examiner’s findings and recorded its consideration of the hearing record. A reviewing court must assess the final Board action as a whole; it cannot treat an ambiguous phrase in a public motion as conclusive proof of an undisclosed basis for decision while disregarding the adopted findings.

The first-tier court’s role is to apply the correct review standard to the local government’s decision, not to reconstruct the Board’s reasoning from an isolated part of the record. *G.B.V. International*, 787 So. 2d at 843–45. Second-tier review in turn cannot become a fresh evidentiary review of the Board’s approval. *Omnipoint Holdings*, 863 So. 2d at 198–99. Because the circuit court’s due-process analysis rested on its treatment of the oral motion and prior County history rather than on the Board’s adopted decision, it applied the wrong legal framework. The error was material: it nullified the Resolution without first reviewing the Board’s actual findings and final action.

### II. Public County actions and an on-record motion do not establish a due-process violation under *Jennings*.

An impartial decisionmaker is a component of due process in a quasi-judicial proceeding. But *Jennings* does not hold that every earlier public position or governmental act establishes bias. It addresses an alleged ex parte communication and holds that, upon proof that a decisionmaker received such a contact, prejudice is presumed subject to rebuttal. *Jennings*, 589 So. 2d at 1341–42. The order here identifies no communication outside the hearing, no undisclosed material delivered to a commissioner, and no private contact between a decisionmaker and an interested party. It instead relies on the County’s earlier public proceedings, a recorded 2022 settlement, and a statement made during the open Board hearing. *Order* at 2–5.

Those facts do not establish the *Jennings* predicate. The settlement and prior rezoning activity were public governmental acts; the residents themselves raised those matters in the first-tier proceeding. Their public existence does not establish that a commissioner received information in secret or that the residents were denied an opportunity to address the current application. Nor does the Board’s consideration of a County-owned property application make the Board legally incapable of adjudicating it. The Attorney General has concluded that a county commission is not precluded from considering a rezoning request involving County-owned property, while advising that staff contacts outside the hearing be documented and placed on the record so interested persons can respond. Op. Att’y Gen. Fla. 94-71 (1994). The order identifies no undisclosed staff contact of that kind.

The motion is also not an ex parte contact: it was spoken on the record in the hearing itself. Respondents had participated in the hearing and could address the application and the County’s prior actions. The order does not identify an individual commissioner’s statement that the commissioner had prejudged this specific application, a refusal to hear opposing views, or any other procedure that deprived Respondents of a fair hearing. Florida courts distinguish mere political bias or an adverse political philosophy—which does not itself invalidate a quasi-judicial proceeding—from pervasive partiality that actually denies a fair hearing. *Seminole Ent., Inc. v. City of Casselberry*, 811 So. 2d 693, 696–97 (Fla. 5th DCA 2001). Instead, this order treats the County’s public institutional history as prejudicial in itself. That is not the test in *Jennings*.

The result is not a request that the Court decide whether a commissioner was subjectively biased or reweigh the hearing evidence. The point is that the order applied a presumption associated with proven ex parte contacts without identifying a contact, and inferred prejudgment from public County conduct without a legal rule making that conduct disqualifying. That misapplication of clearly established law deprived the County of first-tier review of the Board’s actual decision and materially altered the result. It satisfies the demanding second-tier standard.

### III. Chapter 333 and the Lee County Code do not make the FAA five-mile recommendation a dispositive bar to rezoning.

The circuit court’s airport ruling must be measured against the enactments in effect January 1, 2025. Chapter 333 requires local airport land-use-compatibility zoning regulations to address specified subjects. Those include landfill siting and bird hazards related to landfills, noise contours, residential and educational uses near airports without a noise study, and incompatible uses in runway protection zones. Fla. Stat. § 333.03(2)(a)–(e) (2024). The statute does not prescribe a five-mile setback for wastewater treatment facilities. Section 333.03(6) permits a local government to enact stricter airport-zoning provisions; it does not enact any additional restriction by itself. Section 333.04(2) provides that the more stringent requirement governs “[i]n the event of conflict” between airport-zoning regulations adopted under chapter 333 and other regulations. It therefore presupposes an actual conflict between enacted regulations; it does not make an advisory circular a statute or ordinance.

The County Code’s reference to FAA guidance does not supply the missing five-mile prohibition. Section 34-1102 is titled “Purpose and intent.” It describes the protections the Airport Compatibility District is intended to provide and references FAA Advisory Circular 150/5200-33B “as amended, renumbered or replaced.” Lee Cnty., Fla., Land Development Code § 34-1102 (as in effect Jan. 1, 2025). That reference makes the current wildlife guidance relevant to airport compatibility; it does not say that each FAA recommendation is a mandatory local zoning limit or that a wastewater plant within five miles must be denied. The Code separately defines an Airport Wildlife Hazard Protection Zone as the area within 10,000 feet of the nearest point of a runway and prescribes specific lake-bank and littoral standards for development within that zone. *Id.* §§ 10-418(5), 34-1103. The record before the Hearing Examiner included testimony that the site would be outside that locally defined zone after the planned runway expansion. Hearing Tr. vol. 1 at 226–27.

The FAA circular confirms the distinction between guidance and a binding prohibition. Its application section states that the FAA “recommends” its guidance, that the circular “does not constitute a regulation,” “is not mandatory,” and “is not legally binding in its own right.” Fed. Aviation Admin., Advisory Circular (AC) No. 150/5200-33C, *Hazardous Wildlife Attractants on or Near Airports*, at ii (Feb. 21, 2020). Section 1.4 recommends a five-mile separation between the closest point of an airport’s aircraft operations area and a hazardous wildlife attractant; § 2.3.4 says the FAA “recommends against” construction of new wastewater treatment facilities within the identified separations. *Id.* §§ 1.4, 2.3.4, at 1-8 to 1-9, 2-7. If a facility is proposed within those separations, the circular calls for discussion of location, identification of a more suitable site “if possible,” and, if no other suitable site exists, review by a qualified airport wildlife biologist and mitigation. *Id.* § 2.3.4, at 2-7. That is meaningful guidance, but its terms do not create an absolute five-mile setback.

The County does not contend that the Board could ignore the circular or the Code’s airport-protection purpose. The Resolution found that the planned development complied with the Land Development Code and adopted the Hearing Examiner’s recommendation and findings. *Resolution* § E. The hearing record also addressed the Code’s defined 10,000-foot wildlife zone, airport-aware design, and the Port Authority’s position. Nor was the Board required to recite each FAA recommendation in its resolution: although findings may be useful, a rezoning board is not required to make written findings of fact. *Snyder*, 627 So. 2d at 476. The circuit court’s second-tier error is narrower: it did not identify a mandatory local provision imposing the recommended five-mile setback, and it treated the advisory circular’s recommendation as if section 333.04(2) made it the controlling “more stringent” regulation. Whether the Board’s findings are supported by competent substantial evidence is not for this Court to reconsider. The question is whether the circuit court applied the correct legal rule. It did not.

At a minimum, the order’s reliance on “not fully considered” fails to identify the legal standard that the Board supposedly violated. If the circuit court meant that the Board had to consider current FAA guidance because section 34-1102 references it, the order must be confined to that enacted criterion and the first-tier court must evaluate the Board’s adopted findings under the proper review standard. If the circuit court instead treated the AC’s five-mile recommendation as a binding setback, that construction conflicts with the text and structure of chapter 333, section 34-1102, and the AC itself. Either way, the present order cannot stand as written.

## 7. CONCLUSION

Lee County respectfully requests that the Court grant this petition for writ of certiorari, quash the October 25, 2025 order, and remand for the circuit court to reconsider the first-tier petition under the correct legal standards. The Court should clarify that the order under review concerns the Board’s January 17, 2024 action embodied in Resolution Z-23-035; the order’s reference to Z-23-022 and its inconsistent use of “quashed” and “reversed” do not identify a different Board action.
