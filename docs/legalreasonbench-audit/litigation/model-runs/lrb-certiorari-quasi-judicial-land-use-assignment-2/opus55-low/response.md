## INTRODUCTION

This Petition asks the Court to correct a circuit court order that quashed, and then purported to "reverse," a unanimous quasi-judicial rezoning. The rezoning authorizes the County's Southeast Advanced Water Reclamation Facility, public wastewater infrastructure serving the rapidly growing Alico/Corkscrew corridor. Construction began on October 6, 2025.

The circuit court did not hold that the Board's decision lacked competent substantial evidence. It did not identify any statute or ordinance that prohibits a wastewater facility at this location. Instead, it reached three conclusions:

1. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) advisory circulars, which declare on their face that they are "not mandatory" and "not legally binding," were binding law, and the Board violated them because the advisories were "not fully considered."
2. A commissioner's on-the-record motion, referring to "the direction of the board over the last 4 years [and] the needs for the community," was a due process violation under *Jennings v. Dade County*, 589 So. 2d 1337 (Fla. 3d DCA 1991). *Jennings* is a case about undisclosed ex parte communications.
3. The County's long-range utility planning, a land purchase, and a settlement agreement in unrelated litigation "predetermined" the result. To reach this conclusion, the court relied substantially on materials from Respondents' reply appendix.

The court then "REVERSED" the decision, a remedy the Florida Supreme Court has held a certiorari court cannot grant.

Each of these rulings applied the wrong law. Together they leave a public utility facility under construction without zoning, on the strength of legal standards that do not exist. The Court should grant the writ and quash the Order.

## STATEMENT OF THE CASE AND FACTS

### A. The Southeast Advanced Water Reclamation Facility and the Application

The County, through Lee County Utilities, owns a 112.21-acre parcel on the north side of Alico Road in Sections 4 and 9, Township 46 South, Range 26 East. Res. § A & Ex. A (App. B). The parcel lies within the Public Facilities, Density Reduction/Groundwater Resource, and Wetlands future land use categories. *Id.* § A.

The County applied (Case No. DCI2023-00011) to rezone the parcel from Agricultural (AG-2) to Community Facilities Planned Development (CFPD). The rezoning would permit an Essential Service Facility, Group II (wastewater treatment plant), with up to 25,000 square feet of office, maintenance, and administration buildings and infrastructure for treating up to 10 million gallons per day. *Id.*

The County's wastewater master planning for the Corkscrew service area identified this site years earlier. Cir. Pet. at 23–24 (App. C). An earlier application (DCI2018-10023) proposed co-locating the facility with a solid waste transfer station. In August 2019 that application was remanded on joint request "to ensure that all alternatives have been fully reviewed and analyzed," and it was formally withdrawn on September 13, 2023. *Id.* at 14–16. The current application excludes solid waste transfer facilities from the permitted uses. Res. § B (App. B).

### B. The Quasi-Judicial Hearings and the Board's Decision

**The Hearing Examiner hearing.** On October 19, 2023, the Lee County Zoning Hearing Examiner held an advertised public hearing. Respondents and other members of the public participated. Res. at 1 (App. B); Tr. 1 (App. D). The Hearing Examiner "gave full consideration to the evidence in the record" and recommended approval with conditions. Res. at 1 (App. B).

**Airport-compatibility testimony.** The record contains specific testimony on airport compatibility. The applicant's engineer presented an exhibit "generated by Johnson Engineering using that line work provided by the Port Authority." He testified that the Port Authority's 10,000-foot wildlife protection buffer is measured from "the active areas, like the runway," including the runway expansion then under construction. He testified that "we are outside of that buffer zone upon completion of that runway." Tr. 1 at 226–27 (App. D) (as quoted at Cir. Pet. 36 (App. C)).

He further explained that the requested deviations "correspond and correlate directly to what typical projects within that protection zone would do," in order to "avoid attracting birds." *Id.* Those deviations were a 4:1 lake-bank slope and the omission of a planted littoral shelf. Res. § C (App. B). Respondents themselves acknowledged that the deviations were designed to address "airport incompatibility" concerns. Cir. Pet. at 49 (App. C).

**The Board hearing.** On January 17, 2024, the Board held a second advertised public hearing. Res. at 1 (App. B). Commissioner Pendergrass moved approval, stating: "Move the item based upon the direction of the board over that last 4 years, the needs for the community." Tr. 2 at 20:17–19 (App. E). The Board unanimously adopted Resolution Z-23-035.

The Resolution recites that the Board "gave full and complete consideration to the recommendations of the staff, the Hearing Examiner, the documents on record and the testimony of all interested persons." Res. at 1 (App. B). The Board expressly adopted the Hearing Examiner's recommendation and findings, including that the CFPD, as conditioned:

- complies with the Lee Plan;
- complies with the Land Development Code (LDC) "and other County regulations or qualifies for deviations";
- is compatible with existing and planned uses;
- will not adversely affect environmentally critical areas and natural resources.

*Id.* § E.1. The Board also found that the conditions "are sufficient to protect the public interest" and that the deviations "[p]romote the intent of the LDC to protect public health, safety, and welfare." *Id.* §§ E.4–5.

The approval carries eight conditions, including:

- a master concept plan;
- a use schedule;
- water-quality monitoring;
- 60.3% open space and 31.39 acres of indigenous open space;
- an indigenous management plan;
- buffers;
- state and federal permitting.

*Id.* § B.

### C. The First-Tier Certiorari Petition

On February 16, 2024, Respondents petitioned the circuit court for certiorari. App. C. They argued two things:

- **Due process.** They argued that leaving the 2019 application pending "silenced" residents under the County's ex parte rules, and that the motion's reference to the Board's "direction" showed bias. Cir. Pet. at 14–22.
- **Essential requirements of law.** They argued that the rezoning violated several LDC provisions, Chapters 163 and 333, Florida Statutes, and FAA Advisory Circulars 150/5190-4B and 150/5200-33C. *Id.* at 24–49.

Respondents described the FAA guidance in its own terms. They quoted it as providing that "[t]he FAA *recommends* against the construction of new wastewater treatment facilities" within specified separations, that the FAA "*recommends* a distance of 5 miles," and that when such a facility is proposed, the parties "*should* discuss" the location and, "[i]f possible," identify a more suitable site. Cir. Pet. at 35, 37–38 (App. C) (emphasis added).

### D. The Circuit Court's Order

After oral argument on August 7, 2025, the circuit court granted the petition on three grounds. App. A.

**Ground I.** The court held that the motion "based on the direction of the board over the last four years" relied on matters "beyond the quasi-judicial record." With "no further discussion," the court held, the motion "shows prejudice and bias for a pre-determined result," particularly because the County was reviewing its own application. The court concluded this violated procedural due process, citing *Jennings* and Op. Att'y Gen. Fla. 94-71 (1994). Order at 2–3.

**Ground II.** The court held that three prior County actions "created prejudicial presumptions" while the 2019 application remained open, "thereby preventing public contact with their county commissioners":

- the 2019 application;
- the County's purchase of a conservation tract;
- a 2022 settlement agreement with the developer of the Corkscrew Grove ("Kingston") community, whose exhibit "depicted and presumptively chose this site."

The court relied on Respondents' Reply Appendix for these findings. Order at 3–5 (citing Reply App. V3 p. 335; V8 pp. 959–993).

**Ground III.** The court held that the application "did not comply with" LDC Division 12 (Airport Compatibility District), LDC § 34-1102, section 333.04(2), Florida Statutes, and the FAA wildlife-hazard advisories. It reasoned that the "most restrictive" provision controls, and that the current FAA advisories "introduced into the record by Petitioners were admittedly not fully considered," placing "public safety at risk." Order at 5–6.

The Order does not:

- identify any provision of Chapter 333 or the LDC that prohibits a wastewater facility at this location;
- find that the site lies within any mandatory separation;
- address whether competent substantial evidence supports the Board's findings.

The Order's introduction states that the rezoning is "QUASHED." Its decretal paragraph states: "the Petition for Writ of Certiorari is GRANTED and the decision below is REVERSED." Order at 1, 6.

The Order, like the circuit petition's caption, refers to the decision as "Rezoning Resolution Z-23-022." The resolution adopted on January 17, 2024 in DCI2023-00011 is Resolution Z-23-035. *Compare* Order at 1, *with* Res. at 1 (App. B); *see also* Cir. Pet. at 5 (App. C) (referring to "Zoning Action Z-23-035").

## STANDARD OF REVIEW

On first-tier certiorari review of a quasi-judicial decision, the circuit court determines only three things:

1. whether procedural due process was accorded;
2. whether the essential requirements of law were observed;
3. whether the findings and judgment are supported by competent substantial evidence.

*City of Deerfield Beach v. Vaillant*, 419 So. 2d 624, 626 (Fla. 1982); *G.B.V.*, 787 So. 2d at 843.

On second-tier review, this Court determines whether the circuit court (1) afforded procedural due process and (2) applied the correct law. *Heggs*, 658 So. 2d at 530; *Custer Med. Ctr. v. United Auto. Ins. Co.*, 62 So. 3d 1086, 1092 (Fla. 2010). "Applied the correct law" is synonymous with "observing the essential requirements of law." The Court asks whether there was a "violation of a clearly established principle of law resulting in a miscarriage of justice." *Custer*, 62 So. 3d at 1092 (quoting *Heggs*, 658 So. 2d at 528).

"Clearly established law" may derive from controlling case law, rules of court, statutes, and constitutional law. *Allstate Ins. Co. v. Kaklamanos*, 843 So. 2d 885, 890 (Fla. 2003).

The Florida Supreme Court has repeatedly held that two kinds of error are correctable on second-tier review:

- a circuit court that reweighs the evidence, or substitutes its judgment for the local government's, applies the wrong law, *Dusseau v. Metro. Dade Cnty. Bd. of Cnty. Comm'rs*, 794 So. 2d 1270, 1275–76 (Fla. 2001); *Fla. Power & Light Co. v. City of Dania*, 761 So. 2d 1089, 1093 (Fla. 2000); *Miami-Dade Cnty. v. Omnipoint Holdings, Inc.*, 863 So. 2d 195, 199 (Fla. 2003);
- a circuit court that grants a remedy beyond quashal exceeds its jurisdiction, *G.B.V.*, 787 So. 2d at 843–46.

## SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

**I. The airport-compatibility ruling.** The Order's dispositive legal holding is that the rezoning violated the essential requirements of law by failing to comply with FAA wildlife-hazard advisories. That holding applies the wrong law in three respects.

- *Chapter 333 does not apply this way.* Its only mandatory wildlife-attractant siting minimum concerns landfills. § 333.03(2)(a)–(b), Fla. Stat. Its "more stringent" rule in section 333.04(2) resolves conflicts among regulations actually "adopted under this chapter." It does not convert federal guidance into state law.
- *The LDC provision is a purpose clause.* LDC § 34-1102 states the purpose of the Airport Compatibility District. It is not a siting prohibition.
- *The FAA circulars are expressly non-binding.* By their own terms they "do[] not constitute a regulation, [are] not mandatory, and [are] not legally binding in [their] own right."

Having no mandatory rule to enforce, the court faulted the Board because the advisories were "not fully considered." Whether evidence was fully considered and properly weighed is exactly the inquiry *Dusseau*, *Vaillant*, and *Omnipoint* forbid. The record contains competent substantial evidence of airport compatibility, which the Order never addressed.

**II. The *Jennings* ruling.** The court converted a commissioner's on-the-record statement of reasons into a due process violation under *Jennings*. *Jennings* concerns undisclosed ex parte communications. None was alleged or found here, and section 286.0115, Florida Statutes, has since modified *Jennings* in any event.

The Board's decision rests on its adopted record findings, not on one commissioner's articulation of a motion. *Snyder*, 627 So. 2d at 476. And no clearly established law holds that a county denies due process by deciding its own application for a public facility. Decision-makers are presumed honest and impartial, and familiarity with a matter or prior policy positions do not disqualify them. *Withrow v. Larkin*, 421 U.S. 35, 47 (1975); *Hortonville Joint Sch. Dist. No. 1 v. Hortonville Educ. Ass'n*, 426 U.S. 482, 493 (1976).

**III. The "predetermination" ruling.** This ruling treats ordinary capital planning, a land purchase, and a settlement agreement as disqualifying prejudgment. That rule, if accepted, would bar every Florida local government from zoning its own sites for utilities. The court also relied on reply-appendix materials outside the quasi-judicial record, and it identified no prejudice. Respondents fully participated in both hearings.

**IV. The remedy.** The court "REVERSED" the Board's decision. A certiorari court may only quash. *G.B.V.*, 787 So. 2d at 843–44.

**V. Miscarriage of justice.** These errors are not isolated misapplications of correct law. They announce new, erroneous legal standards that will govern every County quasi-judicial decision near the region's international airport, and they halt essential public infrastructure. That is a miscarriage of justice warranting relief.

## ARGUMENT

### I. The Circuit Court Applied the Wrong Law by Treating Non-Binding FAA Advisory Circulars and a Code Purpose Clause as Mandatory Siting Prohibitions, and by Reweighing Whether the Board "Fully Considered" Them.

The "essential requirements of law" prong of first-tier review asks whether the local government applied the correct law. *Heggs*, 658 So. 2d at 530. To find a departure, the circuit court had to identify a binding legal requirement that the Board failed to apply. It did not. The authorities it invoked either do not reach wastewater facilities or are not binding law at all.

#### A. Neither Chapter 333 nor the Land Development Code Prohibits a Wastewater Facility at This Site.

**Chapter 333 regulates only one wildlife attractant.** Chapter 333 requires political subdivisions to adopt airport protection zoning and specifies minimum airport land use compatibility regulations. § 333.03(1)–(2), Fla. Stat. (2024). The Legislature addressed wildlife attractants specifically, and it did so with respect to one land use: landfills.

Section 333.03(2)(a) provides that airport land use compatibility zoning regulations must, at a minimum, address "[t]he prohibition of new landfills and the restriction of existing landfills" within specified areas:

- 10,000 feet from the nearest point of any runway used or planned to be used by turbine aircraft;
- 5,000 feet from the nearest point of any runway used only by nonturbine aircraft;
- outside those perimeters but within the lateral limits of the civil airport imaginary surfaces defined in 14 C.F.R. § 77.19, where "[c]ase-by-case review of such landfills is advised."

§ 333.03(2)(a), Fla. Stat. (2024). Paragraph (b) then requires landfill operators whose facilities attract hazardous bird movements to "incorporate bird management techniques." *Id.* § 333.03(2)(b). The statute's remaining minimum standards concern incompatible uses within noise contours, residential and educational uses near airports without noise studies, and runway protection zones. *Id.* § 333.03(2)(c)–(e).

**Wastewater facilities are not on the list.** Nothing in Chapter 333 prohibits, or establishes a separation distance for, a wastewater treatment facility. Had the Legislature intended to regulate wastewater facilities as wildlife attractants, it knew how to do so. It enumerated landfills alone.

**Section 333.04(2) is a conflict rule.** The circuit court relied on section 333.04(2), which provides that "[i]n the event of conflict between any airport zoning regulations adopted under this chapter and any other regulations applicable to the same area, . . . the more stringent limitation or requirement shall govern and prevail." § 333.04(2), Fla. Stat. (2024). That provision operates only when there is (1) an airport zoning regulation "adopted under this chapter" and (2) a conflict between it and some other regulation. It does not create substantive standards. It does not adopt FAA advisory circulars. It does not authorize a court to treat a federal "recommendation" as the "more stringent" law and then enforce it. FAA advisory circulars are not airport zoning regulations "adopted under" Chapter 333, and the Order identifies no regulation that conflicts with the Board's approval.

Section 333.03(6) is to the same effect. It permits a local government to adopt regulations "more restrictive than prescribed in this section." That is a grant of authority to legislate, not a self-executing incorporation of anything. § 333.03(6), Fla. Stat. (2024). (Respondents cited this provision as § 333.03(5), its number before the 2024 amendments. *See* Cir. Pet. (App. C).)

**LDC § 34-1102 is a statement of purpose.** As Respondents quoted it, LDC § 34-1102 provides that "[t]he purpose of this division is to establish protections around SWFIA and Page Field in accordance with the provisions of F.S. Chapters 330 and 333, as amended, as well as Federal regulations" and identified FAA advisory circulars. Cir. Pet. at 31–32, 39 (App. C). By its terms, it states the *purpose* of Division 12. Its operative standards are found elsewhere in the Division.

A purpose clause does not impose independent substantive obligations. It describes the objectives that the operative provisions implement. The Order does not identify any operative provision of Division 12 that:

- prohibits an essential service facility at the subject site;
- establishes a wildlife-hazard separation applicable to wastewater facilities;
- places the site within a zone where the use is prohibited.

The undisputed record evidence is that the site lies *outside* the Port Authority's 10,000-foot wildlife protection buffer, measured from the runways including the expansion runway. Tr. 1 at 226–27 (App. D).

**The Order applies a non-existent rule.** The Order's conclusion that the application "did not comply with" Division 12, § 34-1102, and § 333.04(2) (Order at 5–6) therefore rests on a rule of law that does not exist. Applying a non-existent legal standard to quash a quasi-judicial decision is the paradigm of applying the wrong law. *See Heggs*, 658 So. 2d at 530; *Kaklamanos*, 843 So. 2d at 889–90.

#### B. The FAA Advisory Circulars Are, by Their Own Terms, Not Mandatory and Not Legally Binding.

The circuit court treated FAA Advisory Circular 150/5200-33C as the "most restrictive" law governing the site. Order at 5–6. But the circular's own "Application" section forecloses that treatment:

> The Federal Aviation Administration recommends the guidance in this AC for land uses that have the potential to attract hazardous wildlife on or near public-use airports. This AC does not constitute a regulation, is not mandatory, and is not legally binding in its own right. It will not be relied upon as a separate basis by the FAA for affirmative enforcement action or other administrative penalty. Conformity with this AC is voluntary, and nonconformity will not affect rights and obligations under existing statutes and regulations . . . .

Fed. Aviation Admin., Advisory Circular 150/5200-33C, *Hazardous Wildlife Attractants on or near Airports*, at ii, ¶ 3 (Feb. 21, 2020).

The circular's limited exceptions confirm the point. Certificated airports "may use" it "as one, but not the only, acceptable means of compliance" with 14 C.F.R. Part 139. And the FAA "recommends" it for grant-funded airports and "for land-use planners and developers." *Id.* ¶ 3(1)–(4).

**Respondents' own quotations confirm it.** The specific provisions Respondents invoked are phrased as recommendations: the FAA "recommends against" new wastewater facilities within the separations; it "recommends a distance of 5 miles"; and the parties "should discuss" the location and, "[i]f possible," find an alternative. Cir. Pet. at 35, 37–38 (App. C).

The companion circular Respondents cited is likewise guidance for planning, not a land use regulation. Fed. Aviation Admin., Advisory Circular 150/5190-4B, *Airport Land Use Compatibility Planning* (Sept. 16, 2022).

**The circuit court could not convert guidance into law.** A document that disclaims binding effect cannot supply the "essential requirements of law" against which a local quasi-judicial decision is measured. That is especially so when the Florida Legislature has enacted its own wildlife-attractant siting standard and limited it to landfills. § 333.03(2)(a)–(b), Fla. Stat. (2024). A court may not convert federal guidance into mandatory state or local law by way of a code purpose clause and a statutory conflict rule. Doing so applied the wrong law.

#### C. Whether Evidence Was "Fully Considered" Is a Weight-of-the-Evidence Question the Circuit Court Could Not Decide.

Lacking a mandatory rule, the circuit court rested Ground III on the observation that the FAA advisories "introduced into the record by Petitioners were admittedly not fully considered," which in its view placed "public safety at risk." Order at 6. This is reweighing, plain and simple.

**The governing rule.** The Florida Supreme Court has held, repeatedly and in unmistakable terms, that a circuit court on first-tier review may not reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for the local agency's. *Dusseau*, 794 So. 2d at 1275–76; *Fla. Power & Light*, 761 So. 2d at 1093; *Educ. Dev. Ctr., Inc. v. City of W. Palm Beach Zoning Bd. of Appeals*, 541 So. 2d 106, 108 (Fla. 1989). In *Dusseau*, the Court explained that the circuit court's question is solely whether competent substantial evidence supports the agency's decision. "Evidence contrary to the agency's decision is outside the scope of the inquiry." 794 So. 2d at 1276. In *Omnipoint*, the Court held that a circuit court departed from the essential requirements of law by reweighing the evidence before a zoning board. 863 So. 2d at 199.

**How the Order departs from that rule.** A conclusion that evidence was "not fully considered" is necessarily a judgment about its weight. Respondents introduced the advisories. The Board heard them, along with contrary engineering testimony that:

- the site lies outside the Port Authority's 10,000-foot wildlife buffer;
- the Port Authority supplied the buffer line work used in that analysis;
- the design deviations were adopted specifically "to avoid attracting birds."

Tr. 1 at 226–27 (App. D). The Board adopted the Hearing Examiner's findings that the project complies with the LDC "and other County regulations," is compatible with existing and planned uses, and that its deviations protect "public health, safety, and welfare." Res. § E (App. B).

A local government need not make express findings in a quasi-judicial rezoning so long as the record supports its decision. *Snyder*, 627 So. 2d at 476. The circuit court's task was to ask whether competent substantial evidence supports those findings. It never asked that question. Instead, it credited Respondents' evidence, discounted the County's, and drew its own conclusion that public safety was "at risk." Order at 6. The Order's other independent observations about panther habitat and flow-ways (Order at 2) reflect the same approach.

That is the very error *Dusseau* condemned. It is independently sufficient to warrant second-tier relief.

### II. The Circuit Court Applied the Wrong Law in Holding That an On-the-Record Motion Violated Due Process Under *Jennings*.

#### A. *Jennings* Governs Undisclosed Ex Parte Communications, and None Was Identified.

*Jennings* held that ex parte communications to a quasi-judicial decision-maker are presumed prejudicial. A party proving that such a communication occurred may be entitled to a new hearing unless the presumption is rebutted. 589 So. 2d at 1340–41. The case concerns *communications from outside parties*, received *outside the hearing*, and *not disclosed* to the parties. *Id.*

**Section 286.0115 modified *Jennings*.** In response to *Jennings*, the Legislature enacted section 286.0115, Florida Statutes. It authorizes local governments to adopt procedures under which ex parte communications are *not* presumed prejudicial if their subject and substance are disclosed on the record before final action. § 286.0115(1)(a)–(c), Fla. Stat. (2024).

**What the Order relied on.** The Order identifies no ex parte communication, disclosed or undisclosed. It identifies a single sentence spoken by a commissioner, in public, at the noticed hearing, on the record, while making a motion: "Move the item based upon the direction of the board over that last 4 years, the needs for the community." Tr. 2 at 20:17–19 (App. E). That statement is not a communication *to* the decision-maker from anyone. It is the decision-maker's own articulation of reasons. Nothing about it was hidden from Respondents, who heard it, quoted it, and litigated it.

Extending *Jennings* to a commissioner's on-the-record explanation of a vote is not an application of *Jennings*. It is the creation of a new rule. The Order's citation of an Attorney General opinion does not supply clearly established law. Attorney General opinions are advisory and do not bind courts.

#### B. A Commissioner's Stated Reasons for a Motion Do Not Displace the Board's Record-Based Findings.

**The Board acts through its resolution.** The decision under review is the Board's, expressed in Resolution Z-23-035. It is not the subjective reasoning of a single commissioner. The Resolution recites that the Board gave "full and complete consideration to the recommendations of the staff, the Hearing Examiner, the documents on record and the testimony of all interested persons." It expressly adopts the Hearing Examiner's record-based findings. Res. at 1 & § E (App. B).

**The test is the record.** Under *Snyder*, a quasi-judicial rezoning is upheld if the record contains competent substantial evidence supporting it. No particular statement of reasons is required. 627 So. 2d at 476. If the Board could have adopted the Resolution with no stated reasons at all, a commissioner's shorthand reference to prior Board direction and "the needs for the community" cannot render the Board's decision a due process violation.

**The statement is innocuous.** The reference to "the needs for the community" is precisely the type of public-need consideration that the rezoning criteria require. *See* Lee Cnty., Fla., Land Dev. Code §§ 34-145(d), 34-411 (2024). The reference to the Board's prior "direction" accurately describes a matter of public record: the County's adopted utility master planning, which identified this site and was before the Board in the application materials. Cir. Pet. at 23–24 (App. C).

The circuit court's contrary holding, that the statement, "with no further discussion," "shows prejudice and bias" (Order at 2–3), effectively requires commissioners to recite record evidence when making motions. No Florida authority imposes that requirement. Creating one applied the wrong law.

#### C. A Local Government Does Not Deny Due Process by Deciding Its Own Public-Facility Application.

The Order treats as aggravating the fact that the County was both applicant and decision-maker. Order at 2–3. But local governments routinely rezone their own property for public facilities. The Legislature has assigned zoning authority over County land to the Board, and no other body could have decided this application.

**No presumption of bias arises.** Decision-makers in quasi-judicial proceedings are presumed to act with "honesty and integrity." The combination of investigative or proprietary and adjudicative functions "does not, without more, constitute a due process violation." *Withrow*, 421 U.S. at 47, 58. Nor are decision-makers disqualified because they are familiar with the facts or have taken prior policy positions on matters of public concern. *Hortonville*, 426 U.S. at 493. The Order identifies no clearly established Florida or federal law holding otherwise. It identifies no evidence of actual bias beyond the motion's wording.

Due process in a quasi-judicial zoning proceeding requires notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard. *See Jennings*, 589 So. 2d at 1340. Respondents received both, at two advertised hearings. The circuit court applied the wrong law in holding otherwise.

### III. The Circuit Court Applied the Wrong Law in Finding "Predetermination" From the County's Planning, Proprietary, and Litigation Activities, and in Relying on Materials Outside the Quasi-Judicial Record.

The Order's second ground holds that three earlier County actions "created prejudicial presumptions" that tainted the 2024 decision:

- the 2019 application;
- the purchase of a conservation tract;
- the 2022 Kingston settlement.

Order at 3–5. That holding is wrong for three independent reasons.

**First, planning is not prejudgment.** A local government that provides utility service must plan where its facilities will go, acquire land, and make commitments to developers about future capacity. Those are legislative and proprietary functions. As quoted in the Order, the Kingston settlement recites only that "[t]he County is designing and planning to construct" a facility on Alico Road. Order at 4. It does not purport to bind the Board's quasi-judicial zoning discretion, and it could not lawfully do so.

The consequence of the circuit court's rule is untenable. Any local government that identifies a site in a master plan, buys it, or references it in an agreement would be disqualified from rezoning it. Florida law contains no such rule, and the circuit court cited none. *Hortonville*, 426 U.S. at 493 (prior involvement and policy positions do not disqualify decision-makers).

**Second, the court looked outside the quasi-judicial record.** First-tier certiorari review is confined to the record before the local tribunal. The circuit court determines whether competent substantial evidence in *that* record supports the decision. *Dusseau*, 794 So. 2d at 1275–76; *Vaillant*, 419 So. 2d at 626. Yet the Order's predetermination findings rest substantially on materials from Respondents' Reply Appendix, including the Kingston settlement exhibits. Order at 3–5 (citing Reply App. V3 p. 335; V8 pp. 959–993). Those materials were not part of the evidence presented to the Hearing Examiner on October 19, 2023 or to the Board on January 17, 2024.

A circuit court that makes factual findings from materials outside the record, and then uses them to quash a quasi-judicial decision, has not applied the certiorari standard at all. It has acted as a trial court. That is a departure from clearly established law.

**Third, the "silencing" theory identifies no prejudice.** The Order reasons that the pendency of the 2019 application prevented "public contact with their county commissioners." Order at 3–5. Section 286.0115 *permits* ex parte communications with commissioners, subject to disclosure. It does not prohibit them. § 286.0115(1), Fla. Stat. (2024).

More fundamentally, the relevant due process question is whether Respondents received notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard *in the proceeding under review*. They did. Respondents testified and presented evidence at both the 2023 and 2024 hearings. Tr. 1; Tr. 2 (Apps. D, E). Any limits on informal lobbying during an earlier, different application did not deprive them of a hearing in this one. The Order identifies no point at which Respondents were prevented from presenting evidence or argument to the Board in DCI2023-00011.

### IV. The Circuit Court Exceeded Its Certiorari Jurisdiction by "Reversing" the Board's Decision.

The decretal paragraph of the Order states that "the decision below is REVERSED." Order at 6. That remedy is unavailable in certiorari.

The Florida Supreme Court has squarely held that on certiorari review a court's authority is limited to quashing the order under review. A certiorari court "has no power . . . to enter a judgment on the merits" or to direct any particular action. *G.B.V.*, 787 So. 2d at 843–44. When an order is quashed, the matter stands "pending before the tribunal . . . as if no order or judgment had been entered," and the parties may "proceed further as they may be advised." *Id.* at 844 (quoting *Tamiami Trail Tours*, 174 So. at 454). In *G.B.V.*, the Court quashed a district court decision that had approved a circuit court's directive to the County, because the directive exceeded the scope of certiorari relief. *Id.* at 845–46.

The Order's use of "REVERSED," following an introduction that says "QUASHED," is not a harmless variation in wording. A "reversal" connotes a merits disposition. It invites the argument that the application was effectively denied and cannot be reheard. That is directly contrary to *G.B.V.*

The Order also misidentifies the decision under review as "Resolution Z-23-022." The resolution adopted in DCI2023-00011 is Resolution Z-23-035. That error compounds the uncertainty about what the Order purports to do.

At a minimum, the Court should quash the remedial portion of the Order. It should confirm that any quashal leaves DCI2023-00011 pending before the Board.

### V. The Errors Resulted in a Miscarriage of Justice Warranting Second-Tier Relief.

Second-tier certiorari is not a second appeal. The County recognizes that relief requires more than ordinary legal error. *Heggs*, 658 So. 2d at 530–31; *Custer*, 62 So. 3d at 1092–93. This case meets that standard for three reasons.

**First, the errors are not misapplications of correct law to particular facts.** The Order announces incorrect legal standards:

- that non-binding FAA advisory circulars are mandatory local law through a code purpose clause and section 333.04(2);
- that a commissioner's on-the-record statement of reasons is a *Jennings* violation;
- that a local government's planning, acquisition, and settlement activities disqualify it from deciding its own application.

Each departs from clearly established principles set by the Florida Supreme Court and the Florida Legislature: *Dusseau*, *Snyder*, *G.B.V.*, and the express text of sections 286.0115 and 333.03.

**Second, the errors will recur.** The County's quasi-judicial decisions are reviewed in the same circuit. The Order's airport-compatibility holding would make advisory FAA guidance a mandatory standard for every property within five miles of Southwest Florida International Airport and Page Field. Its due process holdings would expose every County decision on its own facilities to quashal based on the wording of a motion or on the County's prior planning. These are precisely the kinds of errors whose effect "extends beyond the instant case" and which second-tier certiorari exists to correct. *See Custer*, 62 So. 3d at 1092–93.

**Third, the consequences are serious and public.** The Order strips zoning from essential wastewater infrastructure that serves growing communities in the Alico/Corkscrew corridor. The facility is already under construction. It is designed to provide capacity that the County has committed to provide. The result is not a technical irregularity. It is the loss of a lawful, record-supported approval through the application of legal standards that do not exist. That is a miscarriage of justice.

---

## CONCLUSION

The circuit court applied the wrong law in each of its grounds for decision:

- It enforced non-binding federal guidance as mandatory law and reweighed the evidence about it.
- It misapplied *Jennings* to an on-the-record statement.
- It found "predetermination" from ordinary governmental planning, using materials outside the quasi-judicial record.
- It granted a "reversal" that certiorari does not permit.

The County respectfully requests that this Court grant the Petition, issue a writ of certiorari quashing the Order Granting Petition for Writ of Certiorari in its entirety, and thereby leave Resolution Z-23-035 in effect. In the alternative, the County requests that the Court quash the Order to the extent it "REVERSED" the Board's decision, and confirm that Case No. DCI2023-00011 remains pending before the Board for further proceedings as the County may be advised.
