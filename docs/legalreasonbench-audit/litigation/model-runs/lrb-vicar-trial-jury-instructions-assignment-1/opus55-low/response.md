## ARGUMENT

### II. The District Court Properly Refused Patton's Requested Justification Instruction Because No Evidence Supported It.

#### A. Standard of Review

A district court has "broad discretion" in instructing the jury. Its instructions "do not need to be technically perfect or even a model of clarity." White Horse.pdf at 2–3. This Court reviews the refusal to give a requested instruction for abuse of discretion. It will affirm if "the instructions, taken as a whole and viewed in the light of the evidence and applicable law, fairly and adequately submitted the issues in the case to the jury." Kehoe.pdf at 19; *see* White Horse.pdf at 2–3.

A defendant is entitled to a requested instruction "only if 'it correctly states the law and is supported by the evidence.'" Poe.pdf at 3. Whether the evidence was sufficient to submit an affirmative defense to the jury is a question of law reviewed de novo. *Id.* A defendant must show "an 'underlying evidentiary foundation' as to each element of the defense, such that a reasonable person could conclude that the evidence supported the defendant's position." Poe.pdf at 4. Questions of state law decided by the district court are also reviewed de novo. Kehoe.pdf at 16.

The standard is not met just because the defendant points to some evidence that he was fired upon. The question is whether a reasonable jury, looking at the whole record, could find each element of justification. *See* Poe.pdf at 4 (affirming refusal of a justification instruction where "[n]o reasonable jury could find" the required elements, and declining to reach whether the defense was even available, "because [defendant] cannot satisfy the necessary elements"). The district court may refuse an instruction that is "not related to the factual issues to be decided by the jury . . . even though [it] may set out a correct statement of the law." Ellison.pdf at 9 (citation omitted).

#### B. Patton Was Not Entitled to a Justification Instruction Under Any Applicable Standard.

Count 4 charged Patton with attempted murder in aid of racketeering under 18 U.S.C. § 1959. (Facts.pdf at 10.) Section 1959 reaches violent acts "in violation of the laws of any State or the United States." Kehoe.pdf at 10 (quoting 18 U.S.C. § 1959(a)). The Iowa predicate offense here is attempted murder, defined in part as acting "with the intent to cause the death of another person and not under circumstances which would justify the person's actions." 707.11.pdf, Iowa Code § 707.11(1).

This Court has explained that RICO's "allusion to state crimes was not intended to incorporate elements of state crimes." Instead, it identifies "the type of generic conduct" that qualifies as a predicate. Kehoe.pdf at 10 (citation omitted). Congress likewise "did not intend to incorporate the various states's procedural and evidentiary rules into the RICO statute." *Id.* It is therefore an open question whether Iowa's statutory justification scheme governs a § 1959 prosecution at all, as opposed to the federal justification framework set out in *Poe*.

The Court need not resolve that question. *See* Poe.pdf at 4 (declining to reach the availability of the defense where the elements could not be met). Patton was not entitled to the instruction under either standard. Under Iowa law, the defense was categorically unavailable to him and was unsupported on the merits. Under the federal standard, he cannot show an evidentiary foundation for any of the required elements.

##### 1. Iowa law: the justification defense was categorically unavailable to Patton.

Under Iowa law, a person "is justified in the use of reasonable force when the person reasonably believes that such force is necessary to defend oneself or another from any actual or imminent use of unlawful force." 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.3; *see* Ellison.pdf at 5–6. This defense "comes with several important caveats." Ellison.pdf at 6. The most important is § 704.6, where "[t]he legislature . . . lays out the limited circumstances in which a person will have no justification defense." Ellison.pdf at 10 n.1. Section 704.6 provides that "[t]he defense of justification is not available" to:

> 1. One who is participating in a forcible felony, or riot, or a duel.
> . . .
> 3. One who initially provokes the use of force against oneself by one's unlawful acts, unless:
> a. Such force is grossly disproportionate to the provocation, and is so great that the person reasonably believes that the person is in imminent danger of death or serious injury or
> b. The person withdraws from physical contact with the other and indicates clearly to the other that the person desires to terminate the conflict but the other continues or resumes the use of force.

704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.6(1), (3). Both exclusions apply here, and either one is enough.

**a. Patton was "participating in a forcible felony."**

A "forcible felony" includes "any felonious . . . assault [or] murder." 702.11.pdf, Iowa Code § 702.11(1). The record shows Patton was taking part in exactly such a felony when he fired at Nelson's car.

That afternoon, Patton and Shafer got firearms and brought them to Merle Hay Mall. (Facts.pdf at 5.) When the OTB group ran into Raysean Nelson ("Thrax"), a member of the rival C-Block gang, in the mall parking lot, Patton, Shafer, and Denton ran to Mallory's Trailblazer. Nelson and his girlfriend immediately got into their car and sped away. (Facts.pdf at 6–7.) Nelson's girlfriend feared the group was running to get guns, and she knew that some of them, including Patton, were known to carry guns. (Facts.pdf at 7.) Once everyone was in the Trailblazer, Shafer told Mallory to follow Nelson, and Mallory chased Nelson's car "for several miles across the north side of Des Moines." (*Id.*) As the cars approached 31st and Clark Streets, Patton and Shafer rolled down the back windows. (*Id.*) Once the shooting began, "Shafer and Patton repeatedly fired their guns out of the windows of the Trailblazer toward their intended target: C-block member, Thrax." A police dash camera recorded Patton "hanging out of the Trailblazer window shooting his gun." (Facts.pdf at 7–8.)

Firing a gun "at a vehicle in which a person is known to be" is deadly force by statutory definition. 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.2(1)(d). Firing a dangerous weapon into an occupied vehicle "with the intent to injure or provoke fear or anger in another" is a class "C" felony under Iowa's assault chapter. 708.6.pdf, Iowa Code § 708.6(1). Attempted murder is a class "B" felony. 707.11.pdf, Iowa Code § 707.11(2). An armed, multi-mile pursuit of a rival gang member who was fleeing, ending with windows rolled down and repeated shots at the target's car, is participation in a felonious assault or attempted murder. Each is a forcible felony. 702.11.pdf, Iowa Code § 702.11(1).

The gang context confirms this. The OTB group was not a carload of bystanders who happened to be caught in gunfire. OTB's goals included "gain[ing] respect by committing acts of violence, and retaliat[ing] against . . . the opps." (Facts.pdf at 2.) Members "'put[] in work,' or in other words, shot at the opps (C-Block members)." (Facts.pdf at 3.) Patton himself had publicly disrespected C-Block, including appearing in a video "smoking on" a murdered C-Block member. (Facts.pdf at 4.) And the encounter came four days after OTB members drove around Des Moines hunting C-Block-affiliated POG members and shot at them in retaliation. (Facts.pdf at 4–5.)

Patton's own sworn admission removes any doubt. In his state-court plea, he admitted under oath: "On or about May 10, 2020, in Polk County, Iowa, I did conspire with other individuals to commit a forcible felony by planning with others to engage in a shooting." Patton State Plea.pdf at 5; *see id.* at 4 (petition to plead guilty to conspiracy to commit a forcible felony under Iowa Code §§ 706.1 and 706.3). He also certified that "all of the statements are true and correct." Patton State Plea.pdf at 3, 5. Conspiracy under Iowa law requires an agreement made "with the intent to promote or facilitate the commission of" a felony, plus an overt act. 706.1.pdf, Iowa Code § 706.1(1), (3).

So Patton has admitted that on the very day of this shooting, he agreed with others, intending to bring it about, to commit a forcible felony "by planning with others to engage in a shooting." A defendant who has sworn that he planned a shooting with others that day cannot plausibly claim, on that same day's evidence, that his shooting was a spontaneous act of self-defense by an innocent person. The statute is explicit: justification "is not available" to someone "participating in a forcible felony." 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.6(1). The Iowa Supreme Court has listed "participating in a forcible felony" as one of the circumstances in which a person "will have no justification defense to an aggressor." Ellison.pdf at 10 n.1.

**b. Patton "initially provoke[d] the use of force" by his own unlawful acts, and neither exception applies.**

Section 704.6(3) separately bars the defense. It applies to one who "initially provokes the use of force against oneself by one's unlawful acts." 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.6(3). The Iowa Supreme Court has described this as "provoking someone to use force by one's own unlawful acts and then responding with force." Ellison.pdf at 10 n.1.

Patton started this chain of events. He armed himself, even though he was legally barred from possessing a firearm. He later pleaded guilty to being a felon in possession of that very gun on May 10, 2020. (Facts.pdf at 5, 10.) He ran with fellow gang members to the Trailblazer after a confrontation with a rival gang member. He then took part in a chase of that rival across several miles of city streets while armed. (Facts.pdf at 5–7.) By the time Nelson fired, Nelson had already fled the mall once and been pursued for miles by a carload of armed rival gang members, two of whom had just rolled down their windows. (Facts.pdf at 7.) Whatever force Nelson used was provoked by Patton's unlawful acts.

Neither statutory exception applies:

- **No withdrawal.** The withdrawal exception requires that the defendant "withdraw[] from physical contact with the other and indicate[] clearly to the other that the person desires to terminate the conflict," after which the other "continues or resumes the use of force." 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.6(3)(b). The record shows the opposite. Nelson was the one who withdrew, leaving the parking lot immediately. The OTB group then pursued him. (Facts.pdf at 6–7.) Patton never broke off the chase or signaled any wish to end the conflict. Instead, he rolled down his window, leaned out, and fired repeatedly. (Facts.pdf at 7–8.)

- **No grossly disproportionate response.** The other exception applies only when the provoked force "is grossly disproportionate to the provocation." 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.6(3)(a). Patton provoked the encounter with an armed, multi-mile vehicle pursuit of a rival who was fleeing, by gang members known to carry guns, with windows rolled down as they closed in. (Facts.pdf at 7.) Gunfire in response to that threat is not "grossly disproportionate" to it. It is the very kind of response Patton's conduct invited. No reasonable jury could find otherwise.

**c. Iowa's statutory presumption and "stand your ground" provisions do not help Patton.**

Patton cannot rely on Iowa's statutory presumption of reasonableness. That presumption applies only when the person against whom force is used is unlawfully entering, or trying to remove someone from, the defender's dwelling, workplace, or occupied vehicle. 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.2A(1)(a). Nelson did nothing of the kind. In any event, the presumption "does not apply" where "[t]he person using defensive force is engaged in a criminal offense" or "is using the . . . occupied vehicle to further a criminal offense." 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.2A(2)(a). Both conditions describe Patton exactly.

Nor can Patton rely on § 704.1(3), the "stand your ground" provision. It relieves only "[a] person who is not engaged in illegal activity" of the duty to retreat. 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.1(3). "[T]he usual duty to retreat generally still exists if a person is engaged in illegal activity." Ellison.pdf at 8. Iowa courts have found illegal activity in circumstances like these. *See* Ellison.pdf at 13 (illegal possession of a handgun "directly related to the shooting death" disqualified defendant from the stand-your-ground exception (discussing *Lorenzo Baltazar*)); Ellison.pdf at 11–14 (going armed with intent to use a weapon against another may serve as the "illegal activity"). Patton was a felon unlawfully possessing the gun he fired, as he admitted by pleading guilty to Count 6. (Facts.pdf at 5, 10.) He was also going armed in pursuit of a rival. So he had a duty to retreat, and a safe alternative was obviously available. The Trailblazer was the pursuing vehicle. At any point during the multi-mile chase, the OTB group could simply have stopped following Nelson. (Facts.pdf at 7.)

**d. Even setting § 704.6 aside, no evidence supported a reasonable belief that deadly force was necessary.**

Even without the statutory bars, the evidence does not support the core elements of the defense. Justification requires that the defendant "reasonably believe[]" force was "necessary" to defend against "actual or imminent" unlawful force, and that the force used was "reasonable." 704.pdf, Iowa Code §§ 704.1(1), 704.3. "Reasonable force" means "that force and no more which a reasonable person, in like circumstances, would judge to be necessary to prevent an injury or loss." 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.1(1).

A reasonable person who wanted to avoid harm from Nelson would not have run to a car, chased him for miles, and rolled down the windows as the cars closed in. Patton's conduct before the first shot shows that he was seeking out a confrontation, not trying to avoid one. *See* Poe.pdf at 4–5 (no well-grounded apprehension where the defendant's own conduct was inconsistent with fear and he had reasonable alternatives). The physical evidence fits the same picture. The police dash-cam video shows Patton leaning out of the window firing. Shafer and Patton fired "repeatedly" "toward their intended target." (Facts.pdf at 7–8.) The force Patton used was the culmination of a pursuit. It was not the "force and no more" needed to escape danger. 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.1(1).

Because justification was unavailable under § 704.6 and unsupported on its elements, a justification instruction would not have been "related to the factual issues to be decided by the jury." The district court properly declined to give it. Ellison.pdf at 9 (citation omitted).

##### 2. Federal law: Patton cannot establish an evidentiary foundation for any element of justification.

The result is the same under the federal justification framework this Court has applied. A defendant seeking a justification instruction must show an evidentiary foundation for each of four elements:

> (1) that defendant was under an unlawful and "present, imminent, and impending [threat] of such a nature as to induce a well-grounded apprehension of death or serious bodily injury," (2) that defendant had not "recklessly or negligently placed himself in a situation in which it was probable that he would be [forced to choose the criminal conduct]," (3) that defendant had no "reasonable, legal alternative to violating the law, 'a chance both to refuse to do the criminal act and also to avoid the threatened harm,'" and (4) "that a direct causal relationship may be reasonably anticipated between the [criminal] action and the avoidance of the [threatened] harm."

Poe.pdf at 4 (alterations in original) (citation omitted). Failing on any one element is fatal. *Id.* Patton fails on at least three.

**Element 2: Patton recklessly placed himself in the situation.** This element alone disposes of the claim. Patton armed himself that morning despite being prohibited from possessing a firearm. He ran to the car after the encounter with a rival gang member. He rode in a vehicle that chased that rival for miles, with the rival fleeing. And he rolled down his window as the cars closed in. (Facts.pdf at 5–7.) He did all of this against the background of an active cycle of retaliatory shootings between OTB and C-Block. That cycle included a shooting by OTB members just four days earlier. (Facts.pdf at 4–5.) A person who takes part in an armed pursuit of a rival gang member during a gang war has, at the very least, "recklessly . . . placed himself in a situation in which it was probable that he would be [forced to choose the criminal conduct]." Poe.pdf at 4. His sworn admission that he conspired with others that day to commit a forcible felony "by planning with others to engage in a shooting" confirms the point. Patton State Plea.pdf at 5.

**Element 3: Patton had reasonable, legal alternatives.** Patton had "a chance both to refuse to do the criminal act and also to avoid the threatened harm." Poe.pdf at 4. He could have stayed at the mall. He could have let Nelson drive away, which Nelson did immediately. (Facts.pdf at 6–7.) He and the group could have stopped the chase at any point over several miles. (Facts.pdf at 7.) And he could have declined to arm himself unlawfully in the first place. (Facts.pdf at 5, 10.) In *Poe*, this Court held that the defendant had reasonable alternatives because he "could have disposed of or abandoned the gun at any time." Poe.pdf at 5. Patton's alternatives were even more obvious. The only thing keeping him in Nelson's vicinity was the OTB group's own decision to keep chasing.

**Element 1: There was no well-grounded apprehension of an unlawful threat that Patton did not create.** It is true that Nelson fired as the Trailblazer approached and that a gun battle followed. (Facts.pdf at 7.) But the justification defense protects a defendant who faces an unlawful threat that he did not bring on himself. It does not protect an aggressor whose quarry fights back. *See* Poe.pdf at 4 (requiring the defendant not to have placed himself in the situation). Nelson fled from the OTB group and was then hunted across the city by a car full of armed rivals. Whatever threat Patton faced at 31st and Clark was the foreseeable product of his own pursuit. It was not the kind of independent, "present, imminent, and impending" threat that justifies a criminal response. Poe.pdf at 4.

Because Patton cannot show an evidentiary foundation for each element, he was not entitled to the instruction. Poe.pdf at 4–5. As in *Poe*, the Court need not decide whether the defense would otherwise be available, because "[defendant] cannot satisfy the necessary elements." Poe.pdf at 4.

##### 3. Patton's contrary arguments do not establish an evidentiary foundation.

Patton argues that the evidence showed he was acting in self-defense when he fired at Nelson's car, mainly because Nelson was hiding in the bushes and fired first, and because Shafer was shot during the exchange. (P. Br. __.) Those facts do not carry his burden.

*First*, who fired the first shot does not answer the question under Iowa law. Section 704.6 asks whether the defendant was "participating in a forcible felony" or "initially provoke[d] the use of force against oneself by one's unlawful acts." 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.6(1), (3). The provocation here was the armed pursuit, not the first shot. Patton's own sworn admission shows that he and others had planned a shooting that day. Patton State Plea.pdf at 5. Iowa law does not give the justification defense to a participant in a planned forcible felony just because the intended target managed to shoot first.

*Second*, the fact that Nelson waited in the bushes shows only that Nelson, after being chased for miles, expected the pursuers to keep coming. (Facts.pdf at 7.) It is evidence that Patton's group was the aggressor. It is not evidence that Patton reasonably believed deadly force was necessary to escape a threat he did not create. And the fact that Shafer was shot during the gun battle (Facts.pdf at 8) shows only that the danger Patton helped create came to pass. It does not show that Patton was free of fault in bringing it about. *See* Poe.pdf at 4 (element 2).

*Third*, Patton cannot rely on the principle that unlawful activity alone does not strip a person of all right to self-defense. In *Ellison*, the Iowa Supreme Court noted that "[e]ngaging in illegal activity does not, on its own, leave a person defenseless to respond to unlawful force." Ellison.pdf at 10 n.1. But the court went on to explain that § 704.6 sets out the circumstances in which a person has "no justification defense," including "participating in a forcible felony" and "provoking someone to use force by one's own unlawful acts and then responding with force." *Id.* Patton's case falls squarely within those exclusions. It is not a case of a person who happened to be committing some unrelated offense, such as carrying a gun unlawfully, when an unprovoked attack came. *Ellison* itself shows the difference. There, the defendant was sitting in his parked car when the decedent walked up on the sidewalk, and the decedent, who was subject to a no-contact order, appeared ready to fight. Ellison.pdf at 2–4. Here, the OTB group chased Nelson down.

*Fourth*, the case law Patton relies on does not entitle him to an instruction just because he can point to some evidence that bullets were flying in both directions. The defendant must show an evidentiary foundation "as to each element of the defense, such that a reasonable person could conclude that the evidence supported the defendant's position." Poe.pdf at 4. Patton points to evidence about the first element at most. He does not and cannot point to evidence that he did not recklessly place himself in the situation, or that he lacked any reasonable alternative to firing on Nelson's car. Without such evidence, the requested instruction was not "supported by the evidence," and refusing it was proper. Poe.pdf at 3.

#### C. The Instructions as a Whole Fairly and Adequately Submitted the Issues.

This Court affirms where "the instructions, taken as a whole and viewed in the light of the evidence and applicable law, fairly and adequately submitted the issues in the case to the jury." Kehoe.pdf at 19; *see* White Horse.pdf at 3. A district court "is under no requirement 'to give any particular form of an instruction'" and need only "give instructions that fairly state the law as applied to the facts of the case." Ellison.pdf at 9 (citation omitted).

The issue actually in dispute at trial was whether Patton, with the required intent and for the required enterprise purpose, attempted to kill Nelson. The evidence did not support a justification defense, so an instruction on it was not one of the "issues in the case." Kehoe.pdf at 19. Giving it would only have invited the jury to consider a defense the law does not allow on this record. *See* Ellison.pdf at 9 ("Requested instructions that are not related to the factual issues to be decided by the jury should not be submitted even though they may set out a correct statement of the law." (citation omitted)). The jury heard all of Patton's evidence about the gun battle. It was free to weigh that evidence on the elements the government had to prove, including intent. It convicted Patton on every count. (Facts.pdf at 11.)

The district court acted within its broad discretion, and indeed reached the only legally correct result, when it refused to instruct the jury on a justification defense that had no evidentiary foundation. Poe.pdf at 3–5; White Horse.pdf at 2–3.

#### D. Conclusion

Patton armed himself unlawfully. He joined fellow gang members in chasing a fleeing rival for miles. He rolled down his window as they closed in, and he leaned out firing at his "intended target." He has admitted under oath that on that day he conspired with others "to commit a forcible felony by planning with others to engage in a shooting." Patton State Plea.pdf at 5.

Under Iowa law, the justification defense "is not available" to someone in that position. 704.pdf, Iowa Code § 704.6(1), (3). Under federal law, he cannot show an evidentiary foundation for the required elements. Poe.pdf at 4–5. The Court should reject Patton's claim of instructional error and affirm his convictions.
