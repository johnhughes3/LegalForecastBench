## Supplemental response concerning Patton’s requested justification instruction

### The district court properly declined Patton’s requested justification instruction.

Patton was charged with attempted murder in aid of racketeering for firing at Raysean Nelson’s car. The state-law predicate matters: the VICAR statute reaches an attempt to commit a crime of violence “in violation of” state or federal law, and Iowa defines attempted murder to require an act undertaken with intent to cause death “and not under circumstances which would justify” the defendant’s actions. (Kehoe.pdf at 9; 707.11.pdf at 1.) Iowa’s justification statutes therefore supply the relevant defense rules.

A requested instruction must correctly state the law and be supported by evidence. The Eighth Circuit reviews instructions for abuse of discretion and asks whether, considered as a whole, they fairly and adequately submitted the issues; whether evidence sufficiently supports an affirmative-defense instruction is reviewed de novo. (White Horse.pdf at 2; Poe.pdf at 3.) Under Poe, the defendant must identify an evidentiary foundation for each element of the proposed defense. (Poe.pdf at 3–4.) Iowa likewise requires substantial evidence before an instruction is submitted, even when the instruction states the law correctly. Once a defendant raises justification, the State bears the burden to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the force was not justified, but that burden does not eliminate the threshold requirement that substantial evidence support the defense. (Ellison.pdf at 9; State v. Kuhse, No. 18-0765, slip op. at 9 (Iowa Apr. 8, 2020).)

Iowa permits reasonable force when a person reasonably believes it necessary to defend against actual or imminent unlawful force. Deadly force is reasonable only when the person reasonably believes it necessary to avoid injury or a risk to life or safety; discharging a firearm at a vehicle known to contain a person is deadly force. (704.pdf at 1–2, §§ 704.1(1), 704.2(1)(d), 704.3.) The defense is unavailable, however, to a person participating in a forcible felony, or to one who initially provokes force with intent to use it as an excuse to injure the other person. A person who initially provokes force through unlawful acts also may not claim justification unless the force is grossly disproportionate and creates a reasonable belief of imminent death or serious injury, or the person withdraws and clearly indicates a desire to end the conflict but the other person continues or resumes using force. (704.pdf at 2, § 704.6.) The statute’s no-duty-to-retreat provision applies only to a person who is not engaged in illegal activity and is lawfully present. (704.pdf at 1, § 704.1(3).)

The evidence shows that Patton was not an innocent bystander suddenly forced to defend himself. He and Shafer obtained firearms on the morning of May 10 and brought them to the mall. After the group encountered Nelson, Shafer and Patton ran to Mallory’s vehicle while Nelson and Allison immediately fled in their car. At Shafer’s direction, Mallory pursued them for several miles. As the cars approached Nelson’s location, Patton and Shafer rolled down the back windows. They then repeatedly fired toward their intended target, Nelson; at one point Patton leaned out of the window and fired. (Facts.pdf at 5–7.)

Patton’s own state-court plea petition makes the planning explicit. It identifies Count VIII as conspiracy to commit a forcible felony under Iowa Code sections 706.1 and 706.3, and Patton admitted that on May 10 he “did conspire with other individuals to commit a forcible felony by planning with others to engage in a shooting.” (Patton State Plea.pdf at 3–4.) Section 706.1 defines conspiracy to include an agreement, made with intent to promote or facilitate a felony, to engage in the crime or aid its planning or commission. (706.1.pdf at 1.) His admission, coupled with the trial evidence that he armed himself, joined the pursuit, and fired repeatedly at the pursued target, establishes participation in the planned forcible shooting—not merely association with a gang or possession of a gun. Iowa Code section 704.6(1) makes justification unavailable to a participant in a forcible felony. (704.pdf at 2, § 704.6(1); 702.11.pdf at 1.)

Patton will emphasize that Nelson opened fire as the Trailblazer approached and that shots were fired at the Trailblazer. (Facts.pdf at 7–8.) That is the strongest fact for his claim, but it does not erase the preceding pursuit or his own participation in the planned shooting. Iowa’s forcible-felony bar addresses whether someone engaged in that felony may justify his use of force; it does not turn on who fired the first shot in the final moments of an encounter that the defendant helped pursue and escalate. Nor does the existence of incoming fire itself establish that Patton’s repeated shots toward Nelson were a reasonable and necessary defensive response under sections 704.1 and 704.3. On this record, the district court was not required to isolate the last seconds of the gun battle from the conduct immediately preceding it.

The provocation provisions reinforce that conclusion. The group’s flight to the vehicle followed by a multi-mile pursuit of Nelson, despite his attempt to leave, is evidence of an initiated and sustained confrontation, not a withdrawal from one. Patton’s admission that the group planned to engage in a shooting supplies evidence of the intent behind that conduct. (Facts.pdf at 6–7; Patton State Plea.pdf at 4.) Nothing in the supplied record suggests that Patton withdrew and clearly communicated a desire to terminate the conflict. Even if Nelson’s shots could support Patton’s claim of imminent danger under the grossly-disproportionate-force proviso for unlawful provocation, that proviso does not displace the separate bar against justification for a participant in a forcible felony. (704.pdf at 2, § 704.6(1), (3); Facts.pdf at 6–8.)

This argument does not depend on treating all illegal activity as an automatic bar to self-defense. Ellison explains that illegal activity alone does not always leave a person without a justification defense; the applicable statutory exceptions must be considered. (Ellison.pdf at 10 n.1.) But this record presents more than illegal activity in the abstract: Patton admitted planning a forcible shooting and then participated in the armed pursuit and firing. The no-duty-to-retreat provision does not assist him because he was engaged in that illegal activity, although the forcible-felony bar—not retreat alone—is dispositive here. (Ellison.pdf at 8–10; 704.pdf at 1–2, §§ 704.1(3), 704.6(1).)

### Kuhse confirms the evidentiary threshold, but its instruction-wide prejudice analysis does not control this claim.

Kuhse confirms that justification is an affirmative defense and that the defendant must first produce sufficient evidence to support submitting it. Only after that threshold is met does the State bear the burden to prove lack of justification beyond a reasonable doubt. (Kuhse, slip op. at 9.) The decision therefore reinforces the distinction between the State’s burden once justification is properly raised and the defendant’s antecedent burden to establish an evidentiary basis for the requested instruction. The burden shift cannot itself supply evidence that the defense is legally available.

Kuhse’s holding arose in a materially different instructional posture. There, the marshaling instruction omitted a reference to justification, but the jury received eight other instructions addressing that defense, including an express statement that the State had to prove the defendant was not justified. The defense and prosecution also addressed justification in closing. Considering all the instructions, the arguments, and the evidence, the Iowa Supreme Court concluded that the omission did not create a reasonable probability of a different outcome on the defendant’s ineffective-assistance claim. (Id. at 13–14.) Kuhse did not hold that other instructions can substitute for a complete refusal to instruct when the defendant has produced substantial supporting evidence. Its analysis is record-specific and expressly considers both the evidence and the instructions as a whole. (Id. at 10, 13–14.)

That distinction does not aid Patton. He does not identify an omission from a marshaling instruction that was otherwise accompanied by a full set of justification instructions; he challenges the denial of his requested defense instruction. The pertinent question is therefore whether the record supports giving the instruction under the governing evidentiary threshold and whether Iowa’s statutory bars foreclose the defense. Nelson’s firing as the Trailblazer approached is evidence Patton may rely on as to imminent force, but it does not overcome the evidence that Patton was participating in a planned forcible shooting and joined the armed pursuit and attack. (Facts.pdf at 5–8; Patton State Plea.pdf at 3–4.) The statutory bar applies, the record supplies no evidence of withdrawal, and any potential application of § 704.6(3)’s provocation exception cannot overcome the independent forcible-felony bar in § 704.6(1). Kuhse thus supports the threshold inquiry; it does not require an instruction on a defense that the record and Iowa law make unavailable.

Because Iowa law foreclosed justification for a participant in the forcible felony shown by Patton’s own admission and the trial evidence, he lacked the evidentiary foundation required for the requested instruction. Alternatively, the complete sequence—arming, pursuing a fleeing target, and repeatedly shooting at him—does not support treating Patton’s conduct as a reasonably necessary response to unlawful force. The district court acted within its discretion in refusing the instruction, and its ruling should be affirmed.
