## Bottom line

1. **The DCCA cannot consider the Kucera case, and neither can PERB.**
   - It is not in the arbitration record or the PERB record. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 4.
   - Review under § 1-605.02(6) is limited to what is "on [the] face" of the Award. § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6); FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 2.
   - The DCCA reviews PERB's decision for substantial evidence and will not rely even on record material that PERB did not cite. FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1; p. 5–6, n. 2.
   - MPD never raised the case before PERB. FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2.
   - **Therefore the case changes nothing in the formal legal analysis on review.**
   - **It matters a great deal in three practical ways:**
     - It changes how MPD may responsibly characterize its own disciplinary practice.
     - It changes the risk of the remand remedy that Step 3 recommended.
     - It makes MPD vulnerable if the case surfaces later.

2. **Contrary-to-law arguments.**
   - **Unchanged:**
     - the lead argument that the arbitrator ignored Douglas's "[o]nly if" limit on substituting a penalty;
     - the argument that factor 6 was decided under a "prove" (proof) standard rather than a reasonableness standard;
     - the factor-12 argument that the arbitrator demanded "evidence" for a judgment and that his finding conflicts with his own factor-10 finding;
     - the argument that PERB's decision is unreasoned and should be remanded.
   - **Weakened:**
     - the factor-6 argument as Step 3 framed it, which relied on all comparators having been terminated and on the arbitrator's miscount;
     - the argument built on assumption (c) that Braddock had a fair opportunity to raise disparity;
     - the already-secondary § 1621.6 waiver argument;
     - the practical value of a remand.

3. **Public policy.**
   - **Doctrinally:** unaffected. A negotiated settlement is not a "law[] [or] legal precedent[]," so the case neither supplies nor removes a source for the policy. W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2; Misco, 484 U.S. at 43, ¶ 1.
   - **Practically:** weakened. MPD itself kept on duty a sergeant whose off-duty discharge at an unarmed man was found outside policy. That makes it hard to argue credibly that reinstating Braddock is intolerable to a "dominant" policy. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶¶ 2–3.

4. **The arbitrator's factor-6 finding.**
   - **As a matter of review:** no stronger. It must stand or fall on the record and reasoning the arbitrator actually had. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
   - **On the merits:** stronger. Kucera is the closest comparator now known, and it matches the distinctions the arbitrator drew (off duty, no fatality, lighter record). The penalty she received (40 days) is very close to the penalty he imposed (45 days). Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶¶ 2–3; Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 7, 9.
   - **But it is distinguishable on several important points.** See Part II.B.

5. **Burden.** The burden position still holds, with a qualification. Douglas places the burden of proving the *facts* on which an agency relies, including "consistency with other agency actions," on the agency. Douglas, p. 12 (printed 324), ¶ 4. So MPD should frame the arbitrator's error as demanding proof of a *judgment* (comparability). It should not argue that MPD bore no burden on factor 6.

6. **Preservation.** The preservation position also holds, but only as to the four comparators. Braddock cannot have waived a disparity claim based on a case that was in a database MPD's own search did not cover and that fell outside the years MPD searched. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 1; Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 1; DCMR, p. 2, § 1621.6 ("known" defenses).

## II. Whether the supplemental comparator can be considered on a petition for review

**Conclusion: No.** Neither MPD nor FOP can put the Kucera case before PERB or the DCCA in this proceeding. Six independent reasons support that conclusion.

**1. The statute confines review to the face of the Award.**

- PERB may set aside an award only if "the award **on its face** is contrary to law and public policy" (or on the jurisdiction and fraud grounds, which do not apply here). § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6) (emphasis added).
- PERB lacks authority to act "[a]bsent a clear violation of law — one evident on the face" of the award. FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 2.
- A comparator that the arbitrator never saw cannot show an error "evident on the face" of his Award.
- The DCCA asks whether PERB's application of that same standard was "rationally indefensible." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1. PERB cannot be faulted for failing to consider evidence no one gave it.

**2. The DCCA reviews PERB's decision on the agency record.**

- The court defers to PERB's findings "if they are supported by 'substantial evidence.'" FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1. It upheld PERB in FOP/DOC because "the PERB decision is supported by substantial evidence in the record." FOP/DOC, p. 1, ¶ 1; p. 5, ¶ 3.
- The court will not even rely on material that *was* in the agency record if PERB did not rely on it. In FOP/DOC, the arbitrator's post-award letter "was part of the agency record," but because "the PERB did not cite it as a reason for its decision," the court "may not look to the letter." FOP/DOC, p. 5–6, n. 2. Material that was never in the record at all is even further out of reach.
- That footnote addresses *affirmance*. The same point applies to MPD seeking *reversal*: the court reviews the reasons PERB gave on the record PERB had. FOP/DOC, p. 5–6, n. 2; p. 3, ¶ 1.

**3. The objection was never presented to PERB.**

- "[N]o . . . objection to an order of the Board shall be considered . . . unless such . . . objection was first urged before the Board." FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2 (quoting D.C. Code § 1-617.13(b)).
- MPD's three arguments to PERB did not mention Kucera, and could not have, since it was unknown to counsel. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2.
- This bar would apply equally to FOP if FOP tried to use the case to defend the Award.

**4. Misco forbids reviewing bodies from finding facts or relying on evidence outside the arbitration.**

- A court may not reject an arbitrator's factual findings "simply because it disagrees with them." Misco, 484 U.S. at 38, ¶ 1.
- Misco held that a court could not refuse enforcement because the arbitrator declined to consider "evidence unknown to the Company at the time" of discharge. Such evidentiary and procedural questions "are to be left to the arbitrator." Misco, 484 U.S. at 39, ¶ 3; 484 U.S. at 40, ¶ 1.
- Weighing Kucera against Braddock would be "an exercise in factfinding," which "exceeds the authority of a court asked to overturn an arbitration award." Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 3; 484 U.S. at 45, ¶ 1. "If additional facts were to be found, the arbitrator should find them." Misco, 484 U.S. at 45, ¶ 1.
- As Part I.C shows, the comparison is fact-intensive. It turns on injury, provocation, rank, record, and remorse. That is exactly the kind of judgment Misco reserves to the arbitrator.

**5. The parties bargained for the arbitrator's evaluation of the evidence.**

- PERB held that "[t]he parties bargained for the Arbitrator's interpretation" and his "evaluation of the evidence." PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4.
- The DCCA has said the same. FOP/DOC, p. 5–6, n. 3.
- An after-discovered comparator is an argument that the evidence should have been different. It is not an argument that the Award is facially contrary to law.

**6. The Douglas framework points the same way, by analogy.** Douglas notes that "judicial review of Board decisions is limited to the record established before the Board." Douglas, p. 5 (printed 317), ¶ 1. This describes the federal scheme and is not binding on the DCCA, but it confirms the general principle.

**Where Kucera *could* surface.**

- The material question is not whether the court can consider Kucera. It cannot. The question is whether the case could re-enter the dispute through some other route.
- The main risk is **a remand to the arbitrator.** Step 3 recommended asking for a remand to PERB. If PERB in turn remands to Arbitrator Larrabee for him to apply Douglas correctly, the record could reopen.
  - Douglas allows a decision-maker to invite "additional information relating to possible alternative penalties." Douglas, p. 12 (printed 324), ¶ 1.
  - Douglas also allows the decision-maker to raise "genuine issues of justice or equity" on his or her own initiative. Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
  - Misco contemplates that further facts are found "in the course of any further effort" before the arbitrator. Misco, 484 U.S. at 45, ¶ 1.
- On a reopened record, Kucera is the union's best exhibit. The proposed 60-day penalty and the 40-day settlement are almost exactly where the arbitrator landed (45 days). Award, p. 2, ¶ 9.

**Disclosure obligations.** The uploaded materials do not address what MPD or counsel must disclose about after-discovered comparator information. I flag the issue but do not resolve it. Separate advice is needed from sources outside this record before any brief is filed. At a minimum, nothing in the brief should say or imply anything the Kucera case contradicts (see Part IV.D).

## III. Whether the arbitrator stayed within the required scope of review, as affected by Kucera

### A. The standard (unchanged)

- The arbitrator held that his review was "not de novo." He framed the question as whether MPD's conclusions "fall within tolerable limits of reasonableness." Award, p. 2, ¶ 5.
- Douglas allows a reviewer to substitute a penalty "[o]nly if" the agency "failed to weigh the relevant factors, or . . . the agency's judgment clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.
- The reviewer's role "is not to insist that the balance be struck precisely where [it] would choose to strike it." Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2.

### B. Factor 6: the "prove comparability" ruling

**1. The scope-of-review error is unchanged.**

- The arbitrator held that MPD "did not prove that the comparator officers were similarly situated" and "failed to carry its burden to prove comparability." Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
- That is a proof standard applied to a judgment call. Under assumption (a), reasonable minds can disagree about comparability, so MPD's judgment fell within "tolerable limits of reasonableness" by definition. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5.
- **Kucera does not change this.** The arbitrator reasoned only from the four summaries before him. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7 ("Having reviewed the comparator summaries"). Whether he applied the right standard is judged from the text of the Award. § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6).

**2. Does the factor-6 finding look stronger or weaker? It depends on the question asked.**

- **As a legal ruling on review: no stronger.**
  - Douglas forbids "mathematical rigidity or perfect consistency." It requires only that "the substance of equity in relation to genuinely similar cases [be] preserved." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2.
  - The arbitrator's error was in the *method*: he disqualified each comparator on a single surface difference and demanded "proof." Award, p. 2, ¶ 7. Douglas warns against "surface consistency" in either direction. Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2.
  - Evidence that came to light later cannot cure a methodological error on the face of the Award, and cannot reveal one either.
- **As a substantive outcome: noticeably stronger.**
  - The arbitrator suspected that MPD's four comparators did not capture how MPD treats *off-duty* firearm cases by officers *without* fatalities or serious force histories. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7. Kucera partly confirms that suspicion.
  - The one known off-duty, non-fatal, out-of-jurisdiction firearm discharge at an unarmed man produced a proposed 60-day suspension and a settled 40-day suspension. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 3.
  - Douglas treats a comparable case resolved with a lesser penalty as the classic disparity claim. Douglas, p. 33 (printed 345), ¶¶ 2–4.
- **Douglas also shows how MPD would rebut a disparity claim based on Kucera.**
  - In Douglas, the comparable supervisor's case ended in suspension rather than the proposed removal because of "years of good service," "offer to make restitution," and "good character attestations." The Board held that this lesser penalty did not show disparity, because the agency "could reasonably decide not to reduce the penalty" for appellants whose circumstances differed. Douglas, p. 33 (printed 345), ¶¶ 4–5; n. 83 (rehabilitation potential as a valid distinguishing factor).
  - Kucera can be distinguished on the same kinds of grounds:
    - **Injury.** No injury, versus a near-fatal shooting.
    - **Crime in progress.** An actual offense in progress, versus no crime at all.
    - **Prior record.** No prior record, versus a 2021 reprimand.
    - **Responsibility.** A negotiated acceptance of discipline, versus refusal to accept responsibility.

    Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶¶ 2–3; Final Notice, p. 2, ¶¶ 9–10; p. 3, ¶¶ 16, 23–24.
  - **Settlement versus adjudication.** A negotiated 40-day suspension with a waiver of grievance rights reflects a compromise of litigation risk. It is not a reasoned agency penalty decision in the Douglas sense. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 3; DCMR, p. 1, §§ 1606.3–1606.4. MPD's *proposed* 60 days is the better measure of MPD's judgment, and it is still a suspension.
  - **Rank cuts both ways.** Kucera is a sergeant. Douglas treats a "supervisory . . . role" as aggravating. Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 1; DCMR, p. 1, § 1606.2(b). FOP will argue that MPD treated the *more* senior officer more leniently. MPD can respond only on the distinctions above.
- **Net.** Kucera makes the arbitrator's *result* on factor 6 look more defensible, though still debatable. It leaves his *method* exactly as flawed as before. Because review is confined to the face of the Award, the method is what MPD should argue.

### C. Factor 12: the "likely would have deterred" ruling

- **The scope-of-review error is unchanged.**
  - The arbitrator faulted MPD for offering "no evidence . . . beyond the deciding official's assertion." He then substituted his own prediction that a suspension "would likely have sufficed." Award, p. 2, ¶ 8.
  - Douglas requires only "a concise statement of [the agency's] reasoning" that is not "inherently irrational." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
  - The arbitrator's finding also conflicts with his own sustained findings that confidence in Braddock is "lost" and his potential for rehabilitation is "poor." Award, p. 2, ¶ 6; Final Notice, p. 3, ¶¶ 18, 23.
- **The rhetoric is weaker, though not the law.**
  - The deciding official reasoned that anything short of removal "would signal to the workforce that such conduct is survivable." Final Notice, p. 4, ¶ 25.
  - Kucera shows MPD had already sent that signal for an off-duty unjustified discharge, seven months before Braddock's shooting. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 3; Final Notice, p. 1, ¶ 1.
  - The deterrence point regarding Braddock himself (specific deterrence) is unaffected, because it rests on his refusal to accept responsibility. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 23.
  - The deterrence point regarding other officers (general deterrence) is now exposed. In any brief, MPD should lead with deterrence of Braddock himself and the factor-10 inconsistency, and should not press the "workforce signal" line hard.

## IV. Burden and preservation

### A. Which party bore the burden on the comparator evidence: the position holds, with a sharper framing

**What Step 3 argued.** Disparity is the *employee's* contention, which the agency "remain[s] free" to rebut. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65. Where "no issue has been raised concerning the penalty," a prima facie case "will normally suffice." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.

**What still holds.** The legal allocation does not depend on facts discovered later. No disparity issue was raised. Assumption (b).

**The qualification Kucera forces.**

- Douglas also says that where the agency's penalty "rests upon considerations of fact," including "consistency with other agency actions," "the burden is on the agency to so establish them." Douglas, p. 12 (printed 324), ¶ 4.
- MPD affirmatively relied on factor 6 as *aggravating*. It asserted that "[t]ermination here is consistent with the discipline imposed on those similarly situated members." Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 19.
- So MPD bore the burden of proving the *facts* behind that assertion: that the four cases existed and ended in termination. MPD did prove those facts, and they were undisputed. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 2–5.
- What MPD did not bear was a burden to *prove* the evaluative conclusion that those cases were "similarly situated." That judgment is reviewed for reasonableness. Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶¶ 1–2.
- **Recommended framing.** "MPD proved the facts on which it relied. The arbitrator erred by converting MPD's reasonable judgment about comparability into a fact MPD had to 'prove.'" Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
- **Framing to avoid.** "MPD bore no burden on factor 6." Douglas, p. 12 (printed 324), ¶ 4, contradicts it, and on any reopened record Kucera would show that MPD's description of its own practice was incomplete.
- **The general burden of persuasion is still MPD's.** Douglas places "the ultimate burden . . . upon the agency to persuade . . . of the appropriateness of the penalty." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 3. That point was already a weakness in Step 3. Kucera makes it more dangerous on remand, because the union would have a concrete exhibit for a "genuine issue[] of justice or equity." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.

### B. Consequence of Braddock's failure to raise disparate treatment: holds as to the four, does not reach Kucera

**1. Preservation as to the four comparators: unchanged.**

- Braddock knew of the four comparators before the decision, had counsel and a hearing, and raised no disparity claim. Assumptions (b) and (c); Final Notice, p. 1, ¶ 2.
- Douglas's "fair opportunity to respond" was satisfied as to those four. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), ¶ 2.

**2. Preservation as to Kucera: no waiver argument is available.**

- Assumption (c) covers only the four comparators. Nothing in the file suggests Braddock knew of a settlement that MPD's own adverse-action search did not capture and that fell outside the search window. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 1; Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 1.
- The regulation Step 3 identified waives only a "**known** defense, fact, or matter." DCMR, p. 2, § 1621.6 (emphasis added).
- That argument was already secondary because of the exclusion of sworn MPD members, DCMR, p. 1, § 1600.2(g), and because it was not preserved before PERB. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2. It now has an additional defect: on its own terms it cannot reach Kucera.
- **Recommendation:** drop § 1621.6 entirely.

**3. The fairness argument built on assumption (c) is weaker.**

- Step 3 argued that Braddock had a fair chance to contest the comparator evidence and chose not to.
- That argument assumes the agency's comparator set fairly represented its practice. Kucera undercuts that assumption.
- If the arbitration record is ever reopened, FOP will argue that Braddock's silence was a response to an incomplete picture that MPD itself assembled.
- On the existing record, assumption (b) still means no disparity issue was raised and the arbitrator raised factor 6 without a claim from the grievant. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7. That remains true and arguable.

### A. Contrary to law

### B. Contrary to public policy: unaffected in doctrine, weakened in practice

**1. Doctrinally unaffected.**

- The test requires an "explicit," "well defined and dominant" policy "ascertained by reference to the laws and legal precedents," and a violation "clearly shown." W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2; Misco, 484 U.S. at 43, ¶ 1; FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2.
- The weakest point identified in Step 3 was the *source* of the policy: General Order 120.21, an internal rule documented only through the Final Notice. Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 12; Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 1.
- A negotiated settlement of one officer's discipline is not a law or legal precedent, so it neither supplies nor defeats a source.
- The case also cannot be considered on review. Part II.
- The argument's application to the face of the Award is unchanged. That application rests on the arbitrator's findings that a reasonable officer "would not have perceived an imminent threat," and on reinstatement to Braddock's "position of record." Award, p. 1, ¶ 4; p. 2, ¶ 10.

**2. Practically weakened.**

- The argument's strength in Step 3 was that the Award forces MPD to return deadly-force authority to an officer found to have used deadly force with no objectively reasonable basis. Award, p. 1, ¶ 4; p. 2, ¶ 10.
- MPD itself returned that authority to Sgt. Kucera after an off-duty discharge at an unarmed man that was found outside policy, and it never sought her removal. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶¶ 2–3.
- MPD's own practice therefore cuts against characterizing the policy as one that *demands* removal, which is what the "dominant" element and the "clearly shown" element require in practice. W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2; Misco, 484 U.S. at 43, ¶ 1.
- If Kucera ever surfaces, MPD's public-policy position would look inconsistent with its own conduct, and that would hurt MPD's credibility on its other arguments.

**3. Limits on the damage.**

- The Bell memo states only that the discharge was "outside policy." It does not say Kucera was charged under General Order 120.21, Part A-12, or that the UFRB made an objective-reasonableness finding. Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 3; compare Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 12.
- Kucera faced an actual offense in progress and a man who "turned toward her." Supp. Disclosure, p. 1, ¶ 2. Braddock faced a man who had committed no crime. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 24.
- Kucera's round caused no injury. Braddock's two rounds nearly killed Mr. Pryor. Final Notice, p. 2, ¶¶ 9–10.
- **Recommendation.** If the public-policy argument is kept, confine it strictly to the arbitrator's own findings and the remedy of reinstatement to an armed position. Do not claim that every outside-policy deadly-force discharge requires removal.

### C. Net effect on viability

- Step 3 rated viability "low but not zero," with contrary-to-law as the better ground and remand as the realistic remedy.
- **Formally, that rating is unchanged.** Kucera cannot enter the review record. Part II.
- **Practically, viability is somewhat lower.** Two things drive that:
  - The equities now favor the Award's outcome. The arbitrator's 45-day suspension sits near MPD's own treatment of the closest known comparator.
  - The most realistic remedy, remand, carries a new risk: a reopened record that would include Kucera.
- MPD should decide whether to petition knowing that a "win" on remand could end with the same penalty, this time grounded in comparator evidence the arbitrator never had before.

### D. Drafting guardrails for any petition

1. Argue factor 6 as an **error of standard** ("prove" versus "tolerable limits of reasonableness"). Do not argue it as a claim that MPD's practice is uniformly termination. Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 5, 7.
2. Do not describe the four comparators as MPD's complete or exclusive practice. Describe them as what the record contains. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 1.
3. Do not say MPD bore "no burden" on factor 6. Douglas, p. 12 (printed 324), ¶ 4.
4. Drop § 1621.6. DCMR, p. 2, § 1621.6; p. 1, § 1600.2(g).
5. On factor 12, lead with specific deterrence and the factor-10 inconsistency. Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 6, 8.
6. Narrow the public-policy argument as described in Part V.B.3.
7. Do not try to introduce Kucera, and do not argue facts that would require it. Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 3; 484 U.S. at 45, ¶ 1; FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2.
