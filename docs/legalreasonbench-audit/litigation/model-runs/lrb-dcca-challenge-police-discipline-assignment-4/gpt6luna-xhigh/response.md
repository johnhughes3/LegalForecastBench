# MPD v. PERB — Supplemental Comparator Disclosure

## Bottom line

The Kucera matter makes MPD’s factor-six position materially weaker on the merits. It is a closer comparator than the four cases before the arbitrator: Kucera was an off-duty MPD officer who fired her service weapon at an unarmed person, the Use of Force Review Board found the discharge outside policy, and the Department ultimately imposed a 40-day suspension and one-year supervisory probation through settlement. Braddock’s conduct was more serious in important respects—he fired twice, struck and gravely injured the unarmed man, and had a prior reprimand—but the Kucera outcome gives the arbitrator’s conclusion that termination was not shown to be consistent with discipline in similar cases more factual support. (Memorandum from Marcus T. Bell, Labor Relations Liaison, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Outside Counsel, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (Braddock), re: Supplemental Comparator Case Identified After Close of Arbitration Record (Feb. 24, 2026) (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 2–3; D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. That merits assessment does not change the record-based question on a petition for review: the Kucera case was not before the arbitrator or PERB and should not be treated as evidence in the DCCA review of whether the Award is facially contrary to law. The factor-six challenge is weaker even apart from the record bar because the Award’s use of “prove” is not itself an erroneous burden rule: Douglas places the ultimate burden of persuasion on the agency, and the stipulated fact that reasonable minds can disagree about comparator similarity makes the award harder to characterize as a clear legal error. (Memorandum from Marcus T. Bell, Labor Relations Liaison, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. The factor-twelve theory survives: MPD can still argue that the arbitrator announced deferential review but displaced the deciding official’s reasoned deterrence judgment with his own prediction that a lengthy suspension “would likely” suffice. Kucera does not materially change that legal argument because it was outside the arbitration record and supplies no evidence about whether that sanction deterred Kucera, Braddock, or other officers. It modestly weakens the force of MPD’s factual assertion that discipline short of removal necessarily signals that such conduct is “survivable,” if the case were ever properly considered. The burden and timing positions otherwise remain: MPD retained the ultimate burden to justify the penalty; an unchallenged penalty ordinarily needs only prima facie support, but the penalty was submitted to arbitration as an express issue. Braddock’s failure to allege disparate treatment before the arbitration hearing supports MPD’s position that its four-case presentation was not then subject to a specific comparator challenge and that Braddock had notice of those cases before the Department’s guilt decision. The supplemental comparator does not change the governing public-policy test or cure the existing problem with that exception. As a background matter it is mildly adverse to MPD’s rhetoric that reinstatement after any unjustified off-duty firearm discharge is inherently incompatible with public policy: the Department previously resolved Kucera’s case through a negotiated, non-removal sanction after an off-duty, out-of-policy discharge. But Kucera’s case does not establish a rule requiring or forbidding Braddock’s reinstatement, and it was not before the arbitrator or PERB. On the scope question directly: factor six is not shown to be outside the arbitrator’s review authority. He considered an enumerated penalty factor and applied the correct general burden rule; the challenge is principally to his factual assessment of similarity, not to a facial legal command. Factor twelve presents a colorable concern that he replaced the deciding official’s prediction with his own, but the Award’s evidentiary explanation and MPD’s burden prevent that concern from being a clear facial violation. The fifth comparator does not alter either record-specific conclusion. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. ## 1. Factor six: a closer comparator weakens MPD’s challenge

### A. The Kucera case is materially closer, though not identical

The four cases in the arbitration record involved two on-duty events, one fatality, and one officer with two prior sustained force complaints. Braddock’s conduct was off duty, nonfatal, and followed one unrelated reprimand. The arbitrator relied on those distinctions to find that MPD had not proved the four officers similarly situated. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 7; Memorandum from Yolanda Britt, Director, Disciplinary Review Division, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Sandra M. Delgado, Assistant Chief, Professional Development Bureau, re: Comparator Discipline Cases, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841 (Braddock) (Apr. 11, 2024) (comparator_discipline_cases.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 2–5.)

Kucera shares several central characteristics absent from those four cases: she was an off-duty sworn MPD member; she used her service pistol against an unarmed person; the discharge was found outside policy; and the Department imposed a suspension rather than removal. Kucera fired once and missed, had no prior discipline, and encountered a person after hearing glass break and seeing him reach into her vehicle and turn toward her. Braddock advanced on a dazed man who had committed no offense, fired twice without seeing a weapon, hit him in the chest and shoulder, and caused extensive injuries; Braddock also had a prior reprimand. These differences are substantial, but they do not erase the comparator’s probative force. As stipulated, reasonable minds can disagree on comparability; neither side can turn the comparison into a categorical rule. (Memorandum from Marcus T. Bell, Labor Relations Liaison, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Outside Counsel, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (Braddock), re: Supplemental Comparator Case Identified After Close of Arbitration Record (Feb. 24, 2026) (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 2–3; D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 7–10; PDF p. 3 ¶ 16; Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed p. 333, PDF p. 21, ¶ 2 beginning “In considering whether the agency’s judgment.”)

The actual Kucera disposition makes the arbitrator’s bottom-line factor-six finding look stronger on the enlarged factual picture: the Department proposed 60 days, settled for 40 days without pay plus a year of supervisory probation, and the matter was not grieved or appealed. A settled penalty is not a merits ruling and does not compel equal treatment in a case with materially worse facts. Still, it is an actual Department resolution showing that removal was not imposed in a closer off-duty firearm case. Thus, if this evidence were before a fact finder assessing factor six, it would significantly weaken MPD’s assertion that termination here was consistent with discipline for similar offenses. (Memorandum from Marcus T. Bell, Labor Relations Liaison, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Outside Counsel, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (Braddock), re: Supplemental Comparator Case Identified After Close of Arbitration Record (Feb. 24, 2026) (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4; D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 3 ¶¶ 16, 19.)

### B. “Prove” did not, by itself, impose an unlawful standard

Douglas assigns the agency the ultimate burden to persuade the reviewer that the selected penalty is appropriate. For factor six, that means MPD had to support its claim that termination was consistent with penalties for the same or similar offenses; it does not mean MPD had to show exact identity between cases. Douglas rejects mathematical rigidity and directs practical, case-sensitive comparison. Accordingly, the word “prove” in the Award is not independently a legal error. MPD’s possible contrary-to-law argument remains that the arbitrator applied an unduly rigid threshold of comparability, rather than weighing the four summaries realistically. But the Award identified concrete differences, the parties stipulate that reasonable minds may disagree, and the newly disclosed case would make the arbitrator’s conclusion more defensible on the full facts. That materially weakens this argument. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed pp. 333–34, PDF pp. 21–22, p. 333 ¶ 2 beginning “In considering whether the agency’s judgment” and p. 333 ¶ 3 beginning “Lastly, it should be clear”; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 7.)

The stipulated absence of a pre-hearing disparate-treatment allegation helps MPD only at the initial-justification stage. Douglas says that where the penalty is not challenged, an agency’s prima facie showing—facts supporting the penalty, a concise non-irrational rationale, and conformity with applicable law—will normally suffice; when the employee challenges the penalty or the reviewer perceives a genuine equity issue, the agency may be called on for further evidence. Braddock’s general arbitration grievance expressly put the appropriateness of termination at issue, and the arbitrator decided factor six. The lack of an earlier, specific comparator allegation therefore does not waive review of the penalty or displace MPD’s ultimate burden in arbitration. Braddock’s stipulated knowledge of the four proposed comparators before MPD’s guilt finding does, however, support MPD’s position that it gave notice of the evidence on which its original factor-six assessment rested and had no earlier specific disparate-treatment claim to answer. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶ 2; D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 3 ¶ 19; Memorandum from Yolanda Britt, Director, Disciplinary Review Division, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Sandra M. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed p. 334, PDF p. 22, ¶ 1 beginning “In many cases.”)

## 2. Factor twelve: the principal contrary-to-law theory remains

The Award recited a non-de-novo, “tolerable limits of reasonableness” standard. It then faulted the deciding official for offering no support for the prediction that no lesser sanction would deter, and itself predicted that a lengthy suspension would likely deter Braddock and other officers. MPD’s best remaining contrary-to-law argument is that this substituted the arbitrator’s prediction for the deciding official’s reasoned management assessment. The Final Notice tied removal to this specific shooting and explained that a lesser penalty would communicate that shooting an unarmed, non-threatening person was survivable. Douglas identifies deterrence as relevant but cautions that the reviewer is not to strike the balance anew. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 5, 8–9; D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 4 ¶ 25; Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed p. 332, PDF p. 20, factor (12) and ¶ 1 beginning “Not all of these factors.”)

The argument is not assured. Douglas places the ultimate burden on MPD, and an arbitrator could view the deciding official’s unsupported deterrence assertion as insufficient to carry that burden after the penalty was contested. The Award’s “likely” statement may thus be understood as an evidentiary assessment rather than a facial rule demanding empirical studies or a categorical substitution of judgment. Kucera’s 40-day suspension makes it harder to say, as a general factual matter, that lesser discipline can never be imposed after an out-of-policy off-duty firearm discharge; but the disclosure gives no evidence about deterrence or later conduct. Because Kucera was outside the arbitration record, this disclosure does not change whether the arbitrator exceeded the scope of review on the record before him. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed pp. 333–34, PDF pp. 21–22, p. 333 ¶ 3 beginning “Lastly, it should be clear” and p. 334 ¶ 2 beginning “In many cases”; Memorandum from Marcus T. Bell, Labor Relations Liaison, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Outside Counsel, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (Braddock), re: Supplemental Comparator Case Identified After Close of Arbitration Record (Feb. 24, 2026) (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4.)

Accordingly, factor twelve remains MPD’s strongest contrary-to-law point and the comparator disclosure leaves its legal formulation intact. Factor six should now be presented, if at all, as a secondary argument confined to the four cases actually before the arbitrator; the supplemental case cannot supply proof that the arbitrator’s four-case analysis was legally erroneous. The shared difficulty remains that § 1-605.02(6) allows intervention only where the Award is facially contrary to law and PERB may not substitute its judgment for the arbitrator’s merely because it disagrees with his evidentiary assessment. (D.C. Code § 1-605.02 (2016) (dc_code_1-605.02.pdf), PDF p. 1, subsection (6); Opinion No. 1904, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd. Nov. 6, 2025) (perb_decision_braddock.pdf), PDF pp. 1–2 ¶¶ 3–6; Fraternal Ord. of Police/Dep’t of Corr. Lab. Comm. v. D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd., 973 A.2d 174 (D.C. 2009) (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf), PDF p. 3, § II, ¶¶ 1–2.)

The 6-B DCMR coverage point is also unchanged and remains secondary: § 1600.2(g) excludes sworn MPD members from the chapter, although both the Final Notice and Award invoked the Douglas framework and § 1606.2. The fifth comparator does not affect that regulatory issue. (D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, § 1600.2(g); D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 13; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 5.)

## 3. The fifth comparator is outside the review record

The arbitration hearing occurred in November 2024; the record closed on January 17, 2025. Bell’s disclosure expressly says Kucera’s matter was not before Arbitrator Larrabee or PERB. The statute authorizes review of whether the arbitration “award on its face” is contrary to law and public policy, not a fresh penalty determination on newly supplied evidence. *FOP/Department of Corrections* likewise explains the limits on review and states that the court may not rely on material in the agency record as a basis to uphold PERB when PERB did not rely on it. The Kucera disclosure is further removed: it was not in the arbitration or PERB record at all. It therefore should not be considered as substantive evidence on the petition or used to establish a facial legal defect in the Award. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶ 1; Memorandum from Marcus T. Bell, Labor Relations Liaison, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Outside Counsel, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (Braddock), re: Supplemental Comparator Case Identified After Close of Arbitration Record (Feb. 24, 2026) (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶ 4; D.C. Code § 1-605.02 (2016) (dc_code_1-605.02.pdf), PDF p. 1, subsection (6); Fraternal Ord. of Police/Dep’t of Corr. Lab. Comm. v. D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd., 973 A.2d 174 (D.C. 2009) (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf), PDF p. 2, § I, ¶ 3; PDF p. 5, footnote 2 ¶ 1.)

The record limitation cuts both ways. MPD should not offer Kucera as new proof that the arbitrator applied the wrong standard, and a reviewing court should not rely on it to decide comparability or the proper penalty. Its significance is to update counsel’s assessment of the merits and any proceeding in which evidence may properly be taken—not to alter what the Award says on its face. (D.C. Code § 1-605.02 (2016) (dc_code_1-605.02.pdf), PDF p. 1, subsection (6); Memorandum from Marcus T. Bell, Labor Relations Liaison, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Outside Counsel, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (Braddock), re: Supplemental Comparator Case Identified After Close of Arbitration Record (Feb. 24, 2026) (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶ 4.)

## 4. Public policy: no change to the legal test

The policy theory remains that reinstating a sworn officer after the Award sustained a finding that he shot an unarmed man twice without a reasonable perception of imminent deadly danger conflicts with the rule against unjustified deadly force. The supplied authorities require an explicit, well-defined, dominant policy grounded in law or legal precedent and a conflict clearly shown by the award; they reject speculation about future danger and judicial reweighing of facts. Nothing in the Kucera disclosure adds a statute, regulation, or precedent requiring removal for this misconduct, or changes the Award’s findings and remedy. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4; PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 8–9; PDF p. 3 ¶ 10; D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 12; W.R. Grace & Co. v. Local Union 759, 461 U.S. 757 (1983) (wr_grace_v_rubber_workers.pdf), printed p. 766, ¶ 1; United Paperworkers Int’l Union v. Misco, Inc., 484 U.S. 29 (1987) (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf), printed pp. 43–45, p. 43 ¶ 1, p. 44 ¶¶ 2–3.)

As a matter of rhetoric, Kucera’s negotiated non-removal outcome may make MPD’s claim that reinstatement is inherently incompatible with the policy less persuasive. But that fact does not establish that Braddock’s reinstatement violates the policy, and it cannot be used as evidence on this petition because it was outside the record. The central public-policy weakness remains: the attached materials identify no applicable law mandating termination, and the Award does not compel future unlawful force. The public-policy argument is therefore legally unchanged and remains substantially weaker than the factor-twelve contrary-to-law argument. (Memorandum from Marcus T. Bell, Labor Relations Liaison, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Outside Counsel, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (Braddock), re: Supplemental Comparator Case Identified After Close of Arbitration Record (Feb. 24, 2026) (supplemental_comparator_disclosure.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4; Opinion No. 1904, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd. Nov. 6, 2025) (perb_decision_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 5–6; United Paperworkers Int’l Union v. Misco, Inc., 484 U.S. 29 (1987) (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf), printed p. 44, ¶¶ 2–3.)

| Issue | Updated conclusion |
|---|---|
