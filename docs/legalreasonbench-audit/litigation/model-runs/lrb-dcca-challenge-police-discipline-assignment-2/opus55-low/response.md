## Bottom line

1. **Scope of review.** The arbitrator had to decide whether MPD conscientiously considered the relevant factors and struck a responsible balance "within tolerable limits of reasonableness." He could substitute his own penalty only if MPD failed to weigh the relevant factors or its judgment "clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2 to p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1; p. 17 (printed 329), ¶ 2. DCMR § 1606.2 requires MPD to be "prepared to demonstrate that the following factors were considered," not to prove each factor conclusion. DCMR § 1606, p. 1, § 1606.2.

   The arbitrator stated this standard correctly. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5. **He did not stay within it on either factor.**
   - On factor 6, he turned a deferential review into an affirmative burden on MPD to "prove" comparability. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
   - On factor 12, he replaced MPD's predictive judgment with his own finding that a suspension "would likely have sufficed." Award, p. 2, ¶ 8.
   - He then reweighed all twelve factors and chose the penalty he considered "appropriate." He never found that termination itself clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness. Award, p. 2, ¶ 9.

2. **Burden allocation and the effect of Braddock's silence.**
   - MPD bore the ultimate burden of persuasion on the penalty throughout. Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 3 to p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 1.
   - MPD's initial burden of production was only a prima facie showing. Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
   - Under Douglas, a disparate-penalty claim is the employee's contention to raise. Once the employee raises it, the agency must rebut or explain it. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65; p. 33 (printed 345), ¶¶ 2–5.

   Braddock's failure to raise disparate treatment before the hearing **does not, by itself, forfeit the issue.** Douglas expressly contemplates disparity claims first raised on appeal. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65. The failure does have two consequences:
   - MPD's unrebutted prima facie showing on factor 6 was sufficient at the agency stage.
   - At arbitration, the burden of producing evidence of a genuinely similar, more leniently treated employee remained with the grievant. The Award identifies none. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.

   **MPD's strongest position:** the arbitrator misallocated the burden and applied a non-deferential standard to a question on which reasonable minds can disagree.

   **MPD's weakest point:**
   - Douglas puts the ultimate burden of persuasion on the agency.
   - Douglas allows the factfinder to raise equity concerns on its own.
   - Douglas warns that an agency "will risk having its selected penalty reduced" if it fails to prove the facts underlying its penalty rationale. Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 3; p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2; p. 32 (printed 344), ¶ 1.
   - MPD itself labeled the comparators "similarly situated" and relied on them as aggravating. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 19.

## I. Scope of review on MPD's penalty selection, and whether the arbitrator stayed within it

### A. The governing scope of review

**1. DCMR § 1606 places the penalty decision with management and requires demonstrated consideration, not proof of each conclusion.**

- After cause is established, "managers must determine the appropriate action." DCMR § 1606, p. 1, § 1606.1.
- For all adverse actions, "managers shall be prepared to demonstrate that the following factors were considered." The regulation then lists twelve factors that track Douglas, including (f), "[c]onsistency of the penalty with those imposed upon other employees for the same or similar offenses," and (l), "[t]he adequacy and effectiveness of alternative sanctions to deter such conduct in the future by the employee or others." DCMR § 1606, p. 1, § 1606.2(f), (l).
- The factors "shall be considered and balanced to arrive at the appropriate remedy," and "not all of these factors may be deemed relevant." DCMR § 1606, p. 1, § 1606.3.
- The analysis must appear in the proposed and final actions. DCMR § 1606, p. 1, § 1606.4. The final decision must "[d]emonstrate reasoned consideration of the relevant factors set forth in § 1606.2." DCMR § 1606, p. 2, § 1623.4(d).

The regulation therefore imposes a **consideration-and-balancing** duty on management, documented by reasoned analysis. It does not require the agency to prove each factor conclusion to a factfinder.

The regulation does not itself prescribe the standard an arbitrator applies. The arbitrator took his standard from Douglas, Award, p. 2, ¶ 5, and nothing in the record suggests either party objected. MPD should hold the arbitrator to the standard he adopted.

**2. Douglas makes review of the penalty deferential.**

- The reviewing body is not "free simply to substitute its judgment for that of the employing agencies." Any margin of discretion "must be exercised with appropriate deference to the primary discretion which has been entrusted to agency management." Douglas, p. 16 (printed 328), ¶ 2.
- The reviewer "must give due weight to the agency's primary discretion." Its function "is not to displace management's responsibility but to assure that managerial judgment has been properly exercised within tolerable limits of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 17 (printed 329), ¶ 2.
- The reviewer's role "is not to insist that the balance be struck precisely where the Board would choose to strike it if the Board were in the agency's shoes in the first instance." Review is "essentially to assure that the agency did conscientiously consider the relevant factors and did strike a responsible balance within tolerable limits of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2 to p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.
- **Trigger for substituting a penalty:** "Only if the Board finds that the agency failed to weigh the relevant factors, or that the agency's judgment clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness, is it appropriate for the Board then to specify how the agency's decision should be corrected to bring the penalty within the parameters of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.
- A decision notice that "does demonstrate such reasoned consideration may be entitled to greater deference." Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), ¶ 2.
- On consistency in particular, the "like penalties for like offenses" principle "does not require mathematical rigidity or perfect consistency." It "must be applied with practical realism, eschewing insistence upon rigid formalism so long as the substance of equity in relation to genuinely similar cases is preserved," and "surface consistency should be avoided." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2; see also p. 34 (printed 346), n. 84.

**3. The object of review is the penalty as a whole.** Douglas asks whether the agency struck "a responsible balance" and whether its "judgment clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1. It asks whether "the penalty" is "clearly excessive in proportion to the sustained charges, violates the principle of like penalties for like offenses, or is otherwise unreasonable under all the relevant circumstances." Douglas, p. 17 (printed 329), ¶ 2. The factors "are not to be evaluated mechanistically by any preordained formula." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2.

**Conclusion on the standard.** The arbitrator had to ask two questions:
1. Did MPD consider and balance the § 1606.2 factors?
2. If so, did MPD's choice of termination clearly exceed tolerable limits of reasonableness?

Only if the answer to (1) was no, or the answer to (2) was yes, could he substitute a penalty. Even then, his role was to bring the penalty "within the parameters of reasonableness," not to select the penalty he thought best. Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.

### B. The threshold problem: the arbitrator restated the standard as a factor-by-factor kill rule

The arbitrator correctly said his review was "not de novo." He correctly asked whether MPD's conclusions "fall within tolerable limits of reasonableness." Award, p. 2, ¶ 5.

He then added a rule Douglas does not contain: "If a conclusion on a material factor falls outside those limits, the penalty determination cannot stand as issued." Award, p. 2, ¶ 5. He applied that rule to vacate termination because two of twelve factor conclusions failed. Award, p. 2, ¶ 9.

That departs from Douglas in two ways:
- Douglas evaluates the overall balance, not each factor in isolation. Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2 to p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2.
- Douglas allows substitution only on a finding that the agency failed to weigh the relevant factors or that its judgment "clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.

The arbitrator made neither finding. He found the opposite on the first point: MPD "weighed the factors," and its conclusions on ten factors were reasonable and "supported by the record." Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 5–6. Those ten factors are:

| Factor | Subject | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Grave offense | Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 14 |
| 2 | Sworn officer | Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 15 |
| 3 | Prior record | Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 16 |
| 5 | Lost confidence | Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 18 |
| 7 | Within the table range | Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 20 |
| 8 | Notoriety | Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 21 |
| 9 | Clear notice | Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 22 |
| 10 | Poor rehabilitation potential | Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 23 |
| 11 | No mitigation | Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 24 |

He never asked whether termination, given those ten sustained findings, was itself outside the limits of reasonableness. Instead he conducted his own balancing: "Weighing the sustained findings on the remaining ten factors against the failures on factors six and twelve, I conclude that a 45-day suspension without pay is the appropriate penalty." Award, p. 2, ¶ 9.

That is the balancing Douglas assigns to management, carried out "in the agency's shoes in the first instance." Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2. It is also the conclusion that "the appropriate remedy" is something other than what management chose, a determination DCMR § 1606.3 assigns to managers. DCMR § 1606, p. 1, §§ 1606.1, 1606.3.

### C. Factor 6: requiring MPD to "prove" comparability exceeded the scope of review

**What MPD found.**
- The Disciplinary Review Division identified four 2018–2021 cases in which sworn members who used unjustified force involving a firearm or serious bodily injury were terminated. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 19; Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 1–5.
- MPD found termination "consistent with the discipline imposed on those similarly situated members." Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 19.

**What the arbitrator held.** MPD "did not prove that the comparator officers were similarly situated" and "failed to carry its burden to prove comparability." He concluded that MPD's factor-6 conclusion therefore "falls outside tolerable limits of reasonableness." Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.

**Conclusion: this exceeded the permissible scope, for five independent reasons.**

1. **He applied a proof standard, not a reasonableness standard.**
   - The question under Douglas and § 1606.2(f) was whether MPD's consistency judgment was within tolerable limits of reasonableness. Douglas, p. 17 (printed 329), ¶ 2; p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.
   - The arbitrator asked instead whether MPD had "prove[d]" comparability to his satisfaction. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
   - We accept as true that reasonable minds can disagree about how comparable the four cases are (Assumption (a)). That concession is decisive under Douglas. A judgment on which reasonable minds can differ is, by definition, within tolerable limits of reasonableness. Treating it as unreasonable means "insist[ing] that the balance be struck precisely where the [reviewer] would choose to strike it." Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2.

2. **He demanded the "mathematical rigidity" Douglas rejects.**
   - The arbitrator disqualified comparators on single distinctions: on-duty status, a fatality, two prior force complaints. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
   - Douglas requires "practical realism" and "the substance of equity in relation to genuinely similar cases," not "rigid formalism" or "perfect consistency." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2.
   - The comparators share the core features of Braddock's offense:
     - unjustified force by a sworn member;
     - a firearm or serious bodily injury;
     - termination in each case.

     Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 1–5.
   - Several distinctions cut against Braddock, not for him:
     - **Comparator B (Sadler)** had no prior discipline and used no firearm discharge, and was still terminated. Braddock had a prior sustained reprimand and fired two rounds into an unarmed man's chest and shoulder. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 3; Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 9; p. 3, ¶ 16.
     - **Comparator C (Pietrzak)** was an off-duty firearm discharge, like Braddock's. The arbitrator distinguished it on the fatality. But Braddock's victim survived only "after three surgeries and approximately eight months of treatment." Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 4; Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 10.

3. **His factual premise misreads the record.**
   - The Award states that "[t]wo of the four comparators involved on-duty conduct." Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
   - The Comparator Memo shows three on-duty comparators: A, B, and D. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 2, 3, 5. Only C was off duty. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 4.
   - Ironically, the correct count somewhat strengthens the arbitrator's on-duty distinction. But it confirms that he never engaged with Comparators B and D individually. Neither involved a fatality, and Comparator D's officer had only one unrelated prior complaint, which closely parallels Braddock. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 5.

4. **He used factor 3 inconsistently.**
   - To distinguish the comparators, he described Braddock's prior reprimand as "a single unrelated reprimand." Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
   - Yet he upheld as reasonable MPD's treatment of that same reprimand as aggravating. Award, p. 2, ¶ 6; Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 16.

5. **Even if he were right, the result is a neutral factor, not an unreasonable penalty.**
   - The most his analysis shows is that MPD's comparators are imperfect. That would make factor 6 neutral or of limited weight. It would not show that termination is inconsistent with penalties imposed on "genuinely similar cases." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2.
   - The Award identifies no similarly situated member who received a lighter penalty. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
   - The consistency principle is about avoiding disparity. Douglas, p. 17 (printed 329), ¶ 2 ("violates the principle of like penalties for like offenses"); p. 33 (printed 345), ¶¶ 2–5. A lack of perfect matches is not evidence of disparity.
   - Not every factor must be relevant. DCMR § 1606, p. 1, § 1606.3; Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2.

### D. Factor 12: the finding that a suspension would "likely" have sufficed exceeded the scope of review

**What MPD found.** The Deciding Official considered "lesser penalties, including a suspension of significant length." She concluded that no alternative sanction would adequately deter Braddock or other members, because anything short of removal "would signal to the workforce that such conduct is survivable." Final Notice, p. 4, ¶ 25.

**What the arbitrator held.**
- MPD "offered no evidence supporting that conclusion beyond the deciding official's assertion."
- A lengthy suspension is a severe sanction.
- "On this record, a suspension would likely have sufficed to deter future misconduct by the Grievant and by other members of the force."

Award, p. 2, ¶ 8.

**Conclusion: this exceeded the permissible scope, for four reasons.**

1. **"Likely would have sufficed" is a competing prediction, not a finding of unreasonableness.**
   - Factor 12 calls for a forward-looking managerial judgment about "the adequacy and effectiveness of alternative sanctions to deter such conduct in the future." DCMR § 1606, p. 1, § 1606.2(l); Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 1.
   - The arbitrator's finding that a different sanction "would likely have sufficed" states only his own probabilistic view. It does not find that MPD's contrary judgment "clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.
   - Choosing between two reasonable predictions is exactly the "primary discretion" Douglas reserves to management. Douglas, p. 16 (printed 328), ¶ 2; p. 17 (printed 329), ¶ 2.

2. **Douglas does not require separate empirical evidence of deterrent effect.**
   - An agency's prima facie case consists of "evidence of the facts on which selection of the penalty was based" and "a concise statement of its reasoning from those facts," sufficient to show that the reasoning "is not on its face inherently irrational." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
   - The facts underlying factor 12 were established and undisputed: Braddock shot an unarmed man twice over a suspected property offense. Award, p. 1, ¶¶ 3–4.
   - The reasoning was stated: lesser discipline would signal the conduct is "survivable." Final Notice, p. 4, ¶ 25. That reasoning is not inherently irrational.
   - Douglas itself sustained removals based in part on the agency's deterrence reasoning. It accepted that without removal "the agency's ability to deter such conduct by other employees could be lessened." Douglas, p. 34 (printed 346), ¶ 1; see p. 33 (printed 345), ¶ 1.
   - Where an adverse effect is "self-evident," an agency "need not expect to present detailed evidence" absent rebuttal. Douglas, p. 29 (printed 341), ¶ 1.
   - MPD also made the "responsible determination that a less severe penalty . . . is inadequate" that Douglas describes: it expressly considered and rejected a significant suspension. Douglas, p. 18 (printed 330), ¶ 3; Final Notice, p. 4, ¶ 25.

3. **The finding contradicts factors the arbitrator himself sustained.**
   - He upheld as reasonable MPD's findings that Braddock's rehabilitation potential is "poor" because he "accepts no responsibility" (factor 10), and that the chain of command's confidence is "lost" (factor 5). Award, p. 2, ¶ 6; Final Notice, p. 3, ¶¶ 18, 23.
   - Specific deterrence of the employee turns on the same considerations. Douglas treats rehabilitation potential and prior failure to correct conduct as bearing directly on whether a lesser sanction will work. Douglas, p. 25 (printed 337), ¶ 3; p. 26 (printed 338), ¶¶ 4–5.
   - A finding that a suspension would likely deter "the Grievant" cannot be reconciled with a sustained finding that he accepts no responsibility. Award, p. 2, ¶ 8.

4. **The arbitrator's own reasoning is also only assertion.** He faulted MPD for relying on "the deciding official's assertion." Yet his contrary view rests on his own description of what a suspension means "for a sworn officer," and cites no evidence. Award, p. 2, ¶ 8. Swapping one unsupported prediction for another is substitution of judgment. Douglas, p. 16 (printed 328), ¶ 2.

### E. Remedy

Suppose a factor conclusion had been unsupported. Douglas still authorizes the reviewer only to "specify how the agency's decision should be corrected to bring the penalty within the parameters of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.

Where Douglas found the agency's record on the penalty underdeveloped, it remanded so the parties could introduce additional evidence. Douglas, p. 32 (printed 344), ¶ 1. It did not choose the penalty it preferred.

The arbitrator neither found termination outside the parameters of reasonableness nor remanded. He selected "the appropriate penalty." Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 9–10.

**Conclusion to Question 1:** The arbitrator stated the correct deferential standard but did not apply it. His treatment of factors 6 and 12, and his resulting reweighing, exceeded the scope of review that Douglas and DCMR § 1606.2 permit.

## II. Burden of production and persuasion on the comparator evidence; consequence of Braddock's silence

### A. Burden allocation at each stage

**Stage 1: proposed action, response, and Final Notice (March 8 to May 22, 2024).**

- **Production.** MPD bore the duty to consider factor 6 and to document that consideration. DCMR § 1606, p. 1, §§ 1606.2(f), 1606.4; p. 2, § 1623.4(d). Douglas adds that aggravating factors the agency intends to rely on "should be included in the advance notice of charges," so the employee has "a fair opportunity to respond." Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), ¶ 2. MPD met this burden:
  - the Division compiled the comparators (Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 1–5);
  - the Final Notice relied on them and stated they accompanied the Notice (Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 19);
  - Braddock knew MPD would rely on them before the guilty finding (Assumption (c)).
- **Persuasion.** At this stage the deciding official is the decision-maker, so there is no adversarial burden of persuasion. MPD's obligation was to "demonstrate that the . . . factors were considered" and to show "reasoned consideration." DCMR § 1606, p. 1, § 1606.2; p. 2, § 1623.4(d).
- **Braddock's role.** He responded in writing through counsel and appeared at a hearing. Final Notice, p. 1, ¶ 2. He did not allege disparate treatment (Assumption (b)). The comparators therefore stood unrebutted.

**Stage 2: arbitration (hearing November 13–14, 2024; Award March 14, 2025).**

- **Ultimate persuasion: MPD.** "[T]he ultimate burden is upon the agency to persuade . . . of the appropriateness of the penalty imposed." That deference "cannot have the effect of shifting to the appellant the burden of proving that the penalty is unlawful." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 3 to p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 1. The stipulated issue put the appropriateness of termination before the arbitrator. Award, p. 1, ¶ 2.
- **Initial production: MPD, limited to a prima facie case.** MPD had to present the facts on which it selected the penalty, a concise statement of reasoning that is not inherently irrational, and conformity with law and regulation. "When no issue has been raised concerning the penalty, such a prima facie case will normally suffice to meet also the agency's burden of persuasion." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2. The Final Notice and the Comparator Memo meet this showing on factor 6. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 19; Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 1–5.
- **Production to put disparity in issue: the grievant.** Douglas treats disparate penalty as the employee's contention: "if an appellant contends that a penalty exceeds that imposed upon other employees in like circumstances, the agency would remain free to present evidence to rebut such contention." Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65. In Nocifore, the appellants "claimed that they were treated disparately" and cited six cases. The Board asked whether "the record does not support appellants' contention," and held that "the seeming disparity has been adequately explained by the agency." Douglas, p. 33 (printed 345), ¶¶ 2–5. The sequence is:
  1. The employee identifies more leniently treated, similarly situated employees.
  2. The agency must then rebut or explain the disparity.
- **What shifts back to MPD once severity is challenged.** When the grievant challenges severity, or the factfinder "perceives genuine issues of justice or equity," the agency "will be called upon to present such further evidence as it may choose to rebut the appellant's challenge or to satisfy the presiding official." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2. This is a burden to rebut a challenge actually made. It is not a free-standing burden to "prove comparability" of the agency's own supporting comparators.

**Stage 3: PERB and the DCCA.** None of the uploaded materials addresses the burden or standard of review before PERB or the DCCA. I cannot state it from this record. See Part III.

### B. The arbitrator's allocation was wrong

The arbitrator assigned MPD a "burden to prove comparability." Award, p. 2, ¶ 7. He made no finding that the grievant identified any similarly situated member who received a lesser penalty. Nothing in the Award suggests MPD was asked to rebut such a showing. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.

His allocation therefore:
1. converted MPD's prima facie showing into an affirmative burden to prove the comparators' similarity to his satisfaction; and
2. relieved the grievant of the production burden Douglas places on the party asserting disparity.

Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65; p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2; p. 33 (printed 345), ¶¶ 2–5.

### C. Legal consequence of Braddock's failure to raise disparate treatment before the hearing

**Conclusion: the failure is not a forfeiture bar. It does mean MPD's factor-6 showing was unrebutted, and the production burden on any disparity claim stayed with the grievant.**

1. **No waiver as such.**
   - Douglas expressly contemplates disparity claims raised for the first time before the reviewing tribunal. It provides that the agency may rebut "notwithstanding its failure to have recited such information in its advance or final notices." Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65.
   - In Douglas, the severity challenges were raised "in their appeals before this Board." Douglas, p. 2 (printed 314), ¶ 1.
   - Nothing in DCMR § 1606 or §§ 1622–1623 makes raising an issue in the employee's response a precondition to raising it later. DCMR § 1606, p. 1, §§ 1606.1–1606.4; p. 2, §§ 1622.3–1622.4, 1623.2.
   - MPD should **not** argue that Braddock forfeited factor 6 outright. That argument is contrary to Douglas and would undermine our credibility.

2. **Consequences MPD can press.**
   - **(a) The agency-stage record was complete.** Braddock knew of the comparators before the guilty finding (Assumption (c)), had counsel and a hearing (Final Notice, p. 1, ¶ 2), and said nothing. The "fair opportunity to respond" Douglas requires was given. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), ¶ 2. With the comparators unchallenged, MPD's prima facie showing sufficed, and the Deciding Official had no reason to develop the record further. Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
   - **(b) The production burden stayed with the grievant at arbitration.** Because no disparity claim was made before the hearing, MPD was not on notice of any specific claim it would need to rebut. Douglas places the burden of identifying more lenient treatment on the party asserting it. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65; p. 33 (printed 345), ¶¶ 2–5. The Award identifies no such employee. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
   - **(c) Lack of notice reinforces the error.** If the arbitrator faulted MPD for not proving comparability on a theory first developed at or after the hearing, that compounds the misallocation. MPD was entitled to present "such further evidence as it may choose to rebut." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2. The Comparator Memo shows that full case files were "available . . . on request." Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 6.

### D. MPD's strongest position

**The arbitrator misallocated the burden on factor 6 and applied a non-deferential standard to a question on which reasonable minds can disagree.** The argument runs as follows:

1. Under DCMR § 1606.2, MPD's duty was to consider and demonstrate consideration of consistency. It did. DCMR § 1606, p. 1, § 1606.2(f); Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 19. The arbitrator agreed that MPD "weighed the factors." Award, p. 2, ¶ 5.
2. MPD made a prima facie showing. Braddock had notice and never contested it before the hearing (Assumptions (b), (c)). Douglas puts the production burden on a disparity claim on the employee. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65; p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2; p. 33 (printed 345), ¶¶ 2–5.
3. Reasonable minds can disagree on comparability (Assumption (a)). MPD's view is therefore within "tolerable limits of reasonableness," and the arbitrator's contrary view is substitution of judgment. Douglas, p. 17 (printed 329), ¶ 2; p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2 to p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.
4. No similarly situated employee received a lesser penalty. The arbitrator also misstated the comparator record (two versus three on-duty cases). Award, p. 2, ¶ 7; Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 2–5.
5. Combined with the factor-12 substitution and the arbitrator's own reweighing, the Award replaces management's penalty decision with the arbitrator's. It does so with no finding that termination clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness, despite ten sustained aggravating factors. Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 6, 9; Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.

This position is strongest because it rests on the arbitrator's own stated standard, Award, p. 2, ¶ 5, and on his own sustained findings, Award, p. 2, ¶ 6. It does not require the reviewing body to reweigh any evidence.

### E. The weakest point in that position

**Douglas puts the ultimate burden of persuasion on the penalty on the agency. MPD itself made comparability a factual predicate of an aggravating finding.**

1. **Douglas cuts against an employee-burden framing.** Deference "cannot have the effect of shifting to the appellant the burden of proving that the penalty is unlawful." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 1. The stipulated issue squarely placed the penalty's appropriateness in dispute. Award, p. 1, ¶ 2. A prima facie case suffices only "[w]hen no issue has been raised concerning the penalty." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
2. **The arbitrator could raise the issue himself.** Douglas allows the factfinder to require further agency evidence whenever it "perceives genuine issues of justice or equity." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2. So even without any disparity claim from the grievant, the arbitrator could legitimately probe the comparators.
3. **Douglas expressly warns agencies about unproven predicates.** An agency "will risk having its selected penalty reduced, if it does not meet its burden of proof on the underlying factual issues." Douglas, p. 32 (printed 344), ¶ 1. MPD affirmatively characterized the comparators as "similarly situated" and counted factor 6 as aggravating. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 19. The opposing side will call similarity a factual issue underlying MPD's rationale, not an employee defense.
4. **Our evidentiary support was thin.** MPD appears to have relied on one-paragraph summaries. The Division "expresses no view on the appropriate penalty," and the case files were only "available . . . on request." Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶ 6; Award, p. 2, ¶ 7 ("Having reviewed the comparator summaries").
5. **Assumption (a) cuts both ways.** It also means the arbitrator's view is a reasonable one.

**Response.**

- The Douglas burden of persuasion concerns "the appropriateness of the penalty," not each subsidiary factor. Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 3.
- If factor 6 is reduced to neutral, the remaining ten sustained aggravating factors still have to be weighed. Award, p. 2, ¶ 6. The arbitrator was required to ask whether termination remained within the bounds of reasonableness, not to start the balancing over. Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.
- Even under the Jimenez warning, the remedy Douglas actually applied was a remand to allow additional evidence, not substitution of a penalty. Douglas, p. 32 (printed 344), ¶ 1.

We should lead with the scope-of-review error and present burden misallocation as reinforcing it. We should not make burden misallocation our lead argument.

**Conclusion to Question 2:** On a factor-6 disparity claim, the burden of production rested on the grievant to identify more leniently treated, genuinely similar employees. The burden of persuasion on the overall penalty rested on MPD. MPD's unrebutted prima facie showing satisfied its production burden. Braddock's silence before the hearing does not forfeit the issue, but it left MPD's showing unrebutted. The arbitrator erred by requiring MPD to "prove comparability" without any disparity showing by the grievant.
