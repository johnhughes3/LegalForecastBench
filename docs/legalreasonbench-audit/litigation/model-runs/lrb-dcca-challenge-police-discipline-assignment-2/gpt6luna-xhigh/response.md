# Draft memorandum section: Scope of review and preservation

**Matter:** MPD v. PERB — Braddock penalty award  
**Date:** March 2, 2026

## 1. Scope of review of the penalty

**Threshold point about § 1606.2.** The attached 2017 regulation states that Chapter 16 does not apply to sworn MPD members. Accordingly, the attachment alone does not establish that § 1606.2 directly governed Braddock. The deciding official and the arbitrator nevertheless expressly treated Douglas and § 1606.2 as the applicable penalty framework. This section analyzes the award under that adopted framework, while preserving the threshold textual point. (D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, § 1600.2(g); D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), p. 2 ¶ 13; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 2 ¶ 5.)

Under Douglas, review of an agency’s penalty is not de novo. The reviewer ensures that the agency conscientiously considered the relevant factors and struck a responsible balance within tolerable limits of reasonableness; the reviewer may correct the penalty only if the agency failed to weigh relevant factors or its judgment clearly exceeded those limits. The reviewer may not impose the balance it would have chosen in the first instance. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), pp. 332–33, ¶ 1 (beginning “Not all of these factors”); p. 333, ¶ 2 (beginning “In considering whether the agency’s judgment”).) The DCMR likewise requires consideration of the enumerated factors and their balancing, but cautions against treating them as a formula: all factors are to be considered and balanced, while relevance depends on the circumstances. (D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, §§ 1606.2–.3; see also § 1607.2.) The arbitrator stated the same tolerable-limits standard and expressly disclaimed de novo review. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 2 ¶ 5.)

### Factor six — comparator discipline

Factor six concerns consistency with penalties imposed for the same or similar offenses; it does not require mathematical consistency or identical facts. Douglas directs practical comparison, avoiding rigid formalism while preserving equity in genuinely similar cases and accounting for the individual circumstances. The regulation similarly requires consideration of consistency for “same or similar” offenses as one part of a balanced assessment. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), p. 332, item (6) and ¶ 1 (beginning “Not all of these factors”); p. 333, ¶ 2 (beginning “In considering whether the agency’s judgment”); D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, § 1606.2(f), .3.)

MPD’s stated comparison was not facially irrational. Its four examples all involved sworn members disciplined for unjustified force involving a firearm or serious bodily injury, and each officer was terminated. The underlying summaries disclose material differences: two cases involved on-duty conduct; one involved a fatality; one officer had two prior force complaints; and Braddock’s conduct was off duty, caused no fatality, and followed an unrelated reprimand. (Memorandum from Yolanda Britt, Director, Disciplinary Review Division, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Sandra M. Delgado, Assistant Chief, Professional Development Bureau, re: Comparator Discipline Cases, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841 (Braddock) (Apr. 11, 2024) (comparator_discipline_cases.pdf), p. 1 ¶¶ 1–5; D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), p. 3 ¶ 19.) The stipulated fact that reasonable minds can disagree about how comparable these cases are is decisive under deferential review: those differences could support giving the comparators less weight, but they do not establish that MPD’s broader comparison was outside the range of reasonable judgment.

The arbitrator therefore exceeded the stated scope insofar as he treated the factual differences as requiring MPD to “prove” that the comparators were similarly situated and, on that basis, held factor six outside tolerable limits. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 2 ¶ 7.) The word “prove,” standing alone, is not the error: Douglas places the ultimate burden of persuading the reviewing tribunal on the agency. The error is treating comparator equivalence as a rigid prerequisite and replacing MPD’s reasonable judgment with the arbitrator’s own view of the factual differences. Douglas requires a practical, nonmechanistic comparison, and the agreed fact that reasonable minds may differ prevents the arbitrator from setting aside MPD’s factor-six judgment merely because another reasonable comparison is possible. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), pp. 333–34, ¶ 3 (beginning “Lastly, it should be clear” and continuing on p. 334), ¶ 2 on p. 334 (beginning “In many cases”).)

### Factor twelve — deterrence

Factor twelve asks whether alternative sanctions would adequately and effectively deter future misconduct. MPD did address that factor: the deciding official considered a significant suspension, concluded it would not deter Braddock or other members, and explained that a sanction short of removal for shooting an unarmed, non-threatening person would signal that such conduct was survivable. (D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), p. 4 ¶ 25.) The arbitrator instead found that a lengthy suspension would “likely” suffice, reasoning that it would impose lost pay, lost standing, and a permanent disciplinary record; he found no supporting evidence for MPD’s contrary conclusion beyond the deciding official’s assertion. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 2 ¶ 8.)

On balance, the arbitrator exceeded the non-de-novo scope on factor twelve as well. His decisive statement that a suspension likely would deter is his own competing prediction, not a finding that MPD failed to consider the factor; and the deciding official gave a reasoned, if concise, explanation tied to the seriousness of the conduct and the message to the workforce. Douglas does not make empirical deterrence evidence a prerequisite to agency judgment. It directs a reviewer not to rebalance a factor simply because the reviewer finds a lesser sanction adequate. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), pp. 332–33, ¶ 1; D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, §§ 1606.2(l), .3.) The arbitrator’s conclusion is nevertheless the weaker of MPD’s two merits challenges: the award specifically found no evidence supporting the deciding official’s deterrence prediction beyond the assertion itself, and Douglas keeps the ultimate burden on MPD. That record gap gives Braddock a substantial response that the agency’s stated rationale did not carry its burden once deterrence was disputed. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 2 ¶ 8; Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), pp. 333–34, ¶ 3 on p. 333 and ¶ 1 on p. 334.)

**Conclusion on scope.** MPD has a substantial argument that the arbitrator applied the right verbal standard but crossed its boundary on both factors: he displaced a reasonable factor-six assessment despite acknowledged room for disagreement, and substituted his own factor-twelve prediction for MPD’s stated deterrence rationale. The factor-twelve point is less secure because the award identifies a lack of supporting evidence, while Douglas leaves the ultimate burden of persuasion on MPD. The arbitrator accepted the other ten factors and reduced termination after balancing them against factors six and twelve. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 2 ¶¶ 6, 9.)

## 2. Burdens and preservation of the comparator issue

### Allocation of burdens by stage

**MPD’s initial penalty decision.** Under the framework MPD and the arbitrator used, MPD had to consider the relevant factors and articulate a reasoned basis for its penalty; the employee did not have an affirmative burden to disprove the comparator cases. Section 1606.2 says managers must be prepared to demonstrate factor consideration, but neither it nor Douglas makes “proof” of exact comparator identity a separate burden. (D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, §§ 1606.2(f), .3–.4; Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), pp. 333–34, ¶ 3 on p. 333 and ¶ 1 on p. 334.) The deciding official identified the four comparators and stated why she viewed them as similarly situated; the Britt memorandum supplied the case summaries. (D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), p. 3 ¶ 19; Memorandum from Yolanda Britt, Director, Disciplinary Review Division, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Sandra M. Delgado, Assistant Chief, Professional Development Bureau, re: Comparator Discipline Cases, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841 (Braddock) (Apr. 11, 2024) (comparator_discipline_cases.pdf), p. 1 ¶¶ 1–6.)

**Arbitration.** MPD retained the ultimate burden of persuading the arbitrator that its penalty was appropriate; Douglas expressly rejects shifting to the employee the burden to prove the penalty unlawful. Initially, however, an unchallenged penalty ordinarily requires only a prima facie showing: evidence of the facts underlying the choice, a concise explanation that is not inherently irrational, and conformity with applicable law. If the employee challenges the penalty or the arbitrator perceives a genuine justice-or-equity issue, MPD may be called upon to produce additional evidence to rebut the challenge or satisfy the arbitrator. Thus MPD bore ultimate persuasion throughout, while its further burden of production depended on a challenge or an identified concern; Braddock did not bear the burden of proving unlawful disparate treatment. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), pp. 333–34, ¶ 3 on p. 333 and ¶¶ 1–2 on p. 334.) The award’s statement that MPD failed to “prove” comparability can be defended as applying the agency’s ultimate persuasion burden, but not if it imposed a rigid comparator-identity test or demanded additional evidence before the Douglas trigger arose.

**PERB and DCCA.** The supplied materials contain no PERB decision or separate DCCA review rule. They establish only the award and, as a matter supplied in the assignment, that PERB affirmed it. No distinct production or persuasion burden at PERB or DCCA can responsibly be attributed from this closed record. The Douglas allocation above is the only supplied tribunal-stage allocation. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 1 ¶¶ 1–2; Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), pp. 333–34, ¶ 3 on p. 333 and ¶¶ 1–2 on p. 334.)

### Consequence of not raising disparate treatment before the arbitration hearing

Braddock’s failure to raise the comparator disparity before the arbitration hearing is not an automatic waiver on this record. Section 1621.6 would waive a known defense or mitigating matter not raised in the employee’s written response if that provision applied, but the chapter expressly excludes sworn MPD members. That applicability exclusion is dispositive here. Separately, the DCMR provides that a negotiated grievance system takes precedence over, and supersedes, Chapter 16’s grievance procedures; the award confirms this grievance proceeded under the parties’ collective bargaining agreement. Those negotiated-grievance provisions reinforce that the Chapter 16 grievance process does not supply a bar to this arbitration. (D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, § 1600.2(g); PDF p. 2, §§ 1621.6, 1625.2, 1634.1; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 1 ¶ 1.)

The timing does matter under Douglas. Because no disparate-treatment contention was made before the arbitration hearing—and Braddock knew MPD intended to rely on the four cases before MPD made its final determination—MPD’s initial factor-six presentation was not then subject to a comparator-specific challenge. The final notice and attached memorandum furnished a prima facie comparator rationale; the memorandum also said the underlying files were available on request. (D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), p. 1 ¶ 2, p. 3 ¶ 19; Memorandum from Yolanda Britt, Director, Disciplinary Review Division, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Sandra M. Delgado, Assistant Chief, Professional Development Bureau, re: Comparator Discipline Cases, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841 (Braddock) (Apr. 11, 2024) (comparator_discipline_cases.pdf), p. 1 ¶ 6; Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), p. 334, ¶ 2.) That supports MPD’s strongest argument: the prima facie showing should have sufficed absent an earlier challenge, and MPD should not be treated as having failed to rebut an issue it had not been asked to address.

But the delay does not eliminate MPD’s burden once the issue is raised, nor does it prevent the arbitrator from identifying a genuine equity concern on the record. Douglas expressly allows further production when a challenge is made or the presiding official perceives such a concern. The award’s comparator ruling shows that the issue was considered in arbitration; the materials do not establish precisely when it was raised, whether MPD objected to its timing, or whether MPD was asked to submit the underlying case files. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 2 ¶ 7; Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), p. 334, ¶ 2.) This is the weakest point in MPD’s preservation position: the comparator memorandum contains summaries rather than the underlying files, and it expressly says those files could be requested. The award may therefore be defended as resolving a genuinely disputed issue after it arose, rather than imposing a pre-hearing waiver. (Memorandum from Yolanda Britt, Director, Disciplinary Review Division, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, to Sandra M. Delgado, Assistant Chief, Professional Development Bureau, re: Comparator Discipline Cases, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841 (Braddock) (Apr. 11, 2024) (comparator_discipline_cases.pdf), p. 1 ¶ 6; Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), pp. 333–34, ¶ 3 on p. 333 and ¶ 2 on p. 334.)

**Conclusion on burdens and preservation.** MPD’s best position is not that Braddock irrevocably waived the comparator issue. It is that MPD met the prima facie burden at the agency stage, that the late-raised challenge did not justify demanding exact comparator equivalence, and that—given the stipulated range of reasonable views—the arbitrator could not treat MPD’s factor-six conclusion as outside tolerable limits. MPD’s principal vulnerability is that the agency always retained the ultimate burden, and the supplied record does not show whether MPD sought to supplement the comparator summaries after the issue arose. The separate DCMR waiver provision does not cure that evidentiary weakness because the regulation’s applicability clause excludes sworn MPD members. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), pp. 333–34, ¶ 3 on p. 333 and ¶¶ 1–2 on p. 334; D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, § 1600.2(g); In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep't Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep't, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), p. 2 ¶ 7.)
