## Bottom line

1. **Viability is low but not zero.**
   - The DCCA reviews PERB, not the arbitrator. It defers to PERB's factual findings if supported by substantial evidence. It defers to PERB's reading of the CMPA unless that reading is "plainly erroneous" or "rationally indefensible." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1; p. 7, n. 6.
   - PERB, in turn, could disturb the Award only for a "clear violation of law — one evident on the face" of the Award. FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 2.
   - MPD is therefore asking the court to hold that PERB was rationally indefensible in declining to find a facial legal violation. That is a harder posture than the one in FOP/DOC, where the court deferred to PERB's decision *to* intervene.

2. **"Contrary to law" is MPD's better ground.** The strongest version follows FOP/DOC. The arbitrator expressly adopted Douglas and 6-B DCMR § 1606.2 as the governing law. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5. He then disregarded Douglas's express limit on when a reviewer may substitute a penalty. He substituted his own penalty without finding that MPD failed to weigh the factors or that termination "clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1; Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 5–6, 9.
   - FOP/DOC upheld exactly this kind of ruling: an award is contrary to law where the arbitrator "looks to an external law for guidance and purports to apply that law, but overlooks or ignores the law's express provisions." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 4.
   - MPD can pair this with a request to remand for PERB's unexplained, one-paragraph rejection of its arguments. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶¶ 4–6.
   - **Single weakest point:** Douglas's controlling rule is a *reasonableness* standard. The arbitrator recited it correctly. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5. So the claimed error looks like misapplication, not the ignoring of an unambiguous, self-executing command like the Back Pay Act's offset clause in FOP/DOC. PERB has already labeled it "[m]ere disagreement with the Arbitrator's interpretation of Douglas." PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4.

3. **"Contrary to public policy" is weak.**
   - The test, as stated in W.R. Grace, adopted in Misco, and quoted by the DCCA, has three parts:
     1. an "explicit," "well defined and dominant" public policy;
     2. "ascertained by reference to the laws and legal precedents and not from general considerations of supposed public interests";
     3. a violation that is "clearly shown" on the face of the award.

     W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2; Misco, 484 U.S. at 43, ¶ 1; FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2.
   - The policy MPD should invoke is the prohibition on a police officer's use of deadly force without an objectively reasonable belief of an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury. The record documents it in MPD General Order 120.21, Attachment A, Part A-12, as quoted in Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 12. The arbitrator applied that same standard in sustaining the charge. Award, p. 1, ¶ 4.
   - **Single weakest point:** no uploaded *law or legal precedent* establishes that policy. Its only documentation is an internal MPD General Order, and that order is described in the Final Notice but is not itself in the record. Misco forbids grounding the exception in "general considerations of supposed public interests," however strong the common sense behind them. Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 1.

## Preliminary: the DCCA's standard of review, as reflected in the uploaded opinions

**1. The statute.** PERB may modify, set aside, or remand an arbitration award "only if":

- the arbitrator was without, or exceeded, his jurisdiction;
- "the award on its face is contrary to law and public policy"; or
- the award was procured by fraud, collusion, or similar unlawful means.

This is the "exclusive method" for reviewing such awards. § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6). PERB recited the same standard. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 3.

**2. Two layers of deference.**

- **Layer 1: DCCA review of PERB.**
  - The court reviews PERB's decision "as if the [appeal] had been heard initially in this court." FOP/DOC, p. 2, ¶ 5.
  - "This court will not easily disturb a decision of the PERB." It defers to PERB's factual findings if "supported by 'substantial evidence.'" It defers to PERB's interpretation of the CMPA unless that interpretation is "unreasonable in light of the prevailing law or inconsistent with the statute" or "plainly erroneous." Unless "rationally indefensible," a PERB decision "must stand." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1.
  - The court sustains a reasonable PERB interpretation "even if a petitioner advances another reasonable interpretation." FOP/DOC, p. 7, n. 6.
- **Layer 2: PERB review of the arbitrator.**
  - PERB's authority is "limited." "Absent a clear violation of law — one evident on the face" of the award, PERB lacks "authority to substitute its judgment for the arbitrator's." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 2.
  - Where an arbitrator is "called upon actually to interpret the law, his interpretation merits deference," because it is what the parties "bargained for." FOP/DOC, p. 6, n. 3.
  - PERB applied these principles here. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4 ("The parties bargained for the Arbitrator's interpretation.").

**3. What counts as "contrary to law."**

- "Contrary to law" "may include an award that was premised on 'a misinterpretation of law by the arbitrator that was apparent "on its face."'" FOP/DOC, p. 4, ¶ 2 (quoting *MPD*, 901 A.2d at 787–88).
- One example the DCCA approved: the arbitrator "looks to an external law for guidance and purports to apply that law, but overlooks or ignores the law's express provisions." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 4; p. 4, ¶¶ 2–4; p. 5, ¶ 1.
- A violation is facial where "[n]o interpretation and no 'comprehensive analysis'" is needed to see it. FOP/DOC, p. 4, ¶ 4.

**4. Two procedural limits on the court.**

- **Preservation.** "[N]o . . . objection to an order of the Board shall be considered . . . unless such . . . objection was first urged before the Board." FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2 (quoting D.C. Code § 1-617.13(b)); p. 7, n. 8.
- **No affirmance on new grounds.** The court "may not 'affirm on a ground not relied on by the agency.'" FOP/DOC, p. 5–6, n. 2 (quoting *Teamsters Local 1714*).

**5. The posture problem.** In FOP/DOC, deference ran in favor of a PERB decision that *set aside* part of an award. FOP/DOC, p. 1, ¶ 1; p. 5, ¶ 3. Here, the same deference runs in favor of PERB's decision *refusing* to set aside the Award. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶¶ 4–7.

MPD therefore must show more than that PERB *could* reasonably have found the Award contrary to law. It must show that PERB's refusal was rationally indefensible, or that PERB failed to give a reasoned basis that the court can review.

**6. Uploaded materials do not address the procedural route.** FOP/DOC arose from Superior Court review of a PERB order. FOP/DOC, p. 2, ¶ 5. The uploaded documents do not establish the current procedural route, deadlines, or filing requirements for review of Opinion No. 1904. PERB stated that its decision "is final upon issuance." PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 7. Timing must be confirmed immediately from sources outside this record.

**7. What MPD preserved before PERB.** MPD argued three things:

- the arbitrator misapplied Douglas by requiring MPD to prove comparability under factor 6;
- he substituted his judgment for the deciding official's on factor 12;
- reinstatement contravenes a dominant public policy against unjustified police use of deadly force.

PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2. Arguments outside these three are at risk under § 1-617.13(b). FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2; p. 7, n. 8.

## I. MPD's strongest arguments that the Award is contrary to law

### A. Lead argument: the arbitrator adopted Douglas as governing law, then ignored its express limit on substituting a penalty

**1. The arbitrator expressly chose the governing law.**

- He held that his review "is not de novo."
- Because the deciding official "weighed the factors identified in Douglas . . . pursuant to 6-B DCMR sec. 1606.2," he would ask whether MPD's conclusions "fall within tolerable limits of reasonableness." Award, p. 2, ¶ 5.
- The Final Notice likewise applied Douglas "as required by 6-B DCMR sec. 1606.2." Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 13.

This parallels FOP/DOC, where the arbitrator expressly awarded relief "in accordance with the Back Pay Act." FOP/DOC, p. 4, ¶ 3.

**2. Douglas contains an express, specific limit on substitution.**

> "Only if the Board finds that the agency failed to weigh the relevant factors, or that the agency's judgment clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness, is it appropriate for the Board then to specify how the agency's decision should be corrected to bring the penalty within the parameters of reasonableness."

Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.

Douglas adds that the reviewer's role "is not to insist that the balance be struck precisely where the Board would choose to strike it if the Board were in the agency's shoes in the first instance." Douglas, p. 20 (printed 332), ¶ 2.

**3. The arbitrator made neither required finding, and he found the opposite of the first.**

- **Failure to weigh.** He found MPD "weighed the factors." Award, p. 2, ¶ 5. He found MPD weighed ten of the twelve factors "within tolerable limits of reasonableness," with findings "supported by the record." Award, p. 2, ¶ 6. That rules out the first trigger.
- **Clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness.** He never found that *termination* clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness. He substituted a different trigger that Douglas does not contain: "If a conclusion on a material factor falls outside those limits, the penalty determination cannot stand as issued." Award, p. 2, ¶ 5.
- **His own balancing.** He then did the balancing himself: "Weighing the sustained findings on the remaining ten factors against the failures on factors six and twelve, I conclude that a 45-day suspension without pay is the appropriate penalty." Award, p. 2, ¶ 9.
- **The wrong remedial question.** Douglas allows a reviewer only to bring a penalty "within the parameters of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1. The arbitrator asked instead what penalty was "appropriate." That is the determination DCMR § 1606 assigns to "managers." DCMR, p. 1, §§ 1606.1, 1606.3.

**4. Why this fits FOP/DOC.** In FOP/DOC, the arbitrator invoked the Back Pay Act but ignored its express offset clause. The DCCA held PERB was "not unreasonable" in calling that "contrary to law," given the "obvious incongruity" between the law relied on and the result. FOP/DOC, p. 4, ¶¶ 3–4; p. 5, ¶ 1.

The incongruity here can be seen on the face of the Award itself:

- Paragraph 6 sustains MPD's weighing of ten factors.
- Paragraph 9 nonetheless reweighs all twelve and selects a new penalty.
- No paragraph contains the finding Douglas makes a precondition to that step.

Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 5–6, 9. Showing this requires comparing the Award's text against one sentence of Douglas, not a "comprehensive analysis." FOP/DOC, p. 4, ¶ 4.

**5. The two arguments MPD preserved feed into this lead argument.** The factor-6 and factor-12 errors below are the two "failures" the arbitrator used to justify his reweighing. Award, p. 2, ¶ 9. If either fails, the "factor-by-factor" premise in ¶ 5 collapses as to that factor.

**Preservation caution.** PERB's summary describes MPD's arguments by factor and does not separately mention the overall-reweighing theory. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2. Before filing, check MPD's Arbitration Review Request. If the reweighing theory was not "first urged before the Board," it should be presented as the reason the preserved factor-6 and factor-12 errors were outcome-determinative, not as a stand-alone objection. FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2; p. 7, n. 8.

### B. Factor 6: the arbitrator imposed a burden Douglas does not impose and applied a proof standard instead of a reasonableness standard (preserved; PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2)

**1. What the Award holds.** MPD "did not prove that the comparator officers were similarly situated," and "failed to carry its burden to prove comparability." Its factor-6 conclusion therefore "falls outside tolerable limits of reasonableness." Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.

**2. Why that is contrary to Douglas on its face.**

- **The burden belongs to the grievant.** Douglas treats disparate penalty as the *employee's* contention, which the agency may then rebut: "if an appellant contends that a penalty exceeds that imposed upon other employees in like circumstances, the agency would remain free to present evidence to rebut such contention." Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65; see p. 33 (printed 345), ¶¶ 2–5.
  - Where "no issue has been raised concerning the penalty," the agency's prima facie case "will normally suffice." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
  - Under assumption (b), no disparity claim was made before the hearing. The Award identifies no similarly situated member who received a lighter penalty. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
- **Reasonable disagreement means the judgment was reasonable.** Under assumption (a), reasonable minds can disagree about comparability. A judgment on which reasonable minds differ falls "within tolerable limits of reasonableness" by definition. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5; Douglas, p. 17 (printed 329), ¶ 2.
  - The arbitrator's own words show the switch. He asked whether MPD "prove[d]" comparability, which is a proof standard, not a reasonableness standard. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
- **Douglas rejects rigid matching.** The consistency principle "does not require mathematical rigidity or perfect consistency." It is to be applied with "practical realism." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2.
  - The arbitrator disqualified comparators on single features: on-duty status, a fatality, prior complaints. Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
  - All four comparators involved unjustified force with a firearm or serious bodily injury, and each ended in termination. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 1–5.

**3. A factual misstatement is visible on the face of the Award.**

- The Award says "[t]wo of the four comparators involved on-duty conduct." Award, p. 2, ¶ 7.
- The Comparator Memo shows **three**: Guzman, Sadler, and Voss. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 2, 3, 5.
- The Award never addresses Comparator B (Sadler: no prior discipline, terminated) or Comparator D (Voss: one unrelated prior complaint, no fatality). Both closely track Braddock's profile. Comparator Memo, p. 1, ¶¶ 3, 5; Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 16.

**Use with care.** Misco holds that a reviewing body may not reject an arbitrator's findings "simply because it disagrees with them," even for "improvident, even silly, factfinding." Misco, 484 U.S. at 38, ¶ 1; 484 U.S. at 39, ¶ 2. MPD should present the miscount only as evidence that the arbitrator never conducted the Douglas comparison he purported to conduct. It should not be presented as an independent factual error.

**4. Assumption (c) strengthens the burden argument.**

- Braddock knew of the comparators before the guilty finding.
- He had counsel and a hearing.
- He raised no disparity claim.

Final Notice, p. 1, ¶ 2. The "fair opportunity to respond" that Douglas requires was given. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), ¶ 2. MPD's unrebutted prima facie showing was therefore all Douglas required at that stage. Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.

### C. Factor 12: requiring "evidence" beyond the deciding official's reasoning contradicts Douglas, and the finding is irreconcilable with factors the arbitrator sustained (preserved; PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2)

**1. What the Award holds.**

- MPD "offered no evidence supporting that conclusion beyond the deciding official's assertion."
- "[A] suspension would likely have sufficed to deter future misconduct by the Grievant and by other members of the force."

Award, p. 2, ¶ 8.

**2. Douglas does not require separate evidence for this kind of judgment.**

- An agency's prima facie case is the facts plus "a concise statement of its reasoning from those facts," showing the reasoning is not "inherently irrational." Douglas, p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
- Where an adverse effect is "self-evident," detailed evidence is not required. Douglas, p. 29 (printed 341), ¶ 1.
- MPD's facts were undisputed. Award, p. 1, ¶ 3. Its reasoning was stated: anything less than removal "would signal to the workforce that such conduct is survivable." Final Notice, p. 4, ¶ 25.
- "Would likely have sufficed" is the arbitrator's own competing prediction. It is not a finding that MPD's prediction "clearly exceeded the limits of reasonableness." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 1.

**3. The Award contradicts itself.** The arbitrator sustained two of MPD's findings as reasonable and supported by the record:

- confidence in Braddock is "lost" (factor 5); and
- his rehabilitation potential is "poor" because he "accepts no responsibility" (factor 10).

Award, p. 2, ¶ 6; Final Notice, p. 3, ¶¶ 18, 23. A finding that a suspension would likely deter *the Grievant* cannot be reconciled with those findings. The inconsistency is visible within the Award itself. Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 6, 8.

### D. Secondary argument, probably not preserved: the waiver provision of the regulation the arbitrator invoked

**1. The provision.** Chapter 16 provides that the employee "shall raise every defense, fact, or matter in extenuation, exculpation, or mitigation" in the written response. "The failure of the employee to raise a known defense, fact, or matter shall constitute a waiver of such defense, fact, or matter in all subsequent proceedings." DCMR, p. 2, § 1621.6.

**2. Application.**

- Under assumptions (b) and (c), Braddock knew of the comparators before decision and never claimed disparate treatment in his response. Final Notice, p. 1, ¶ 2.
- The arbitrator expressly anchored his review in Chapter 16. Award, p. 2, ¶ 5 ("pursuant to 6-B DCMR sec. 1606.2").
- On FOP/DOC's reasoning, an arbitrator who relies on a body of law must follow its express terms. FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 4.
- FOP/DOC also suggests the court need not resolve whether the external law *independently* applied, because the arbitrator's own reliance on it was enough. FOP/DOC, p. 6, n. 4 (declining to resolve whether the Back Pay Act applied to District employees).

**3. Why this is secondary. It has three serious defects.**

1. **Applicability.** Chapter 16 states it does not apply to "[s]worn members of the Metropolitan Police Department." DCMR, p. 1, § 1600.2(g). Even where it applies, a conflicting CBA provision controls. DCMR, p. 1, § 1602.2(c).
2. **Douglas points the other way.** Douglas expressly contemplates disparity claims first raised on appeal. Douglas, p. 19 (printed 331), n. 65. Misco treats procedural questions about what evidence an arbitrator considers as matters left to the arbitrator. Misco, 484 U.S. at 39, ¶ 3; 484 U.S. at 40, ¶ 1.
3. **Preservation.** PERB's summary of MPD's arguments does not mention § 1621.6. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2. Unless MPD's Arbitration Review Request raised it, § 1-617.13(b) likely bars it. FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2; p. 7, n. 8.

**Recommendation.** Use § 1621.6 at most as context supporting the burden argument in Part I.B. Do not present it as an independent ground unless the PERB filings show it was raised.

### E. PERB's decision is unreasoned and cannot be sustained on grounds PERB did not give

This is the argument most likely to produce relief: a remand.

1. **No analysis.** PERB's entire analysis is four conclusory paragraphs. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶¶ 3–6. It does not address:
   - MPD's theory that a misapplication of law apparent on the face of an award is "contrary to law" (FOP/DOC, p. 4, ¶ 2);
   - the Douglas substitution limit;
   - the burden allocation under Douglas, n. 65.
2. **A non-statutory test.** PERB rested in part on its conclusion that the Award "draws its essence from the parties' agreement." PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 6. That is not a ground in § 1-605.02(6), and it does not answer whether the Award is contrary to law. § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6).
3. **The court cannot fill the gap.** The DCCA "may not 'affirm on a ground not relied on by the agency.'" FOP/DOC, p. 5–6, n. 2. Deference attaches to PERB's "interpretation." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1. Where PERB offered no interpretation of how Douglas interacts with the "contrary to law" standard, there is little to defer to.
4. **Labeling legal error as "interpretation."** PERB treated the Douglas issues as "the Arbitrator's interpretation," entitled to deference. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4. FOP/DOC confirms that arbitral deference applies only where the arbitrator is "called upon actually to interpret the law." FOP/DOC, p. 6, n. 3. Here the arbitrator did not interpret Douglas. He recited it and then departed from it. Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 5, 9.
5. **Lack of engagement with the record.** PERB misstated the very penalty under review (40 days, not 45). PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 1; Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 9–10.

**Realistic remedy.** The most plausible relief is a remand directing PERB to apply the FOP/DOC framework to MPD's Douglas arguments. Outright reinstatement of the termination is unlikely.

### F. The single weakest point in the contrary-to-law argument

**Douglas's governing rule is a reasonableness standard, the arbitrator recited it correctly, and so the error reads as misapplication, not as ignoring an express command.**

**Why it is the weakest point.** It attacks the element every contrary-to-law theory here depends on: a *facial* violation of *law*.

1. **FOP/DOC turned on clear, self-executing text.** The Back Pay Act's "less any amounts earned" clause was "clear on its face." FOP conceded it "unambiguously requires" offset, and "[n]o interpretation and no 'comprehensive analysis'" was needed. FOP/DOC, p. 4, ¶ 4. Douglas has no comparable clause.
   - Its factors "are not to be evaluated mechanistically."
   - Its consistency principle demands "practical realism."
   - It expressly allows the factfinder to probe "genuine issues of justice or equity."

   Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 2; p. 22 (printed 334), ¶ 2.
2. **The arbitrator said the right words.** He stated the deferential standard correctly ("not de novo"; "tolerable limits of reasonableness"). Award, p. 2, ¶ 5. Opposing counsel will argue that his ¶ 5 "material factor" rule is simply how he applied Douglas. On that view it is an interpretation the parties "bargained for." FOP/DOC, p. 6, n. 3; PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 4.
3. **Assumption (a) cuts both ways.** If reasonable minds can differ on comparability, the arbitrator's view is also a reasonable one. Douglas puts "the ultimate burden" on the agency to persuade "of the appropriateness of the penalty." Douglas, p. 21 (printed 333), ¶ 3.
4. **Misco supports arbitral remedial discretion.** Misco recognizes that "[n]ormally, an arbitrator is authorized to disagree with the sanction imposed for employee misconduct." Misco, 484 U.S. at 41, ¶ 2. The parties stipulated that the issue was whether "the penalty of termination [is] appropriate" and, "[i]f not, what shall the remedy be." Award, p. 1, ¶ 2. That stipulation weakens any claim that the arbitrator lacked authority to choose a penalty.
5. **Double deference compounds every weakness.** The DCCA asks only whether PERB's refusal was rationally indefensible. It sustains PERB's reading even against "another reasonable interpretation." FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1; p. 7, n. 6.

**The other weaknesses are less serious.** Preservation and the miscount can be managed by careful framing. This weakness goes to the threshold element and cannot be framed away.

**Best response.**

- The violation is visible from the Award's own text, without analysis of evidence: ¶ 6 versus ¶ 9, measured against Douglas's "[o]nly if" sentence.
- The Award's "material factor" rule is not an interpretation of Douglas at all. It is a rule Douglas does not contain, and its effect is to bypass the one express limit Douglas does contain.

## II. MPD's strongest arguments that the Award is on its face contrary to public policy

### A. The test

1. **The statute.** PERB may set aside an award only if it "on its face is contrary to law and public policy." § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6).

2. **The W.R. Grace / Misco standard, as adopted by the DCCA.**
   - If an agreement as interpreted "violates some explicit public policy," a court must refuse to enforce it. "Such a public policy, however, must be well defined and dominant, and is to be ascertained 'by reference to the laws and legal precedents and not from general considerations of supposed public interests.'" W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2.
   - Misco confirms that W.R. Grace does "not otherwise sanction a broad judicial power to set aside arbitration awards as against public policy." The question is whether the award creates an "explicit conflict with other 'laws and legal precedents.'" "[A]n alleged public policy must be properly framed under the approach set out in W.R. Grace, and the violation of such a policy must be clearly shown." Misco, 484 U.S. at 43, ¶ 1.
   - The DCCA adopted this formulation. A public policy "is to be ascertained by reference to the laws and legal precedents and not from general considerations of supposed public interests." FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2 (quoting *MPD*, 901 A.2d at 789, quoting W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766).

3. **The elements MPD must establish.**
   1. **Source.** A policy grounded in laws and legal precedents, not common sense. Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 1 (rejecting a policy "firmly rooted in common sense" but not shown to rest on existing law).
   2. **Character.** The policy must be explicit, well defined, and dominant. W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2.
   3. **Clear violation by the award itself.** The *award*, here reinstatement, must violate the policy. The violation must be "clearly shown" and "must rest on more than speculation or assumption." Misco, 484 U.S. at 43, ¶ 1; 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 2.
   4. **No factfinding by the reviewing body.** The reviewing body may not draw its own factual inferences about the grievant's future conduct or "amenability to discipline." Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 3; 484 U.S. at 45, ¶ 1.
   5. **On the face of the award.** The violation must appear on the face of the Award. § 1-605.02, p. 1, ¶ (6). PERB calls the exception "extremely narrow." PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 5.

4. **"Law and public policy" is conjunctive.** The DCCA has indicated that an award contrary to a specific law "ipso facto may be said to be contrary to the public policy that the law embodies." FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2. In practice, this ties the public-policy ground to an identifiable law. It reinforces the source element.

### B. The specific documented policy MPD should invoke, and where the record documents it

**The policy.** A police officer may not use deadly force against a person without an objectively reasonable belief that he or another person faces an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury.

**Where the record documents it.** The source is **MPD General Order 120.21, Attachment A, Part A-12**, as recited in the charge: Braddock "used deadly force against an unarmed person without an objectively reasonable belief that he or another person faced an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury." Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 12. The Final Notice calls MPD's use-of-force policy "the most heavily trained directive in the agency." Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 22.

**Why this formulation, and not the broader one MPD gave PERB.** MPD argued to PERB a "dominant public policy against the unjustified use of deadly force by police officers." PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2. The General Order version is better for three reasons:

1. **It is tied to a written legal standard in the record**, not to "general considerations of supposed public interests." W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2.
2. **The arbitrator adopted it word for word.** He found that "[a] reasonable officer in the Grievant's position would not have perceived an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury." Award, p. 1, ¶ 4. MPD therefore needs no factfinding by PERB or the court. That avoids the defect identified in Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 3.
3. **It stays within the preserved argument.** It is a narrower version of the policy MPD presented to PERB, so it is within the scope of the preserved objection. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 2; FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2.

**Supporting regulatory material, with limits.** Chapter 16 provides that District employees must meet standards of conduct "both on and off the job." DCMR, p. 1, § 1605.1. It identifies as cause "[c]onduct that an employee should reasonably know is a violation of law or regulation" and off-duty conduct with an "identifiable nexus to the employee's position." DCMR, p. 1, § 1605.4(a)(3)–(4). It singles out MPD employees and employees "authorized to carry a firearm" for a heightened standard. DCMR, p. 1, § 1605.5.

These provisions help show the policy is *explicit* in District law as applied to armed employees. They are only supporting material, because Chapter 16 excludes sworn MPD members. DCMR, p. 1, § 1600.2(g). Section 1605.5(a) also depends on a "criminal offense," and Virginia declined prosecution. Final Notice, p. 1, ¶ 1.

### C. Applying the test to the Award: MPD's strongest arguments

**1. The conduct that violated the policy is established on the face of the Award, without speculation.**

- **Misco's problem was speculation.** There, the "assumed connection" between marijuana in a car and drug use on the job was "tenuous, at best." Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 2.
- **Here, nothing needs to be inferred.** The arbitrator found every element of the policy violation:
  - off duty, Braddock "armed himself" and "confronted an unarmed man";
  - he "fired two rounds into Mr. Pryor's chest and shoulder";
  - he "knew he held no police authority in Virginia";
  - he "never saw a weapon";
  - no reasonable officer "would have perceived an imminent threat."

  Award, p. 1, ¶¶ 3–4.
- **No new factfinding is required.** MPD's argument uses only the arbitrator's own findings, consistent with Misco, 484 U.S. at 45, ¶ 1.

**2. Reinstatement is to the very position that carries the authority he misused.**

- **Misco's alternative-job point does not apply.** Misco found it significant that the grievant could be placed in "an equivalent [job]" where he would not "pose a serious threat to the asserted public policy." Misco, 484 U.S. at 45, ¶ 1.
- **The Award orders reinstatement "to his position of record."** Award, p. 2, ¶ 10. That position is sworn officer entrusted with the lawful use of force. Final Notice, p. 3, ¶ 15.
- **The conflict is therefore direct.** It lies between the remedy and the policy, not merely between the misconduct and the policy. Unlike Misco, there is no alternative assignment in the Award that would avoid the conflict.

**3. The arbitrator's sustained findings show the risk will continue.** The Award sustains as reasonable and supported by the record MPD's findings that:

- the offense was "grave";
- the chain of command's confidence is "lost";
- Braddock's rehabilitation potential is "poor" because he "accepts no responsibility for the shooting."

Award, p. 2, ¶ 6; Final Notice, p. 3, ¶¶ 18, 23. The violation of policy is therefore established by the arbitrator's own findings, not by MPD's characterization of them. It is "clearly shown." Misco, 484 U.S. at 43, ¶ 1.

**4. The factor-12 finding does not save the Award.**

- **The counterargument.** FOP will rely on Misco's statement that where an arbitrator "imposed discipline short of discharge because he found as a factual matter that [the grievant] could be trusted," a court "could not upset the award." Misco, 484 U.S. at 45, ¶ 1.
- **The arbitrator made no trust finding.** He found only that a suspension "would likely have sufficed to deter." Award, p. 2, ¶ 8. That is a general deterrence prediction, not a finding that Braddock can be trusted with deadly force.
- **His sustained factor-10 finding points the other way.** Braddock "accepts no responsibility." Award, p. 2, ¶ 6.
- **The prediction is not a credited finding of fact.** The arbitrator made it while faulting MPD for lack of evidence, and he cited no evidence of his own. Award, p. 2, ¶ 8.

**5. W.R. Grace's reasoning supports MPD.** W.R. Grace refused to enforce terms whose performance would conflict with an explicit legal obligation, but found no conflict on its facts. W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2; 461 U.S. at 767, ¶ 1. The conflict here is concrete. The Award requires MPD to return a deadly-force authority to an officer the arbitrator found used deadly force with no objectively reasonable basis. Award, p. 1, ¶ 4; p. 2, ¶ 10.

**6. PERB gave no reasoning on this issue.** PERB's entire treatment is two sentences. It did not identify or evaluate the policy MPD invoked. PERB Decision, p. 1, ¶ 5. Misco and W.R. Grace both treat the public policy question as "ultimately one for resolution by the courts." W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2; Misco, 484 U.S. at 43, ¶ 1. MPD can argue this calls for less deference to an unexplained PERB conclusion. The uploaded DCCA authority does not decide that point, however. FOP/DOC, p. 3, ¶ 1, applies deference to PERB's CMPA interpretations generally.

### D. The single weakest point in the public policy argument

**The policy is not documented in any "laws and legal precedents" in the record. Its only source is an internal MPD General Order, and that order is quoted in the Final Notice but is not itself in the record.**

**Why it is the weakest point.**

1. **It fails a threshold element, so the analysis may never reach application.**
   - W.R. Grace and Misco require that the policy be "ascertained by reference to the laws and legal precedents." W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2; Misco, 484 U.S. at 43, ¶ 1.
   - Misco reversed precisely because the lower court "made no attempt to review existing laws and legal precedents." It did so even though the policy was "firmly rooted in common sense." Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 1.
   - The DCCA links the exception to "a specific law." FOP/DOC, p. 5, ¶ 2.
   - If the source fails, the strong application arguments in Part II.C never come into play.

2. **The available sources each fall short.**
   - **General Order 120.21.** It is the employer's own work rule, documented only through the Final Notice. Final Notice, p. 2, ¶ 12. In Misco, the employer's own plant rule (Rule II.1) did not supply the public policy. Misco, 484 U.S. at 44, ¶ 1; 484 U.S. at 39, ¶ 2.
   - **The DCMR provisions.** They exclude sworn MPD members. DCMR, p. 1, § 1600.2(g).
   - **Criminal law.** No criminal conviction or charge exists. Virginia declined prosecution. Final Notice, p. 1, ¶ 1.
   - **Misco and W.R. Grace.** Neither identifies a policy concerning police use of force.
   - **Within this record, MPD cannot cure the gap.**

3. **It turns the argument into the one Misco rejects.** Without a legal source, MPD's position reduces to "general considerations of supposed public interests," which is exactly what W.R. Grace and Misco prohibit. W.R. Grace, 461 U.S. at 766, ¶ 2.

**Why this outranks the other weaknesses.** The next most serious problem is Misco's rule that a reviewing body cannot override an arbitrator's choice of "discipline short of discharge" based on its own view of the employee's trustworthiness. Misco, 484 U.S. at 45, ¶ 1; Award, p. 2, ¶ 8. MPD at least has a textual answer to that: the arbitrator made no trust finding, and he sustained the factor-10 finding. Award, p. 2, ¶¶ 6, 8. No document in the record answers the source problem.
