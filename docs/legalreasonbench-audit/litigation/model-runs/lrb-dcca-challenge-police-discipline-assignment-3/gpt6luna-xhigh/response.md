# MPD v. PERB — Viability of a DCCA Challenge Under D.C. Code § 1-605.02(6)

## Bottom line

A petition is colorable, but the statutory exceptions make it a long shot. MPD’s better route is “contrary to law,” principally because the arbitrator announced the deferential Douglas standard and then arguably replaced MPD’s prospective deterrence judgment with his own. The comparator ruling is a weaker, fact-bound extension of the same theory. A public-policy challenge is weaker still: the record proves extremely serious misconduct and reinstatement, but contains no applicable law requiring removal or showing that reinstatement itself compels unlawful force. The DCCA’s deference to PERB, and PERB’s limited authority to disturb an award, are substantial obstacles.

## I. Governing review standard

D.C. Code § 1-605.02(6) makes PERB review of a grievance-arbitration award exclusive and permits modification, vacatur, or remand only on specified grounds, including where “the award on its face is contrary to law and public policy.” (D.C. Code § 1-605.02 (2016) (dc_code_1-605.02.pdf), PDF p. 1, subsection (6).) The DCCA reviews PERB’s decision as if the appeal had initially been heard in that court, not by making a fresh determination of the penalty. (Fraternal Ord. of Police/Dep’t of Corr. Lab. Comm. v. D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd., 973 A.2d 174 (D.C. 2009) (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf), PDF p. 2, § I, ¶ 3, beginning “After the Superior Court affirmed.”) The court said it would not easily disturb PERB: it defers to PERB fact findings supported by substantial evidence and to PERB’s CMPA interpretation unless unreasonable under prevailing law, inconsistent with the statute, or plainly erroneous; a decision must stand unless rationally indefensible. (Fraternal Ord. of Police/Dep’t of Corr. Lab. Comm. v. D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd., 973 A.2d 174 (D.C. 2009) (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf), PDF p. 3, § II, ¶ 1, beginning “This court will not easily disturb.”)

That deference operates alongside the statute’s narrow face-of-the-award limit. *FOP/Department of Corrections* says PERB cannot substitute its judgment for the arbitrator absent a clear violation of law evident on the award’s face; a facially apparent legal misinterpretation may qualify. (Fraternal Ord. of Police/Dep’t of Corr. Lab. Comm. v. D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd., 973 A.2d 174 (D.C. 2009) (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf), PDF p. 3, § II, ¶ 2, beginning “The PERB itself has limited authority”; PDF p. 3, § III, ¶ 2, beginning “As we read the PERB decision”; PDF p. 4, § III, ¶ 3, beginning “In our view, section 1-605.02(6).”) The *Braddock* PERB Decision likewise says review is narrow, mere disagreement with the arbitrator’s Douglas interpretation or evidentiary assessment is insufficient, and PERB will not substitute its judgment for the arbitrator’s. (Opinion No. 1904, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd. Nov. 6, 2025) (perb_decision_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4.) *FOP* also recognizes deference to an arbitrator’s interpretation when the parties have bargained for it. (Fraternal Ord. of Police/Dep’t of Corr. Lab. Comm. v. D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd., 973 A.2d 174 (D.C. 2009) (fop_doc_labor_comm_v_perb_2009.pdf), PDF p. 6, footnote 3, ¶ 1.)

Accordingly, MPD must show more than that the arbitrator misweighed the Douglas factors or that PERB could reasonably have reached another result. Its best formulation is that the Award’s own reasoning facially departs from Douglas’s stated limits on review. MPD should not make the exclusion of sworn MPD members from 6-B DCMR chapter 16 its principal theory: although § 1600.2(g) excludes them, the Final Notice and Award both used the Douglas/§ 1606.2 framework, and the Award’s stated review standard can be challenged on its own terms. (D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, § 1600.2(g); D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 13; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 5.)

## II. MPD’s strongest “contrary to law” argument

### A. The Award arguably recited Douglas’s rule but did not apply it to factor twelve

The Award expressly disclaimed de novo review and stated that the test was whether MPD’s conclusions fell within “tolerable limits of reasonableness.” (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 5.) Douglas, in turn, says the reviewer does not strike the balance where the reviewer would have struck it; the question is whether the agency conscientiously considered relevant factors and made a responsible balance within tolerable limits. Only a failure to weigh a relevant factor or a judgment clearly outside those limits permits correction. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed pp. 332–33, PDF pp. 20–21, ¶¶ 1–2, beginning “Not all of these factors” and “In considering whether the agency’s judgment.”)

MPD’s clearest example of a possible departure is factor twelve. The deciding official considered a significant suspension and explained that a sanction short of removal for shooting an unarmed, non-threatening person would signal to the workforce that such conduct was “survivable.” (D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 4 ¶ 25.) The arbitrator found no supporting evidence beyond the deciding official’s assertion, then supplied his own prediction that a lengthy suspension—with lost pay, lost standing, and a permanent record—would “likely” deter Braddock and other members. He therefore held MPD’s conclusion outside tolerable limits. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 8.)

MPD can argue that the Award converted a discretionary, predictive assessment of deterrence into a demand for evidence beyond a reasoned management judgment. The deciding official did not merely state a conclusion: she tied it to the grave, specific misconduct and explained the message a lesser sanction would send to the force. Douglas recognizes deterrence as a relevant factor, but directs the reviewer to test the reasonableness of the agency’s judgment, not make a competing prediction about what sanction will deter. The arbitrator accepted the other ten factors as within tolerable limits, yet used his contrary deterrence prediction, together with factor six, to replace removal with a 45-day suspension. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed p. 332, PDF p. 20, factor (12); printed pp. 332–33, PDF pp. 20–21, ¶¶ 1–2; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 6, 8–9.)

The argument should be framed as an error in the rule of review—not as a claim that the arbitrator was required to credit MPD. Douglas leaves the ultimate burden of persuading the reviewing tribunal on the agency and permits additional evidence when the penalty is challenged or the reviewer perceives a genuine equity issue. It also says an unchallenged penalty may be supported by a prima facie showing consisting of the relevant facts, a concise non-irrational rationale, and conformity with law. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed pp. 333–34, PDF pp. 21–22, ¶ 3 on p. 333 and ¶¶ 1–2 on p. 334, beginning “Lastly, it should be clear” and “In many cases.”) Thus the best point is that no rule required empirical deterrence evidence and the written rationale was not facially irrational—not that MPD’s burden disappeared.

### B. Factor six supports a secondary, narrower version of the same argument

Douglas cautions that the “like penalties for like offenses” principle is not mathematical, does not require perfect consistency despite differences in circumstances, and should be applied with practical realism rather than rigid formalism. It also cautions against “surface consistency” that prevents consideration of the individual case. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed p. 333, PDF p. 21, ¶ 2, beginning “In considering whether the agency’s judgment.”) MPD identified four terminated sworn members disciplined for unjustified force involving a firearm or serious bodily injury. The Award acknowledged those comparators but found them not proven similarly situated because two cases involved on-duty conduct, one involved a fatality, and one involved two prior sustained force complaints; Braddock’s shooting was off duty, nonfatal, and followed one unrelated reprimand. (D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 3 ¶ 19; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 7.)

The strongest response is that those distinctions affect weight, not whether MPD could reasonably use the cases as “same or similar” comparators. The common features—sworn officers, unjustified serious force, and termination—gave the deciding official a rational basis to treat the cases as probative; the stipulated fact that reasonable minds can disagree about comparability reinforces that MPD’s assessment was at least within a range of reasonable judgment. The arbitrator’s finding that MPD had not “prove[n]” comparability may therefore be attacked as turning practical comparison into a threshold identity requirement. But the word “prove” alone is not error: Douglas assigns the ultimate burden to MPD. The problem, if any, is the alleged rigid method of comparison. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed pp. 333–34, PDF pp. 21–22, ¶ 2 on p. 333 and ¶¶ 1–2 on p. 334; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 7.)

Timing helps MPD only at the margin. Braddock knew before MPD’s final guilt determination that it intended to rely on the four cases, yet neither he nor the union alleged disparate treatment before the arbitration hearing. Douglas says the unchallenged penalty ordinarily requires no more than prima facie justification, but also permits the agency to be asked for further evidence once a challenge is made. The arbitrator considered the issue at arbitration; the supplied record does not establish whether MPD was asked to supplement the summaries. Accordingly, the timing supports an argument that MPD’s original presentation was not irrational, but does not eliminate its ultimate burden once the penalty was contested. (D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 3 ¶ 19; Memorandum from Yolanda Britt, Director, Disciplinary Review Division, Hum. Res. Mgmt. Bureau, D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, to Sandra M. Delgado, Assistant Chief, Professional Development Bureau, re: Comparator Discipline Cases, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841 (Braddock) (Apr. 11, 2024) (comparator_discipline_cases.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶ 6; Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed p. 334, PDF p. 22, ¶ 2, beginning “In many cases.”)

### C. Why PERB’s affirmance is challengeable—and the weakest point

MPD should distinguish its claim from the “mere disagreement” PERB rejected. The Award itself states the correct deferential formula, then arguably makes a new evidentiary requirement on deterrence and substitutes its own prediction; that is a facially visible challenge to legal method, not simply a request to reweigh which penalty is preferable. PERB’s Decision recites the narrow standard and concludes there is no statutory basis, but does not explain why the Award’s factor-twelve reasoning is consistent with Douglas’s limits on second-guessing. (Opinion No. 1904, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd. Nov. 6, 2025) (perb_decision_braddock.pdf), PDF pp. 1–2, ¶¶ 3–6; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 5, 8.)

**Single weakest point—contrary to law:** the Award expressly announced the Douglas/tolerable-limits standard, and Douglas puts the ultimate burden on MPD. The arbitrator found that the deterrence conclusion lacked support beyond the deciding official’s assertion and made a factual/evaluative finding that a severe suspension likely would suffice; a reviewing court could reasonably treat that as the arbitrator’s assessment of whether MPD carried its burden, not a facial legal rule requiring empirical proof. The comparator determination is similarly a disputed application of facts, and the stipulated possibility of reasonable disagreement makes it difficult to call the result a clear violation apparent on the Award’s face. (Douglas v. Veterans Administration, 5 M.S.P.R. 280 (1981) (douglas_v_veterans_admin.pdf), printed pp. 333–34, PDF pp. 21–22, ¶¶ 3 and 1–2 respectively; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 7–9.)

**Assessment:** this is MPD’s strongest statutory exception, especially as to factor twelve, but remains a difficult petition under the face-of-the-award rule and DCCA deference to PERB. Factor six is best used to corroborate that the arbitrator may have crossed from review into reweighing; it is unlikely to carry the claim on its own.

## III. MPD’s strongest “on its face contrary to public policy” argument

### A. Test and the policy available in this record

The public-policy exception is exceptionally narrow. The asserted policy must be explicit, well-defined, and dominant; it must be identified by reference to laws and legal precedents, not generalized views of the public interest. And the violation must be clearly shown by the award; speculation and judicial factfinding cannot supply the missing conflict. (W.R. Grace & Co. v. Local Union 759, 461 U.S. 757 (1983) (wr_grace_v_rubber_workers.pdf), printed p. 766, ¶ 1, beginning “As with any contract”; United Paperworkers Int’l Union v. Misco, Inc., 484 U.S. 29 (1987) (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf), printed p. 43, ¶ 1, beginning “In W.R. Grace”; printed p. 44, ¶¶ 1–3, beginning “As we see it,” “Even if the Court of Appeals’ formulation,” and “In any event.”) PERB applied that narrow formulation and found no clear violation on the face of the Award. (Opinion No. 1904, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd. Nov. 6, 2025) (perb_decision_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 5.)

MPD’s most specific policy statement in the supplied record is the prohibition, attributed in the Final Notice to MPD General Order 120.21, Attachment A, Part A-12, against using deadly force on an unarmed person without an objectively reasonable belief of an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury. The Final Notice identifies that rule as the basis of the sustained Conduct Unbecoming charge. (D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 12.) The Final Notice documents the decision’s specific factual basis: Braddock was off duty, knew he lacked police authority in Virginia, confronted an unarmed man, never saw a weapon, and fired twice; the arbitrator found that a reasonable officer would not have perceived an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury. (D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF pp. 1–2 ¶¶ 3–11; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4.)

As corroboration for the public-safety and operational-integrity dimension, the attached DCMR describes a District policy of high standards of integrity on and off duty and action to protect the integrity of government service; it also identifies conduct detrimental to public health, safety, or welfare as a basis for summary action. (D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, §§ 1605.1–.2, 1616.2(c).) But the same chapter expressly excludes sworn MPD members. It is therefore corroborative evidence of the District’s stated policy, not a directly applicable discipline rule for Braddock. (D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, § 1600.2(g).) The attached record does not include the text of General Order 120.21 itself; MPD can rely only on the Final Notice’s description of the order, not represent the omitted order as an independently supplied legal authority.

### B. Application to the Award

MPD’s best public-policy presentation is that this is not a case in which the arbitrator merely found an unproven allegation or a technical violation. The Award sustains cause after finding that Braddock shot an unarmed man twice in the chest and shoulder without a reasonable perception of imminent deadly danger, and it orders reinstatement to his sworn position. (In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶¶ 3–4; PDF p. 3 ¶ 10.) MPD can contend that reinstating an officer after that express finding conflicts with the public-safety policy embodied by the rule against unjustified deadly force and the District’s stated concern for public safety and government integrity. The argument is strongest when tied to the explicit rule described in the Final Notice, rather than to generalized outrage, public confidence, or a policy preference for harsher discipline.

The answer under *W.R. Grace* and *Misco* is formidable. The Award imposes a 45-day suspension and reinstatement; it does not direct Braddock to use force, authorize future unjustified force, or require any act that the identified rule forbids. The public policy documented in the Final Notice prohibits a type of conduct; the record does not identify a legal rule requiring permanent removal for a proven violation. Nor does the Award make a finding that Braddock will repeat the conduct or that reinstatement will result in unlawful force. *Misco* rejects a policy challenge where the asserted future danger depends on an inference or speculation and holds that a reviewing court cannot make the arbitrator’s factual findings itself. (United Paperworkers Int’l Union v. Misco, Inc., 484 U.S. 29 (1987) (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf), printed pp. 44–45, ¶¶ 2–3, beginning “Even if the Court of Appeals’ formulation” and “In any event.”) The *Braddock* PERB Decision’s conclusion that no clear policy violation appears on the Award’s face is thus difficult to label plainly erroneous. (Opinion No. 1904, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd. Nov. 6, 2025) (perb_decision_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶¶ 5–6.)

**Single weakest point—public policy:** the closed record does not establish an applicable law making reinstatement itself unlawful or requiring termination. The most tailored rule is reported in the Final Notice as an MPD General Order, while the attached DCMR policy provisions expressly exclude sworn MPD members; neither establishes a mandatory penalty of removal. Under *Misco*, showing that the misconduct violated a rule is not enough—the award must itself clearly conflict with a well-defined, dominant policy, and anticipated future danger cannot be assumed. (D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, Professional Development Bureau, Final Notice of Adverse Action to Officer Colin R. Braddock, Adverse Action Case No. IS-23-002841, UFRB Case No. 23-UF-0117 (May 22, 2024) (final_notice_adverse_action_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 2 ¶ 12; D.C. Mun. Regs. tit. 6-B, § 1606 (2017) (dcmr_title6b_1606.pdf), PDF p. 1, § 1600.2(g); United Paperworkers Int’l Union v. Misco, Inc., 484 U.S. 29 (1987) (paperworkers_v_misco.pdf), printed pp. 43–45, ¶¶ 1–3.)

**Assessment:** MPD can make a forceful policy argument from the Award’s explicit findings and the reinstatement order, but its legal footing is poor. The record’s best direct rule is departmental, the attached regulatory policy excludes Braddock, and the Award does not compel future unlawful conduct. The public-policy exception is therefore materially less viable than the contrary-to-law theory.

## Citation convention

The Award, PERB Decision, Final Notice, comparator memorandum, Code, and regulation have numbered paragraphs or subsections, which are cited as printed. Douglas, *W.R. Grace*, *Misco*, and *FOP/Department of Corrections* do not use numbered paragraphs throughout; for those authorities, paragraph numbers are assigned sequentially within the cited page, or within the cited section where a section is specified, and the opening words are supplied as an additional locator. Page references to Douglas, *Grace*, and *Misco* are the printed reporter pages; PDF page numbers are added where useful.

**Record correction:** PERB’s procedural summary says the arbitrator imposed a 40-day suspension, but the Award orders a 45-day suspension. The operative award is 45 days; the discrepancy does not change the statutory analysis. (Opinion No. 1904, PERB Case No. 25-A-09 (D.C. Pub. Emp. Rels. Bd. Nov. 6, 2025) (perb_decision_braddock.pdf), PDF p. 1 ¶ 1; In re Arbitration Between Fraternal Ord. of Police/Metro. Police Dep’t Lab. Comm. (Braddock Grievance) & D.C. Metro. Police Dep’t, FMCS Case No. 24-51267, Grievance No. 24-0392 (Mar. 14, 2025) (Larrabee, Arb.) (braddock_arbitration_award.pdf), PDF pp. 2–3 ¶¶ 9–10.)
