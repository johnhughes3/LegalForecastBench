\section{Introduction}
Reinforcement learning (RL) techniques have led to significant recent gains in the reasoning abilities of frontier AI models, but the largest and most significant gains appear to have come in verifiable domains. RL training environments specify what a model can observe and do, and which outcomes receive reward. In \emph{reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards} (RLVR), correctness checks (like mathematical answer matching, executable tests, or formal proof checking) supply a scalable training signal that does not require a human to assess every model response \citep{deepseek,rlve}. Earlier self-play systems demonstrated the power of outcome feedback in games with explicit rules \citep{alphazero}.

The efficacy of these techniques in verifiable domains has been exceptional. During reinforcement learning, DeepSeek-R1-Zero's average pass@1 on AIME 2024 rose from 15.6\% to 77.9\%; the final DeepSeek-R1 system, which used a broader training pipeline, reached the 96.3rd percentile on Codeforces \citep{deepseek}. AlphaProof coupled reinforcement learning with Lean's formal proof checker and proved three problems from the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad, including its hardest problem, solved by only five of 609 human contestants \citep{alphaproof}. And OpenAI's September 2026 announcement that it had solved the Navier--Stokes Millennium Prize Problem reportedly relied on an internal model developed through large-scale reinforcement learning, followed by Lean formalization and verification. \citep{navierstokesproof,navierstokesreport}.

Despite its economic importance, the legal domain has not seen similar capability gains in longer-horizon tasks that are not as susceptible to verifiable rewards. Models have increasingly saturated some legal benchmarks that focus on bounded tasks with objectively scored answers, reducing their usefulness for distinguishing frontier models. For example, the Multistate Bar Examination, administered to prospective lawyers, contains multiple-choice questions that test the application of well-settled legal principles to stylized facts against an answer key \citep{ncbe}. LegalBench, a benchmark consisting of 162 tasks published in August 2023, similarly focused on tasks with unambiguous answers \citep{legalbench}. But frontier models have moved well beyond these benchmarks. Models have achieved perfect scores on an MBE practice-question benchmark. \citep{stubenbergperfect}. And Vals AI describes LegalBench as ``largely saturated.'' \citep{valslegalbench}.

Researchers have tried at least two approaches to benchmark and train models on higher-level legal tasks: preference evaluation (comparing which of two outputs practicing attorneys prefer) and rubric-based reinforcement learning (grading model outputs against specified criteria to produce a reward). \citep{judgmentbench}. The rubrics as rewards approach has been studied in medicine and science \citep{rubricsrewards}, and leading vertical AI companies such as the Harvey AI Corporation report using the technique to train specialized legal models. \citep{harveyTraining,tenet}. Rather than using human-written demonstrations for supervised training \citep{instructgpt}, the rubric defines what the system is rewarded for doing. Separate LLMs then judge outputs against the rubric to produce a reward signal.

In practice, however, the efficacy of these techniques in the legal domain is unproven, and some reinforcement learning environments appear to be artificial in ways that are likely to limit their usefulness for making AI models better at real-world tasks. In the course of working on this research, I discovered a number of errors in Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark. I used frontier models (GPT-6-Sol and Claude Opus 5.5, each using their first-party coding harness) to review all the litigation task rubrics for similar errors; both models agreed that hundreds of rubrics contained errors. In some cases, the answer key was inaccurate (such that, if the benchmark tasks were used for training, they could train the models into giving incorrect answers). In other cases, the task specifications contradicted information provided in the environment, or the requested work product diverged from the task environment. These construct validity issues could mean that the score does not adequately represent the professional capability being evaluated (or, if used for training, that the reward is misspecified). \citep{jacobswallach}. Past research into reward-model overoptimization shows that optimizing a proxy does not necessarily improve the underlying objective \citep{amodei,gao}.

\textbf{LegalForecastBench} is an experiment in exploiting real-world litigation data as an evaluation metric (and potentially, later, as a training signal) for legal reasoning. The key insight motivating this approach is that legal tasks should be understood as lying on a spectrum of verifiability and specificity. At one end of the spectrum, some legal propositions are so objectively settled that they can give rise to simple and clear rules like those that LegalBench uses, for example, the rule that a car must stop before entering the intersection if a traffic light is red. Professor Hart describes such bright-line rules as having "a core of settled meaning" \citep{hart}. But even seemingly simple rules have "a penumbra of debatable cases in which words are neither obviously applicable nor obviously ruled out." \citep{hart,fuller}. And some legal questions, such as the meaning of constitutional provisions, may be heavily laden with value judgments or policy preferences and thus lack a clear and objective answer.

For lawyers and the legal system, litigation is the core mechanism for resolving uncertainty about the law and reducing disputes over meaning into binding law. For example, if a contract dispute arises between two sophisticated parties, each side's lawyer may advance arguments for why their preferred reading of the contract is the correct one. But if the dispute is submitted to a court, either in an action for breach or pursuant to the Declaratory Judgment Act, the court's final judgment resolves that issue once and for all and constitutes binding law amongst those parties.  \citep{declaratory}. Even an erroneous judgment is still binding law. \citep{moitie}. Litigation thus turns a disputed question into an observable, legally operative disposition. From the perspective of training AI models, a difficult-to-verify legal question becomes a verifiable disposition every time a court enters judgment on a matter.

That makes predicting litigation outcomes an essential skill for lawyers in a variety of high-level judgment tasks. Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., one of the most influential jurists in American history and a major precursor to American Legal Realism, famously wrote: "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law." \citep{holmes}. While there are many components of high-level legal advice, decomposing many high-level legal judgment tasks into their constituent elements reveals that predicting litigation outcomes is often a key sub-task even in cases that never go to court. For example, lawyers frequently advise on commercial disputes. Prior work establishes that, even for disputes that never go to a judge, the legal system provides a framework that constrains the parties' respective bargaining range. \citep{shadow}. In many disputes, each side's best alternative to a negotiated agreement is to take the dispute to court, so the likely litigated outcome often sets a floor (or ceiling) on what each side is willing to accept (or offer) out of court. Even when a lawyer is merely giving general advice or preparing a contract between two parties who do not expect litigation, a good lawyer anticipates and prepares for potential disputes. \citep{nylawyerskills,shadow}. The value of prediction therefore extends beyond forecasting cases that ultimately reach judgment.

For the initial run of LegalForecastBench, I used AI models to collect documents from 91 real cases in federal court that had written decisions on motions to dismiss. Models, with my oversight, identified the moving defendants and the claims they challenged, and labeled the judge's ruling based on whether it granted entirely a motion to dismiss a specific claim asserted against a defendant (or group of similarly situated defendants). AI models received the same briefing and docket that was before the judge (including the identity of the real parties and the name of the assigned judge, to the extent that information was on the docket prior to the judge's disposition) and were asked to predict the judge's ruling as to each claim-defendant unit. The headline metric is micro-Brier loss, a score for probabilistic forecasts. \citep{brier,gneiting}. GPT-6 Sol had the best observed scores, but this small, single-retained-run comparison is intended primarily to establish the framework for using real-world litigation data as an evaluation metric (and potentially a training signal). We separately tested a discriminative, non-autoregressive decision model (TypeSafe AI's Jev model), using LLM-generated summaries of the court records (in order to fit the task within Jev's small context window). Jev was not competitive with frontier models on this specific task.

Although this benchmark is merely evaluative, we are separately developing a training dataset intended to directly test the hypothesis that training models to predict litigation outcomes might give them skills that generalize to other legal judgment tasks. Just as training LLMs to predict the next token helped LLMs to understand latent facts about the world, in principle, teaching frontier models to predict litigation outcomes might help them understand the principles that drive real-world legal outcomes. Prior work shows that outcome-based forecasting can be a useful training signal, at least in helping models to forecast other events outside the training distribution. \citep{outcomerl,openforecaster,sentencing}. Whether improving models' ability to predict litigation outcomes transfers to other legal judgment tasks remains an empirical question.


\section{Related work}

The approach here is consistent with previous research that (i) used older AI techniques to build smaller, custom models for predicting the outcome of litigation (especially U.S. Supreme Court decisions), and (ii) more recent efforts to use frontier models to predict the outcomes of events defined in prediction markets.

\subsection{Legal outcome prediction}
Using AI models and other techniques to predict litigation outcomes has a long lineage. Ruger and colleagues used hand-coded metadata and statistical techniques to predict the 2002 Term of the U.S. Supreme Court \citep{ruger}. Later work incorporated more sophisticated machine-learning and natural language processing techniques. Aletras and colleagues in 2016 used a text-based approach to predict the outcomes of European Court of Human Rights decisions \citep{aletras}. Katz, Bommarito, and Blackman in 2017 used a random-forest classifier over structured Supreme Court data to predict Justice votes and case outcomes. \citep{katz}. CAIL2018 used a dataset of more than 2.6 million Chinese criminal cases to predict charges and sentences, with text-classification models providing initial baselines. \citep{cail}. As the transformer architecture became dominant, later legal prediction systems increasingly used models like BERT and T5. CLC-UKET, released in 2024, compared fine-tuned BERT and T5 models to zero- and few-shot GPT-3.5 and GPT-5 to predict U.K. Employment Tribunal cases. \citep{clcuket}. The field has thus progressed from statistical models over hand-coded metadata through conventional MLP and supervised machine learning to transformer-based classifiers and, more recently, large language models. Much of this literature differs from the approach studied here, because the models operate on structured metadata or facts and claims extracted from judicial opinions. The approach here simply asks frontier models to reason about the actual pre-decision litigation record, essentially the documents filed in court before the judge who decided the motion, without any access to statistical data or models showing prior decisions.

\subsection{Legal AI evaluation}
One of the most impressive academic benchmarks is LegalBench, a collaborative benchmark to which 40 authors contributed 162 tasks that aimed to assess legal domain expertise. Although some of the legal issues tested could give rise to disputes in real litigation (for example, there are tests of whether a given statement is hearsay or not), the benchmark deliberately uses relatively clean examples that can be reduced to a binary answer. \citep{legalbench}. The hearsay tasks, for example, explicitly articulate what proposition a given statement is being used to prove or disprove, and then ask whether that use is hearsay; in real-world litigation, what proposition the statement is being offered to prove may itself be disputed so providing a clean prompt on that point narrows the task to a relatively mechanical application of an explicit rule.

Most recent benchmarks test higher-level judgment tasks that lack an objective ground truth. JudgmentBench, released in May 2026, and Harvey's LAB targeted open-ended professional legal work, like drafting, analysis, and advice, for which there are potentially multiple defensible outputs and no independently verifiable ground truth. \citep{harveyLAB,judgmentbench}. These benchmarks take different approaches to reward specification. JudgmentBench explicitly compared rubric-based scoring to comparative judgment (pairwise preference judgments collected from practicing attorneys), and found that comparative judgments recover the intended quality ordering substantially better than rubrics. LAB relies on a rubric-based approach. Preference-based evaluation is a form of RLHF that can amplify human biases and errors into the rewards signal \citep{sharmaSycophancy,shapiraSycophancy}; for example, reviewers could reward briefs that are longer, sycophantic, overly confident, or more consistent with the reviewer's own judicial philosophy or political preferences, rather than rewarding briefs that are most effective at persuading a judge.

\subsection{Verifiable rewards and forecasting}
Frontier models are trained on several different forms of feedback, as described in prior work. InstructGPT in 2022 provided an influential demonstration of reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{instructgpt}. In 2023, Lightman and colleagues showed that, on mathematical reasoning tasks, process supervision (assigning feedback to individual reasoning steps) outperformed outcome supervision, which gives rewards based on whether the final answer is correct \citep{lightman}.

More recent work has used externally resolved real-world events to supply an analogous signal outside domains like mathematics. For example, Autocast, released in 2022, assembled forecasting questions from tournaments and a date-indexed news corpus to allow language models to make predictions based on the information a human would have had at the time. \citep{autocast}. Halawi and colleagues in 2024 used a retrieval-augmented forecasting system that could search for information, and generate and aggregate multiple forecasts; the system approached the performance of competitive human forecasters \citep{halawi}. ForecastBench, introduced in 2024, extended this approach into a dynamic benchmark that continually collects questions about unresolved future events and scores models once the event outcome is known \citep{forecastbench}.

Outcome-Based Reinforcement Learning to Predict the Future showed in 2025 that resolved binary forecasting questions could serve as reinforcement-learning rewards, training a 14-billion-parameter model on more than 100,000 events using adaptations of GRPO and ReMax \citep{outcomerl}. OpenForecaster similarly explored training language models directly on resolved forecasting questions \citep{openforecaster}. Some research in China has also used frontier models to predict criminal sentences \citep{sentencing}. LegalForecastBench is an effort to apply a similar approach to litigation outcomes, at least as an evaluation metric for now.

\subsection{Benchmark validity and specification}
The correct scoring rules are those that encourage honest probabilities in expectation \citep{brier,gneiting}. Statistical evaluation also must respect dependence among questions sharing a record \citep{miller}. OpenAI's published work on SWE-bench Verified also emphasizes the importance of avoiding specification error (which is relevant to the findings about rubric-based benchmarks, such as the preliminary audit of LAB). After publishing that benchmark in August 2024, OpenAI noted concerns in February 2026 that the benchmark no longer measures the coding capabilities of frontier models because the tests reject correct solutions and models were increasingly contaminated by having seen the open-source repositories often used for training purposes \citep{swebench,swecritique}. These specification problems apply both to rubric-based approaches and to our own outcome labels.

\section{LegalForecastBench: Dataset and task}
\subsection{Prediction target and information set}
Let $c\in\{1,\ldots,C\}$ index cases and $u\in\mathcal U_c$ index scored units within case $c$. Each unit identifies a challenged claim or a separately resolvable pleaded subclaim against a moving defendant (or a group of similarly situated defendants, for whom the outcome is predicted together since the legal and factual arguments for dismissal are materially identical as to all of them). We used models to identify appropriate units based on the arguments articulated in each motion; an affirmative defense, an element of a legal cause of action, a requested remedy, or a distinct factual theory was not automatically treated as a separate unit. Rather, units generally tracked claims asserted in the complaint (in some cases with a single claim asserted against multiple defendants being treated as different units, if different defendants advanced different grounds for dismissal). The model receives the pre-decision record that the judge received $X_c$ and the unit specification $U_{cu}$ and returns
\begin{equation}
 p_{cu}\in[0,1],\qquad p_{cu}\approx \Pr(Y_{cu}=1\mid X_c,U_{cu}),
\end{equation}
where $Y_{cu}=1$ means that the judge dismissed that claim-defendant unit in its entirety, in the target motion's first written disposition. That event is not a final victory in the lawsuit, since a plaintiff might be given an opportunity to file an amended complaint with additional allegations supporting a dismissed claim, other claims might survive even if some are dismissed, and the plaintiff normally has a right to appeal dismissal and seek a de novo adjudication of the issue from the circuit court of appeals.

A full dismissal with leave to amend still has label 1 (i.e., a dismissal is coded the same way, even if it is without prejudice and the plaintiff may seek leave to amend the complaint and try again). A partial dismissal of some claims or theories that leaves the frozen unit alive in a material respect has label 0, meaning the claim-defendant unit was not entirely dismissed. Later amendment, settlement, reconsideration, and appellate reversal do not change the target; the model is specifically asked to predict only the trial judge's ruling on the specific motion to dismiss. Dispositions that cannot be reliably mapped to a unit were excluded from primary scoring. These choices sacrifice some procedural detail for a stable, objectively interpretable forecast event. Even with these limitations, label construction is a substantive legal task in its own right as the labeling models had to analyze the filed motions to determine what claims were being challenged and ascertain from the judge's ruling how those were resolved. Two labeling models had to agree on the units, and had the option to escalate ambiguous cases to me for review; in practice, the labeling process was iterative in this preliminary run, as we discovered a number of edge cases that needed to be resolved. The process started with 100 cases but seven were eliminated during the labeling process because the order did not decide the frozen claims, one was eliminated because it was a stipulated dismissal, and one was a plaintiffs' Rule 12(c) motion for judgment on the pleadings rather than a defendant's motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b).

\subsection{Cohort construction}
The analyzed set consists of 91 U.S. federal cases with written decisions dated June 30--August 7, 2026. The cases, as labeled, contained 409 claim-defendant units that could be forecasted. Of those, 387 are scored. Of the scored claim-defendant units, 285 were fully dismissed and 102 were not. The shared-summary preparation inventory contains 482 selected documents. The sample was not random; our pipeline favored cases where some of the necessary documents could be obtained from online databases, although we typically had to purchase a number of documents from the federal PACER system to ensure that the task included all the substantive filings on the motion that were before the judge.

\subsection{Unitization and outcome labeling}
Of the 409 claim-defendant units, 387 are scored; 22 units therefore do not enter the headline metrics. The unscored units involved situations where the plaintiff voluntarily withdrew the frozen claim, the order did not cleanly grant or deny the frozen claim, or (for four units) we determined after freezing the unit that the frozen defendant was the wrong target or the motion did not address the frozen claim.

The case construction and labeling pipeline used models as well as human legal oversight from me. We did take pains to maintain separation between \emph{unitization} and \emph{outcome coding}. In particular, we wished to avoid the possibility that the prediction units could leak information about the judge's ultimate outcome to the models that were performing prediction. AI models first were asked to identify the claim-defendant units that a motion sought to dismiss, based only on the pre-decision materials (the motion and the docket). A later stage was given access to the claim-defendant units and the judge's order disposing of the motion, to interpret the disposition for each frozen target. The labeling pipeline we used requires structured outcome agreement and supporting disposition excerpts for automatic acceptance; disagreements between models, ambiguity, and structural defects in the cases were routed to me for lawyer review and a decision. I resolved disputed units and corrected labels as necessary. For purposes of the initial release, this process was iterative and we did not track a blinded human-audit error estimate.

\subsection{Temporal isolation and contamination}
The models predicting outcomes were not given access to the target decision or the outcome. The construction pipeline also screens docket and filing text to avoid outcome leakage. Models had access to document text tools in a sandbox with no Internet access. Model API calls were made with provider-side web search tools disabled where applicable.

The decisions used in the benchmark were handed down after the reported knowledge or training cutoffs for fifteen of the seventeen tested models. Kimi and Muse did not publish training cutoffs, but based on the model release dates, it appears unlikely that those models were exposed to the decisions being tested in training.

The acquisition and labeling pipeline is designed to create new cohorts in the future, when new decisions become available. This enables fresh comparisons even after models might have been trained on the decisions at issue here. However, the downside is that refreshing a benchmark changes its case mix and difficulty; scores from different versions cannot be interpreted as a time series of model improvement.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\subsection{Model execution}
The main comparison uses a common document-access interface. Models can read documents using tools and produce a probability for each unit, together with a short rationale. Provider-specific reasoning settings are retained in the run registries. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} lists the configuration settings. The study population is the seventeen configurations in Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\subsection{Metrics}
For $N=\sum_c |\mathcal U_c|=387$ and $C=91$, the primary metric is the micro-Brier loss
\begin{equation}
 B_{\mathrm{micro}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{c=1}^C\sum_{u\in\mathcal U_c}(p_{cu}-Y_{cu})^2.
\label{eq:micro}
\end{equation}
The equal-case score changes the estimand by giving each case the same weight:
\begin{equation}
 B_{\mathrm{case}}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^C\frac{1}{|\mathcal U_c|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal U_c}(p_{cu}-Y_{cu})^2.
\label{eq:case}
\end{equation}
Lower is better for both. Micro-Brier treats each scored claim--defendant unit equally, so cases that involve a motion to dismiss many separate claims receive more weight than cases where only one claim and defendant was subject to a motion to dismiss. Equal-case Brier weights each prediction unit in inverse proportion to the number of units from that case.

The Brier rule is strictly proper for the binary target. If the true conditional event probability is $q$, then
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E[(p-Y)^2\mid X,U]=(p-q)^2+q(1-q),
\label{eq:proper}
\end{equation}
which is minimized by reporting $p=q$. The second term is irreducible uncertainty for that information set. A set of observable judicial events therefore create a verifiable score across the entire probability distribution, even though a single realized outcome standing alone does not reveal whether the individual probability a model assigned to that outcome was correct ex ante \citep{brier,gneiting}.

We report accuracy at threshold $p\geq0.5$ as a secondary descriptive metric; in other words, accuracy percentage is simply the percentage of units where the model assigned a 50% or greater probability to the correct outcome.

The observed dismissal frequency is $\widehat\pi=285/387=0.73643$. Always predicting dismissal has accuracy 73.64\%, so a constant forecast $p=\widehat\pi$ has micro-Brier $\widehat\pi(1-\widehat\pi)=0.19410$. The constant is fitted retrospectively on this cohort, so it is a descriptive reference, not a baseline derived from earlier cases. The uninformative forecast $p=0.5$ has Brier 0.25.

Log loss is a complementary diagnostic:
\begin{equation}
 L=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{c,u}\left[Y_{cu}\log p_{cu}+(1-Y_{cu})\log(1-p_{cu})\right].
\end{equation}
For a wrong forecast assigning 0.99 to the predicted class, Brier loss is 0.9801 and log loss is approximately 4.605; at confidence 0.90 the corresponding losses are 0.81 and 2.303. Thus log loss imposes a large penalty on extreme errors, specifically, instances where the model assigned a very high probability to the outcome that did not occur.

\subsection{Statistical dependence and uncertainty}
Claim--defendant units from the same case share facts and legal issues; distinct claims in a single case may suffer from closely related legal defects, so the outcomes are not independent. We therefore measure within-case dependence using an observed-scale intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) \citep{makicc}.

% BEGIN GENERATED CLUSTERING
Across the 387 scored units in 91 cases, the observed-scale outcome ICC was 0.76 (95\% CI: 0.61--0.87). Prediction losses also clustered within case because if a model failed to account, for example, for the possibility that a given motion might be denied on procedural grounds, all of the claims in a case that it expected to be dismissed could survive: GPT-6 Sol's Brier-loss ICC was 0.42 (95\% CI: 0.24--0.58). These intervals use 20,000 whole-case bootstrap resamples, and assume independence between cases.
% END GENERATED CLUSTERING

The observed dependence supports resampling whole cases, with model predictions kept paired, when estimating uncertainty in score differences \citep{cameronmiller}. The seventeen-configuration table is descriptive and does not claim a significance ordering. It does not measure variation across repeated model runs \citep{miller}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Frontier models substantially outperform unconditional references}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the seventeen configurations on the ranked leaderboard. GPT-6 Sol achieves the best observed micro-Brier (0.11763), equal-case Brier (0.12949), and accuracy (327/387, 84.50\%). All seventeen beat the retrospectively fitted constant micro-Brier of 0.19410; GPT-6 Sol's micro-Brier is lower than that constant by about 39.4\%.

Sixteen configurations also beat majority-class accuracy (the 73.64\% accuracy of predicting dismissal for every unit). Muse Spark 1.3 does not: 279/387 correct forecasts is 72.09\%. Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview has the highest micro-Brier in the panel (0.18345) and still beats that accuracy baseline (302/387, 78.04\%).

\subsection{Confidence and calibration}
Define confidence $h_{cu}=\max(p_{cu},1-p_{cu})$ and the high-confidence subset $H_m=\{(c,u):h_{cu}\geq0.9\}$ for configuration $m$. Comparing the mean confidence in $H_m$ with classification accuracy in $H_m$ gives a useful conditional calibration diagnostic. Of course, this is not a full reliability curve; the subset differs by model, and its units remain clustered by case.

OpenAI models appeared more likely than others to make high-confidence forecasts. GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Luna, and Astra make high-confidence forecasts on 58.9\%, 47.0\%, and 53.5\% of units respectively, compared with 20.9\% for Fable 5.1. Their mean confidence in those subsets is approximately 95--97\%, while realized accuracy is approximately 89--90\%, suggesting some overconfidence, particularly for Astra, which missed 21 of 207 such predictions. Gemini 3.8 Flash also had relatively high sharpness, making 276 high-confidence forecasts (71.3\% of units) and missing 35. Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview is sharper still, making 320 high-confidence forecasts and missing 57. GPT-6 Sol provides a useful counterexample within OpenAI, missing 8 of 169 high-confidence forecasts, for 95.3\% subset accuracy. GPT-6.1 Sol is sharper and less accurate in that subset, missing 18 of 199. GPT-6 Luna misses 19 of 183. Claude Opus 5.5 misses none of 65.

\subsection{Inference cost}
The normalized successful-workload costs range from \$0.62 for GPT-6 Luna to \$83.18 for Fable. These are not actual invoice charges. For example, Fable was initially run without caching, resulting in an actual cost of \$189.70. The normalized price was reconstructed after the fact using turn-by-turn token counts, with the reconstruction validated against real billing data from other Anthropic model runs. In several other cases, provider discounts that we received were normalized to standard API rates.
On these standardized estimates, GPT-6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Luna, and GPT-6 Sol form the observed micro-Brier cost--score frontier.

\subsection{Shared-summary experiment: Jev and Luna}
Jev is described by its developer as a probabilistic ``System One'' model, exposing native Boolean probabilities rather than generating text or using tools agentically as frontier models do \citep{jevdocs}. Because Jev's context window and lack of agentic tool calling differ from the document-enabled configurations used with frontier models, Jev was evaluated as a separate experiment.

Jev and two Luna comparator configurations then received the same frozen summary and unit questions in a single request per case, without document tools or follow-up turns. Jev was not well-calibrated for this task. Jev's micro-Brier of 0.36754 is worse than the 0.25 loss of always reporting $p=0.5$. Its mean predicted dismissal probability was approximately 0.305, while the observed frequency in this data set was 0.736. This resulted in severe underprediction in the aggregate. Its 32.6\% threshold accuracy is also below majority-class prediction.

We tested Luna using the same summaries that Jev received in order to determine how much of Jev's underperformance was attributable to receiving only summaries of the litigation documents, rather than the full documents that we used in our primary evaluation. However, Luna performed much better than Jev even on this comparison. GPT-5.6 Luna with the identical summaries achieves micro-Brier 0.15489 and accuracy 78.6\%. The paired case-cluster bootstrap gives a Luna-minus-Jev difference of $-0.21265$, with a 99.44\% Bonferroni-adjusted interval (95\% familywise coverage) of $[-0.32644,-0.06433]$ across the three model pairs and three metrics. The result remains exploratory because potential cross-case dependence was not assessed.

The shared-summary control shows that the summaries contain useful predictive signal that the Jev configuration does not exploit. Of course, that does not indicate that every important detail survived the summarization process. Luna performed better in the primary evaluation, where it had full document access, than it did in this comparison, with only summaries. That could mean either that the summaries lost some relevant signal, or that the interactive environment (where Luna could choose which documents to read) allowed better performance, or some mix of the two.

Although this experiment indicates that Jev is inferior to frontier models on this particular task, it does not establish that probabilistic or non-generative architectures are unsuitable for litigation prediction or that they will necessarily underperform. It is possible that Jev was not exposed to sufficient training data for this task, or that its architecture or interface was not well-suited to the task, or some combination of the three.

\section{Prediction is a valuable benchmarking and training target, because it is critical to many high-level legal judgment tasks}
\label{sec:strategy}
\subsection{Judicial dispositions supply an objective ground truth, because court rulings have the force of law, whether or not they are correct}

Litigation converts a messy legal dispute as to which reasonable minds can differ into a concrete and fixed disposition. Courts prize finality, and final judgments are explicitly written with the goal of achieving a clear and objective disposition of the dispute between the parties that leaves no room for further debate or relitigation of the issues the court has resolved. A final judgment on the merits, with appeals exhausted, has preclusive effect and the force of law, even if a litigant could show that the judgment was based on a legal error \citep{moitie}.

While LegalForecastBench does not directly test models' ability to predict the court's final judgment (instead, testing only their ability to predict the first written disposition of a specified motion), that first-level prediction task is a good initial target because many more cases reach the motion to dismiss stage than the final judgment stage (a litigated final judgment is comparatively rare). And the outcome of the motion to dismiss itself is of critical importance in many disputes and potentially the most economically consequential single stage of litigation. In securities fraud class actions, for example (a specialty that results in billions of dollars in settlements paid in the United States every year), the motion to dismiss effectively determines whether the defendant will ultimately have to make a settlement payment to resolve the case or not. While slightly more than half of the filed cases are dismissed before trial, of those cases that are not, almost all end in settlement. (In the past 18 years of filings, for example, only one securities fraud defendant elected to proceed to trial, Tesla, Inc.) [[cite  https://securities.stanford.edu/litigation.html?filter=CASE%20REACHED%20TRIAL%20VERDICT]]

\subsection{Litigation forecasts are a key subtask essential to high-level legal strategic advice and decision-making}

Litigation prediction is a key subcomponent of legal work, even when litigation is not at all contemplated or likely. While there are many other tasks involved in giving strategic advice to a client (like understanding the client's objectives), outcome prediction is a key subtask, consistent with the MacCrate skills framework and the New York State Judicial Institute's account of counseling and negotiation \citep{nylawyerskills}. Past research shows that even parties who do not intend to litigate with each other bargain "in the shadow of the law" \citep{shadow}. When parties are negotiating, their best alternative to a negotiated agreement is often to litigate. As a result, the parties' predictions of how a court will resolve their dispute constrain the bargaining range; neither party is likely to accept an agreement that is less favorable than the result they believe they could obtain in court (after taking into account the costs and risks of litigation).

A simple risk-neutral model illustrates this \citep{shavell}. Suppose a plaintiff would receive a fixed award $J>0$ if successful and zero otherwise. Let $q_P$ and $q_D$ be the plaintiff's and defendant's respective estimates of the probability of that success, and let $k_P,k_D\geq0$ be their remaining litigation costs. Assuming each side bears its own costs, ignoring settlement costs, and restricting attention to monetary payoffs, the plaintiff's expected litigation payoff is $q_PJ-k_P$, while the defendant's expected trial cost is $q_DJ+k_D$. A payment $s$ from defendant to plaintiff is weakly acceptable to both when
\begin{equation}
 q_PJ-k_P\;\leq\;s\;\leq\;q_DJ+k_D.
 \label{eq:settlement-range}
\end{equation}
The interval is nonempty when
\begin{equation}
 (q_P-q_D)J\;\leq\;k_P+k_D.
\end{equation}
This is an illustrative reservation-value calculation \citep{priestklein,settlement}. Where the parties agree about the probability of success, they can enjoy a surplus from settling the case, equal to the avoided costs. The possibility of settlement disappears when the plaintiff's estimate of success exceeds the defendant's estimate of the plaintiff's success by enough that $(q_P-q_D)J>k_P+k_D$. Even when some interval exists where both parties believe they are better off settling, private information or game theoretic considerations/strategic behavior might prevent agreement \citep{settlement}. Nonmonetary goals, risk aversion, fee shifting, and uncertainty around the actual damages that would be awarded at trial are additional complications that would require a more complex model. Nevertheless, this simple example illustrates how forecasting litigation outcomes can impact even out-of-court negotiations in a broad swath of commercial disputes or transactions.

Notably, better AI forecasts could yield more surplus to litigants, systematically, if such forecasts help parties' expectations to converge; simply put, if an AI system becomes sufficiently accurate at predicting litigation outcomes that parties are willing to adjust their expectations based on its output, then the parties are more likely to agree on the likely outcome of a trial. Where $q_P>q_D$, narrowing the gap between their estimates reduces $(q_P-q_D)J$, making the settlement condition easier to satisfy and potentially creating a mutually acceptable payment where none previously existed. If the parties adopt a common forecast, $q_P=q_D$, the settlement interval has width $k_P+k_D$: the full amount of the avoided litigation costs. While modern litigation is much better than many historical options used to resolve disputes (such as feud, self-help, trial by ordeal, etc.), reaching a mutually acceptable agreement is virtually always preferable to the costs, risks, and distraction brought on by protracted, adversarial litigation. Improved forecasting of outcomes can thus help parties more realistically understand the weaknesses and risks of their case, and increase the likelihood of settlement early in the matter.

\subsection{Prediction is a subcomponent of legal drafting tasks}

One major limitation of LegalForecastBench, relative to alternatives, is that it focuses primarily on the models' analytical ability and does not directly evaluate their ability to prepare legal work product. But litigation prediction is at least a subcomponent of many drafting tasks, and could improve models' skills.

A basic principle observed in the legal profession is that lawyers must confine their argument to the strongest points, not raise every possible point, and generally should put the strongest arguments first in the brief, moving on to points that are less likely to be dispositive later. In \emph{Jones v. Barnes}, the Supreme Court expressly recognized the value of selecting stronger appellate arguments and the risk that adding weaker ones can dilute them \citep{jonesbarnes}. In order to prepare good work product, then, models need to distinguish the potentially dispositive issues from objections or points that are technically valid but less likely to drive the ultimate outcome. 

\section{Harvey's LAB: How rewards from human-specified rubrics compare with externally realized outcome signals}
\subsection{Why the reward definition matters}

In the absence of an external event that can resolve a target and provide reward, evaluation generally must substitute a human-specified proxy. JudgmentBench compared two alternative approaches: human preference evaluation and human-specified rubrics. [[cite]] Although JudgmentBench suggested that preference evaluation recovered the intended quality ordering better with less annotation time than rubrics, the most frequently cited legal benchmark today, Harvey's LAB, uses rubric-based scoring (with LLMs as judges). Launched on May 6, 2026, LAB contains 1,200 tasks spanning 24 practice areas and more than 75,000 criteria \citep{harveyLAB,harveyInitial,labAA}. LAB-style tasks have been used for evaluation as well as for training. Harvey and Applied Compute undertook full-parameter reinforcement learning of a legal agent using rubric-based rewards \citep{harveyTraining}. And in August 2026, Harvey announced it was creating its own model (Tenet), using Kimi K3 as a base, that achieved gains on LAB and other legal benchmarks \citep{tenet}. 

One major advantage of rubric-based approaches like LAB is that they can help score 



Rubrics are useful when they encode meaningful requirements and are reliably graded. The risk is a mismatch among the requested work, the record supplied, the legal answer prescribed by a criterion, and the grader's application of that criterion. These are distinct failure modes. JudgmentBench's comparison of expert rubric scores and pairwise preferences on 30 BigLaw Bench tasks provides related empirical motivation, although its constructed quality levels and task selection limit generalization \citep{judgmentbench}.

The evaluation question is one of construct validity: whether the measurements adequately represent the capability the benchmark claims to assess \citep{jacobswallach}. If the same criteria supply training rewards, a defective criterion also raises a question of reward misspecification. Reward hacking is a possible learned response to such a specification, and reward-model overoptimization is a possible divergence between an improving proxy score and the underlying target. These diagnoses require different evidence \citep{amodei,gao}. Gao and colleagues study this divergence using a synthetic gold reward model; our audit examines task and criterion validity and does not measure a training trajectory. Outcome scores also have a limited target: they reward accurate forecasts, without directly rewarding good advice or faithful explanations.

\subsection{Preliminary audit and a targeted task review}
Our retained AI-assisted review covers the pinned litigation-and-dispute-resolution subset: 52 tasks and 2,858 criteria. GPT-6 Sol flagged 616 criteria; Claude Opus 5.5's final reconciliation flagged 564. Their union contains 835 flagged criteria, leaving 2,023 unflagged by either final report. Of 345 overlapping flags, both reviewers called 83 problematic and 135 arguable; 127 received different levels of concern. The union represents 29.2\% of the 2,858 criteria; the other 70.8\% were not flagged, which does not establish that they are correct. These are review outputs, not a verified error rate. Opus's reconciliation saw Sol's findings, so agreement in the final reports is not independent confirmation \citep{labaudit}.

The author's targeted review of \texttt{analyze-counterparty-motion-to-dismiss} motivates a narrower case study (Appendix~\ref{app:lab}). Among its 34 criteria, the AI reviewers flagged 16. Source inspection supports concrete concerns: a categorical integration-clause proposition despite express non-reliance language; a required characterization of accrual as factually disputed despite dated allegations requiring careful analysis; and output requirements, including a minimum number of recommendations, that the user-facing instruction does not specify \citep{labtask}. These examples do not justify treating every flagged criterion as wrong, or treating every solver failure as a grading defect.

The task also illustrates why a realism critique must be precise. The complaint quotes a Texas forum-selection clause while filing in Georgia, but it also advances venue and scope arguments. That is a contestable litigation position, not proof that real litigants never behave this way. Similarly, the inspected motion contains a caption, table of contents, and table of authorities. Programmatic document metadata establishes templating, not LLM authorship. The strongest criticism concerns how the rubric evaluates the supplied legal problem, rather than inferring professional invalidity from the documents' appearance.

\subsection{Implications for evaluation and training}
All-pass aggregation can magnify one defective criterion: a useful work product fails if any required item fails. This does not make all-pass evaluation inherently inappropriate; some legal requirements are indispensable. It does mean that criteria should distinguish dispositive analysis, necessary completeness, and optional presentation. A benchmark should permit a well-supported rejection of a planted argument when the record or law makes that argument weak.

OpenAI's 2026 analysis of SWE-bench Verified makes an analogous concern concrete: in a selected audit of 138 difficult tasks, it reported problems involving test requirements and task specification \citep{swecritique}. That finding supports examining task--test alignment; it provides no defect estimate for LAB. Neither the preliminary LAB audit nor the present forecasting results establish that rubric-based RL is ineffective, that synthetic tasks generally harm performance, or that training on litigation tasks caused degradation. A stronger experiment would compare matched environments built from synthetic and authentic records, independently adjudicate their criteria, and evaluate transfer to held-out professional tasks.

There is also evidence that rubric-based training can improve performance under a different evaluation format. Gunjal and colleagues report gains on GPQA-Diamond, a multiple-choice science benchmark, relative to direct Likert-based judge rewards \citep{rubricsrewards}. This result does not establish transfer to legal tasks, but it cautions against assuming that rubric rewards merely teach compliance with the training rubric.

Real litigation is valuable because it supplies naturally occurring arguments, procedural complexity, and an outcome that the benchmark designer did not choose. It still requires expert construction. Authentic records can support both outcome scoring and carefully validated rubrics. The research opportunity is to test which supervision signals teach useful judgment, rather than assuming that either the court's outcome or an expert checklist is a complete measure of legal competence.

\paragraph{Industry use and the limits of that evidence.} Harvey's product announcements report LAB all-pass results for Opus 4.8 (10.4\%, May 28), Fable 5 (13.3\%, June 9), and Sonnet 5 (5.8\%, June 30) \citep{opusAnnouncement,fableAnnouncement,sonnetAnnouncement}. These examples show repeated use in model evaluation and product positioning. They are Harvey's evaluations of third-party models, not evidence that each model developer independently adopted LAB as its definitive legal benchmark. The LAB-AA partnership broadens distribution, but neither prominence nor a commercial partnership establishes evaluation validity.


\section{Outcome feedback and LegalForecastGym}
LegalForecastGym implements a separate, single-step forecasting environment intended for post-training experiments. It renders a static litigation packet and receives a structured action containing a dismissal probability and cited evidence spans. Training loaders admit only explicitly designated training collections and reject locked test matters. Episode assembly keeps the label out of the observation. The current phase does not implement a multi-turn litigation simulator or train an agent to take procedural actions.

For a valid forecast, the implemented Brier-shaped reward is
\begin{equation}
 R(p,Y)=0.02+0.98\bigl[1-(p-Y)^2\bigr],
\end{equation}
while invalid output receives zero. The positive affine transformation preserves the proper-score optimum among valid forecasts. The implementation also supplies threshold-based and clipped-log alternatives. Evidence spans are logged rather than rewarded. This design separates a directly scored prediction from an assertion that the accompanying reasoning is legally correct.

The checked Gym artifacts contain no completed parameter-update experiment or held-out before/after training comparison. They do contain a live sampling comparison on 50 synthetic development episodes, used to assess output format and select an open-weight sampling model. That run is infrastructure evidence, not evidence of legal forecasting improvement. A separately recorded Gym export of 100 matters and 425 scoreable units is also distinct from the 91-case, 387-unit benchmark analyzed here; those populations are not pooled.

The intended learning study asks whether earlier outcome feedback improves predictions on later, disjoint matters and whether any gains extend beyond forecasting. Parameter learning, in-context feedback, and changes to the surrounding agent system are different interventions. Each needs a matched control and a held-out endpoint. Forecasting literature makes such learning plausible, and OpenForecaster reports calibration transfer to other question-answering benchmarks; those findings do not establish improved litigation strategy \citep{outcomerl,openforecaster}. Appendix~\ref{app:study} specifies a concrete prospective design.



A June 2026 Harvey industry article characterizes litigation as slower to adopt agents than some volume-driven workflows; this is a vendor observation, not a measured cross-practice adoption study \citep{litigationAdoption}. 

\section{Limitations and generalization}
\paragraph{Selection into adjudication.} The target population is a set of accessible cases with written motion decisions, not all disputes, all filed cases, or all contemplated motions. Parties settle, abandon claims, amend pleadings, or choose not to sue. Cases reaching a published disposition may differ in uncertainty, stakes, representation, and bargaining conditions. Calibration conditional on reaching this dataset does not establish calibration for counterfactual outcomes in the broader dispute population \citep{priestklein}. At the same time, selection does not prove that learned skills cannot transfer: that is an empirical question requiring an independently defined evaluation population.

\paragraph{Prediction is not legal correctness.} Judicial outcomes reflect doctrine and record facts, but also procedure, judicial behavior, and institutional context. Judge identity remains visible. We have not established how much performance comes from close legal analysis rather than judge, court, party, or case-type priors. A metadata-only comparison, judge masking, and a human forecasting baseline are important future tests. Neither fluent rationales nor a correct forecast demonstrate valid legal reasoning. Training to imitate outcomes can reproduce historical disparities or errors; professional advice also has obligations that prediction scores do not capture.

\paragraph{Labels and completeness.} Unitization can omit, merge, or split legally distinct targets; dispositions can be ambiguous or procedural. Human oversight helps but does not quantify residual error. The absence of a complete blinded label audit and acquisition/exclusion census limits claims about both label accuracy and representativeness. Excluding 22 unscored units preserves an interpretable target but may also change difficulty. These limitations deserve the same scrutiny as disputed rubrics in another benchmark.

\paragraph{Statistical and execution limits.} One successful prediction per unit does not characterize run-to-run variance, operational reliability, or expected total cost. The cohort is small and imbalanced. Case clustering does not resolve unverified dependence among related cases. Reported scores compare model--harness--provider configurations, not isolated model weights.

\paragraph{Temporal and transfer limits.} A provider-reported cutoff and disabled browsing reduce specific leakage risks without proving contamination absent. New benchmark versions change the case distribution. Generalization to other jurisdictions, procedural stages, settlement decisions, drafting, or strategic advice is not established by this study. Outcome feedback may improve a narrow probability estimate without improving explanations or real client decisions.

\section{Conclusion}
LegalForecastBench operationalizes an uncertain but consequential legal judgment as a probabilistic forecast of a later judicial event. In the beta, all seventeen document-enabled configurations outperform a cohort-fitted constant in micro-Brier, while confidence behavior and model ordering differ in informative ways. GPT-6 Sol has the strongest observed scores, and the shared-summary comparison exposes a large gap between Jev and Luna that cannot be explained by the summaries containing no signal.

The broader opportunity is to make legal evaluation more empirically grounded. Real outcomes provide an external scoring reference, but useful training signals still require careful task definition, information boundaries, and legal labeling. The next research question is whether feedback on such forecasts improves performance on later cases and transfers to independently assessed legal work. The present evidence motivates that question; it does not answer it.

\section{A bounded LAB motion-to-dismiss case study}
\label{app:lab}
This case study uses the task and supplied documents at the pinned upstream revision recorded by the local audit. The requested output is an opposition issues memorandum. The fictional dispute concerns a software-services agreement, alleged pre-contractual representations, implementation defects, and a motion asserting jurisdictional, venue, and pleading defenses. We distinguish direct record observations from legal interpretations and unverified AI flags.

\paragraph{Forum selection and litigation strategy.} Complaint paragraph 48 sets out a clause selecting state or federal courts in Travis County, Texas, while the action is filed in the Northern District of Georgia. Paragraphs 18--19 assert statutory venue in Georgia, and paragraph 123 argues that the statutory deceptive-practices claim falls outside the clause. Those arguments may be weak, but the complaint is not wholly silent about the forum issue. A rigorous task should ask whether transfer is likely, which claims the clause covers, and whether a procedural objection changes the client's position. Under \emph{Atlantic Marine}, a valid clause selecting another federal forum is generally enforced through section 1404(a), while a state or foreign forum implicates forum non conveniens \citep{atlantic}. Correcting an opponent's procedural vehicle is not the same as establishing that the chosen Georgia forum will retain the dispute. A criterion that emphasizes the former without measuring the latter can reward issue identification without strategic assessment.

\paragraph{Accrual and pleaded dates.} Criteria C-013--C-015 require an accrual dispute and alternative discovery dates. The complaint alleges discovery of integration problems in August 2022, latency problems by November 2022, an acknowledgment in January 2023, and an unsuccessful patch on April 15, 2023. Paragraph 83 alleges that the plaintiff could not know the full extent of the breach until the patch failed, claims reliance on a promised fix, and states a March 22, 2024 filing date. These allegations require analyzing knowledge sufficient for accrual, distinct breaches, tolling or estoppel, and enforceability of the one-year contract term. Texas law itself restricts contractual limitations periods, subject to statutory exceptions \citep{texasLimitations}.

The appropriate critique is therefore not that the dates mechanically prove the entire claim time-barred. Nor is it enough to announce that knowledge is always a fact issue immune from dismissal. A plaintiff's allegations can establish an affirmative defense on the face of the pleading, while a genuine factual or legal issue may prevent that result \citep{jones}. The rubric should require applying the governing rule to the actual chronology and contract. It should allow the model to distinguish stronger from weaker opposition arguments instead of requiring the conclusion that every accrual question is unresolved.

\paragraph{Integration versus non-reliance.} Criterion C-012 requires the model to characterize the merger-clause defense as legally incorrect. MSA section 14.7 also contains express non-reliance language, which is analytically distinct from an ordinary integration clause. Texas authority distinguishes these contractual concepts and examines the language and surrounding circumstances \citep{italiancowboy}. The source record therefore does not justify a categorical rubric requiring the ordinary-merger-clause answer without that analysis. This observation identifies an overbroad criterion; it does not establish that the particular disclaimer is enforceable or that the fraud claim must fail.

\paragraph{Hidden output requirements.} C-028 requires concrete opposition recommendations for at least eight issues, although the prompt supplies no minimum count. C-022--C-025 prescribe high-severity treatment of specified issues. These criteria permit equivalent severity language; it would be inaccurate to say they necessarily require literal labels. C-026 prescribes organization tied to the motion's framework and expressly permits omitting Rule 12(b)(1) when no genuine issue exists. Whether those requirements improve usefulness to counsel is a measurement question. They should not be confused with independently established legal errors.

\paragraph{Document construction and limits of the review.} The supplied documents have programmatic-generation metadata and include a stale table-of-contents instruction in the agreement. The motion nevertheless has recognizable filing components. We have not established a court-rule violation warranting striking it, proven LLM authorship, or estimated how often such artifacts occur across LAB. Synthetic design can be useful for controlled testing; the concern is whether the rewarded conduct transfers to actual matters. The local audit also records genuine solver mistakes and unresolved legal disagreements. A benchmark-wide conclusion would require a representative expert review of source documents, criteria, model outputs, and grading decisions.


\section{A prospective test of outcome feedback}
\label{app:study}
A follow-up would freeze a base model and an earlier training cohort, then compare outcome feedback with a matched control on later, disjoint cases. Related cases should remain together across splits. The primary endpoint would be a predeclared difference in proper score; calibration, failure rates, cost, and reasoning validity would be secondary endpoints. Blinded expert assessment of advice or drafting would test transfer.

For a model emitting a rationale $z$ and forecast $p$, a candidate objective is
\begin{equation}
 \max_\theta\;\mathbb E_{(X,U,Y)\sim\mathcal D_{\mathrm{train}},\,(z,p)\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid X,U)}\left[1-(p-Y)^2\right].
\end{equation}
This is an outcome-scored prediction objective, not a simulator that allows the agent to alter the litigation trajectory. Optimizing it need not require reinforcement learning: supervised probabilistic prediction, calibration, and policy-gradient approaches are competing methods to test. It also does not directly reward faithful rationales. Leakage-controlled held-out cases and an independent transfer endpoint are therefore necessary to distinguish generalization from memorized case outcomes or improved probability reporting alone.
