## I. BOTTOM LINE (for all readers)

**You are right to be worried, but conspicuousness is not the biggest problem.** The warranty framework for the AquaPure Max 9000 fails at a more basic level, for five reasons:

1.  **The “Total Performance Guarantee” is an express warranty. The disclaimer cannot undo it.** The 99.97% contaminant-removal figure, the 98.5% uptime figure, the 500,000 gallons-per-day figure, and the promise that “your water will meet or exceed all EPA and state purity standards” all appear in the brochure, the sales deck, the quotation letter, and the sales scripts. Each is a specific, measurable statement of fact about the product, and CIT calls it a “guarantee.” Under UCC § 2-313 these are express warranties. Under UCC § 2-316(1), fine print cannot cancel an express warranty that is inconsistent with it. No formatting change to the disclaimer fixes this.

2.  **The disclaimer and remedy limits probably aren’t part of the contract at all.** The Standard Limited Warranty is handed over only at delivery. The January 8 sales memo tells reps *not* to give it to customers before purchase. For a negotiated purchase of \$475,000–\$525,000, a court is likely to treat terms first shown after the sale as proposals that never became part of the deal (UCC § 2-207; § 2-209). If they drop out, CIT is left with (a) the marketing warranties, (b) the implied warranties of merchantability and fitness for purpose, and (c) the full UCC damages remedies, which include consequential damages. There would be no liability cap.

3.  **The current warranty does not clearly cover the AquaMonitor v3.2 software, and it does not clearly *protect CIT* on it either.** The warranty defines “Product” as hardware. So the software may fall outside the warranty’s coverage *and* outside its disclaimer and liability cap. Meanwhile, the marketing says AquaMonitor gives “accurate, real-time data you can rely on for regulatory compliance” and is “21 CFR Part 11 compliant.” If AquaMonitor falsely shows an “all clear,” that is the largest exposure we have not addressed, and it reaches into bodily-injury and public-health liability.

4.  **The repair-or-replace remedy is already failing in practice. The Max 9000 guarantee makes it worse.** Average time to resolve a claim has risen from about 28 days to about 47 days. Full-unit replacements now take 78–85 days. CIT paid \$680,000 in FY2024 to settle consequential-damages claims that argued the remedy had “failed of its essential purpose.” Repairing a unit cannot compensate a municipality or food processor for the weeks it spent producing out-of-spec water. Yet that is exactly the loss a performance guarantee invites customers to claim.

5.  **Insurance will probably not respond to the claims this program generates.** A standard CGL policy covers bodily injury and property damage. It does not cover a customer’s financial loss from a product that underperforms a promise. Stoneridge’s point about the contractual-liability exclusion is valid, but coverage is weaker still because of the “your product,” “impaired property,” and recall exclusions. The \$154,000 premium increase buys *no* added coverage for guarantee claims. The underwriter is signaling a risk assessment, not billing a rounding error.

**What this means for the business.** We do **not** recommend dropping performance claims or walking away from the \$12.3 million pipeline. We recommend turning the “Total Performance Guarantee” from an open-ended marketing promise into a **defined, contractual Performance Warranty**. It would have measurable terms, a testing protocol, a cure period, a stepped set of remedies (repair, then performance credits, then buy-back), and a liability cap the customer signs *before* purchase. Well-drafted, this lets Sales keep saying “guaranteed” truthfully, while CIT knows what the guarantee costs when it is called. Four items cannot wait for March 17:

- **Immediately:** withdraw and replace the January 8 sales-team memo. Stop the “make customers whole,” “don’t hedge,” and “don’t provide the warranty” instructions. Reps are creating express warranties in live conversations with Triton, Great Lakes, and Harmon Valley right now.
- **Immediately:** preserve all Max 9000 marketing and sales materials and communications, because the *Redmond* litigation enters discovery this month (see Part VIII.C).
- **Before any pre-order is accepted (April 1):** put in place a pre-sale contract package: a master purchase agreement with the warranty attached, a signed acknowledgment, and separate software terms.
- **Before the Board relies further on the launch plan:** brief the CEO, and through him the Board, on the insurance gap and the *Redmond* linkage.

## IV. ISSUE 1 — COMPLIANCE OF THE CURRENT WARRANTY FRAMEWORK WITH UCC ARTICLE 2

### A. Question presented

Does the SLW effectively (i) exclude the implied warranties of merchantability and fitness, (ii) limit the buyer to repair or replacement, and (iii) exclude consequential damages and cap liability, for a Max 9000 sale?

### B. Short answer

On paper, the SLW’s language is largely adequate. It is also better than the version BSK described. In practice it is **unlikely to be enforced as written in a Max 9000 sale**, for three reasons, listed in order of importance:

- 1.  It is not presented before the contract is formed, so it probably never becomes part of the contract.

- 2.  It is inconsistent with the express performance warranties, and § 2-316(1) resolves that conflict in the buyer’s favor.

- 3.  The conspicuousness concern you raised is real but secondary. It is also *more* serious in Texas than elsewhere.

### C. Analysis

**1. Requirements for an effective disclaimer (§ 2-316(2)–(3)).** To exclude merchantability, the language must mention “merchantability” and, if in writing, be conspicuous. To exclude fitness for a particular purpose, the exclusion must be in writing and conspicuous. SLW § 5 meets the *content* requirements: it names both warranties, cites §§ 2-314 and 2-315, and also disclaims course-of-dealing and usage-of-trade warranties.

**2. Conspicuousness (§ 1-201(b)(10)).** A term is conspicuous if “a reasonable person against which it is to operate ought to have noticed it.” Whether it is conspicuous is a question of law for the court. The statute gives safe-harbor examples: a heading in capitals, or body text in larger or contrasting type, font, or color, or set off by symbols or marks. Working through our document:

- *In CIT’s favor:* the disclaimer sits under a bold, underlined heading, “SECTION 5: DISCLAIMER OF WARRANTIES,” and the body is in all capitals. Many courts treat a capitalized, labeled paragraph as conspicuous, especially between merchants.
- *Against CIT:* per your description, the disclaimer is in the same font and size as the surrounding text. The warranty *also* sets § 6 in all capitals, so capitals do not distinguish the disclaimer from other text. The document is multi-page, uniform, and received after delivery, often by operations staff rather than the purchasing decision-maker.
- *Texas specifically:* in *Cate v. Dover Corp.*, 790 S.W.2d 559 (Tex. 1990), the Texas Supreme Court held a disclaimer not conspicuous where it was printed in the same typeface and size as the surrounding text and was not set apart. The Court also held that a buyer’s *actual knowledge* of the disclaimer can cure a lack of conspicuousness. That second point is precisely what a signed pre-sale acknowledgment establishes. For the Triton transaction, the current formatting is a material weakness.
- *The price-point factor you identified:* the test is objective, but courts do look at context. A \$475,000 negotiated purchase built around a “guarantee” is exactly the setting in which a court will ask whether a reasonable buyer ought to have noticed that the guarantee was being taken away in the fine print.

*Conclusion:* your concern is well founded. A court could plausibly find § 5 conspicuous. But it is a genuine litigation risk, it is acute in Texas, and it costs almost nothing to remove. BSK’s 2021 formatting recommendations should be implemented now: bold, larger type, a bordered or shaded box, a stand-alone “IMPORTANT — DISCLAIMER OF WARRANTIES” heading, and buyer initials. The same treatment should apply to § 4 (exclusive remedy) and § 6 (limitation of liability). **But formatting alone will not save the framework.**

**3. Assent and timing: the threshold defect.** Contract terms are fixed when the contract is formed. Under CIT’s current practice, the contract forms when CIT sends its written order acknowledgment in response to the buyer’s purchase order. The SLW arrives weeks or months later, in the crate. At that point it can operate only as (a) a proposal for additional terms under § 2-207(2), or (b) a proposed modification under § 2-209. Between merchants, additional terms do not become part of the contract if they “materially alter” it. Official Comment 4 to § 2-207 gives, as its lead example of material alteration, a clause “negating such standard warranties as that of merchantability or fitness.” A modification requires assent, and here the buyer has already paid 70% and taken delivery.

The “rolling contract” cases that uphold terms-in-the-box, *ProCD, Inc. v. Zeidenberg*, 86 F.3d 1447 (7th Cir. 1996) and *Hill v. Gateway 2000, Inc.*, 105 F.3d 1147 (7th Cir. 1997), involve consumer mass-market goods that can be returned. They are hard to extend to an 8,500-lb unit that has been custom-configured, installed, and commissioned, and no one can realistically “return it if you don’t like the terms.” Contrast *Step-Saver Data Systems, Inc. v. Wyse Technology*, 939 F.2d 91 (3d Cir. 1991), which treated post-formation box-top terms as § 2-207 proposals. BSK reached the same conclusion in 2021 and called it the “highest-priority” issue. It has not been fixed, and the January 8 sales memo now instructs reps to *withhold* the warranty. That instruction converts an oversight into documented intent. It will support unconscionability and fraudulent-concealment arguments, and it will read badly in front of a jury.

**Consequence if the SLW drops out:**

- The implied warranties of merchantability (§ 2-314) and fitness for purpose (§ 2-315) apply undisclaimed. The fitness warranty is especially potent here, because the sales memo tells reps to “tailor performance commitments” to each customer’s water challenges and regulatory needs. That is the § 2-315 fact pattern: the seller has reason to know the particular purpose, and the buyer relies on the seller’s skill.
- The buyer has the full Article 2 remedies (§§ 2-711 through 2-715), including incidental and consequential damages.
- There is no liability cap.
- The SLW’s integration clause (§ 7.3), Oregon choice of law (§ 7.1), and Oregon forum (§ 7.2) fall with the rest of it.

**4. Parol evidence and the integration clause (§ 2-202).** Even if the SLW were part of the contract, its merger clause does less than the drafting suggests:

- 1.  The quotation letter will likely be the pre-contract document the parties actually negotiated from, and it recites the 99.97% and 98.5% specifications.

- 2.  Written specifications in the parties’ transaction documents are not “prior” statements that a merger clause excludes. They are part of the writings that make up the agreement.

- 3.  The SLW purports to be “the entire agreement . . . with respect to warranty coverage” together with “CIT’s standard purchase order terms,” which I have not located. If the buyer’s purchase order or CIT’s acknowledgment incorporates the quotation, the specifications come in.

- 4.  Merger clauses do not bar fraud-in-the-inducement claims in most states.

**5. Other drafting issues in the SLW:**

- **30-day notice condition (§ 3.3(e)).** § 2-607(3)(a) requires notice within a “reasonable time.” A contractual shortening is generally enforceable between merchants if reasonable. Thirty days from *discovery* is probably defensible, but a court may refuse to enforce it against slow-developing performance degradation. The Meridian and Oceanview claims involved drift over 2–4 months.
- **Coverage limited to “materials and workmanship” (§ 3.1).** This excludes design defects and performance shortfalls. It does not match the product CIT is actually selling. Design-based performance shortfalls, such as a membrane that cannot reach 99.97% on certain feed water, would fall *outside* the SLW entirely and be governed only by the marketing warranties.
- **“CIT determines, in its reasonable judgment” (§ 4.1).** Letting the warrantor decide whether a defect exists is acceptable, but it invites a bad-faith argument if claims are denied while the performance “guarantee” is being marketed.
- **The “basis of the bargain” risk-allocation acknowledgment (§ 5, final paragraph).** This is useful *only* if the buyer saw it before contracting. Delivered afterward, it is unsupported by any assent.
- **§ 1 says the Buyer “acknowledges” the terms “by taking delivery.”** This is the classic post-formation device and carries little weight in a negotiated B2B sale.
- **“Successor models” coverage (§ 1).** The SLW applies to “successor models in the AquaPure series,” so it arguably reaches the Max 9000 without a product-specific warranty. It was drafted for \$18,000–\$475,000 legacy units with no performance guarantee and no cloud software.

## V. ISSUE 2 — EXPRESS WARRANTY CREATION THROUGH MARKETING AND SALES MATERIALS (UCC § 2-313)

### A. Question presented

Do the brochure, sales deck, quotation template, and sales memo, individually or together, create express warranties? If so, can the SLW limit them?

### B. Short answer

**Yes. Individually and cumulatively. And no, not as currently structured.** The quantified performance figures, the regulatory-compliance statements, and the software-accuracy statements are affirmations of fact and descriptions of the goods. Once they reach a buyer during negotiations, they are presumptively part of the “basis of the bargain.” The “guarantee” branding removes any argument that they are opinion or puffery. The SLW cannot negate them (§ 2-316(1)). The best CIT can do is limit the *remedy* for their breach under § 2-719. That requires a remedy clause the buyer actually agreed to, which brings the analysis back to Part IV.

### C. Analysis

**1. The elements.** Under § 2-313(1):

- 1.  any affirmation of fact or promise relating to the goods that becomes part of the basis of the bargain,

- 2.  any description of the goods made part of the basis of the bargain, and

- 3.  any sample or model

creates an express warranty that the goods will conform. No formal words like “warrant” or “guarantee” are required, and no specific intent to warrant is required (§ 2-313(2)). The only safe harbor is for a “mere affirmation of the value of the goods” or a “statement purporting to be merely the seller’s opinion or commendation.”

**2. Puffery is not available for these statements.** Courts distinguish vague praise (“industry-leading,” “unmatched,” “peace of mind”) from specific, measurable, verifiable claims. “99.97% contaminant removal,” “98.5% annual uptime,” “500,000 gallons per day,” “meets all EPA Safe Drinking Water Act standards,” “USP purified water specifications,” and “21 CFR Part 11 compliant” are all objectively testable. Calling them a “guarantee,” pairing them with “or we’ll make it right — guaranteed,” and quoting the CEO saying “If it doesn’t perform, we’ll make it right. Period.” make them promises about future performance. Ms. Dasgupta’s email makes the point conclusively: “They aren’t puffery. They are the product’s core selling proposition.” The sales memo tells reps that if asked “whether these are estimates or guarantees, the answer is clear: these are the numbers we commit to.” In litigation, that sentence would be Exhibit A.

The generic statements (“unmatched,” “pinnacle,” “peace of mind”) are likely puffery. They add color but not liability.

**3. Basis of the bargain.** Official Comment 3 to § 2-313 provides that “no particular reliance on such statements need be shown”; affirmations made during a bargain are “fairly to be regarded as part of the description of those goods.” The states split on how much reliance matters:

- *California*: *Hauter v. Zogarts*, 14 Cal.3d 104 (1975), and *Keith v. Buchanan*, 173 Cal.App.3d 13 (1985). *Keith* holds that sales brochure specifications create express warranties without proof of specific reliance, and places the burden on the seller to show the statement was *not* part of the bargain.
- *New York*: *CBS Inc. v. Ziff-Davis Publishing Co.*, 75 N.Y.2d 496 (1990). The buyer need only show the warranty was part of the bargain, not that it believed the statement.
- *Illinois* and *Texas* courts sometimes require reliance or at least awareness, but awareness is certain here. The statements are the reason these buyers are paying a \$195,000+ per-unit premium over competitors (Ms. Dasgupta’s figure: \$475,000 vs. \$280,000).

The facts could hardly be stronger for a buyer. Triton “came to us specifically because of the performance guarantee.” Great Lakes’ FDA compliance is “directly tied” to it. The guarantee is “what got \[Harmon Valley\] to consolidate their purchasing.”

**5. Cumulative effect.** Courts read all of the seller’s representations together. The consistent, repeated message across four channels, with CEO endorsement and Board-approved branding, makes it impossible to argue that any one statement was an isolated slip or a rep’s unauthorized opinion. SLW § 5 says that “no oral or written information or advice given by CIT . . . shall create a warranty.” But (a) that clause arrives after the sale, and (b) courts widely hold that a seller cannot authorize its sales force to make specific promises and then disclaim their authority to do so.

**6. § 2-316(1): why the disclaimer cannot fix this.** Where words creating an express warranty and words negating it cannot reasonably be read together, “negation or limitation is inoperative.” A guarantee of 99.97% removal cannot reasonably coexist with “Seller makes no warranty regarding the results that may be obtained from the use of the Products” (SLW § 5). The express warranty prevails. The only lawful ways to manage an express warranty are to (i) not make it, (ii) define it precisely so its scope is controlled, or (iii) limit the *remedy* for breaching it under § 2-719.

**7. Exposure beyond warranty law.** The same statements support non-contract claims that warranty disclaimers do not reach:

- **Fraudulent or negligent misrepresentation.** Examples: the claims that the performance is “verified” and “validated,” and statements about regulatory compliance that CIT may not have substantiated. Is WFI production actually validated? Has AquaMonitor been assessed for Part 11 compliance, which depends heavily on how the customer configures and validates the system? Does “verified across all target contaminant categories” hold at every feed-water profile?
- **State unfair-practices statutes** that reach business buyers: Illinois Consumer Fraud Act, Massachusetts ch. 93A § 11, California UCL/FAL, and potentially Texas DTPA (addressed in Part V-State below).
- **Federal Trade Commission Act § 5** substantiation doctrine for objective claims.

These routes often bypass contractual caps and consequential-damages exclusions. Before any quantified claim remains in the materials, **CIT should hold a written substantiation file** for it. Six beta sites over 14 months can support a 99.97% claim *under tested conditions*. They cannot support “your water supply” universally, a 15-year design life as a guarantee, or “meets all state standards” in every state.

**8. The Board-approval point.** Ms. Dasgupta notes the Board endorsed the “Total Performance Guarantee” program. Board approval of a marketing strategy does not change how a court reads the resulting statements. If anything, it forecloses the argument that the statements were unauthorized. The Board approved a *strategy*. It can and should approve a *legally sound implementation* of that strategy. My recommendations preserve the program and its branding.

## VI. ISSUE 3 — MULTI-STATE ANALYSIS

**Threshold choice-of-law point.** The SLW’s Oregon choice-of-law and forum clauses would, if enforced, displace much of the state-by-state variation discussed below (UCC § 1-301 permits the choice of a state bearing a reasonable relation to the transaction, and CIT’s headquarters and FOB-origin shipping supply that). But (i) those clauses live in the post-sale document and probably are not part of the contract (Part IV.C.3); (ii) the Quotation Template leaves governing law as “\[Oregon / Buyer’s state — to be determined\]”; (iii) statutory consumer-protection and public-procurement rules usually apply regardless of a contractual choice of law; and (iv) governmental buyers commonly refuse out-of-state law and venue. So the analysis must assume each buyer’s home-state law may apply.

### A. Texas — Triton Municipal Water Authority (\$5.7M)

**Short answer.** I could not confirm your recollection that Texas has a statute *specifically forbidding or limiting UCC warranty disclaimers* in sales of goods to governmental entities. Texas’s UCC provisions (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §§ 2.313–2.316, 2.719) track the uniform text, and I am not aware of a Texas statute that overrides them for public buyers. This should be confirmed with Texas counsel before we rely on it. **But the compliance trap you sensed is real. It comes from procurement law and public-entity contracting practice, not from Article 2:**

1.  **Whose paper governs.** Texas water authorities, districts, and municipalities generally must award contracts above statutory thresholds through competitive procurement. Examples: Tex. Loc. Gov’t Code ch. 252 for municipalities, and Tex. Water Code § 49.273 for many water districts. The applicable regime depends on how Triton was created (for instance, a conservation and reclamation district under Tex. Const. art. XVI, § 59, or a municipal utility), which we must confirm. If Triton procures by bid or RFP, **its specifications and contract form will govern**, and those forms routinely (a) require the vendor to warrant conformance to the specifications and fitness for the stated purpose, (b) prohibit or cap vendor limitations of liability, (c) require Texas law and venue in the entity’s county, and (d) reject vendor terms that conflict. A CIT submission that takes exception to those terms may be deemed non-responsive. Worse, if the RFP incorporates CIT’s marketing specifications (99.97%/98.5%) as requirements, the guarantee becomes a *specification the vendor warrants*, with no cap. The sales memo’s instruction to “tailor performance commitments to the specific municipality’s water quality challenges” accelerates exactly that outcome. *Action:* obtain Triton’s procurement documents now. Confirm whether this is a competitive procurement, a cooperative-purchasing contract, or a sole-source/professional exemption. Note that the pre-order campaign, which invites Triton to “reserve a production slot,” may itself be inconsistent with Triton’s bidding obligations.
2.  **Governmental immunity is asymmetrical.** Local governmental entities in Texas have immunity from suit on contract claims, waived only as provided in Tex. Loc. Gov’t Code §§ 271.151–.160 for written contracts for goods or services. Recovery under that waiver is limited mostly to the balance due, and consequential damages are excluded (§ 271.153). So *CIT’s* claims against Triton, such as for non-payment, are constrained, while Triton’s warranty claims against CIT are not. Texas public entities also generally cannot agree to indemnify a vendor or accept open-ended obligations, because of constitutional debt and credit restrictions. The practical point: CIT will have little leverage and few remedies of its own. The protective terms must be won in the contract.
3.  **Texas DTPA.** The Deceptive Trade Practices–Consumer Protection Act (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 17.41 et seq.) defines “consumer” to include “this state, or a subdivision or agency of this state” (§ 17.45(4)), and allows claims for breach of an express or implied warranty (§ 17.50(a)(2)), with enhanced damages for knowing conduct. Two exemptions likely protect CIT, if the transaction is structured correctly: § 17.49(f) (written contract over \$100,000 where the consumer is represented by legal counsel), and **§ 17.49(g) (a transaction or set of related transactions involving total consideration over \$500,000, other than a residence)**. A \$5.7M, 12-unit purchase should fall within § 17.49(g). The “business consumer” exclusion for entities with \$25M or more in assets (§ 17.45(4)) may not reach a governmental entity. *Action:* document the purchase as a single project so the § 17.49(g) exemption clearly applies, and confirm Triton is represented by counsel. A DTPA waiver under § 17.42 requires specific statutory language and consumer counsel. It is a secondary option.
4.  **Conspicuousness is strictest here.** See *Cate v. Dover Corp.* (Part IV.C.2). Without a pre-sale, signed acknowledgment, our current disclaimer format is at serious risk in a Texas court.
5.  **Public-contract compliance items** (confirm with Triton or Texas counsel as applicable): Form 1295 disclosure of interested parties (Tex. Gov’t Code § 2252.908) for contracts requiring governing-body approval or over the statutory value; statutory verifications for governmental contracts over \$100,000 (e.g., Tex. Gov’t Code ch. 2271 (Israel boycott), ch. 2274 (energy-company and firearm-entity certifications)); possible payment and performance bond requirements under Tex. Gov’t Code ch. 2253 if CIT’s installation scope makes the contract a public work; and the Texas Prompt Payment Act (Tex. Gov’t Code ch. 2251), which *helps* CIT on collection.
6.  **Public-health overlay.** Triton has Safe Drinking Water Act obligations, including the new PFAS maximum contaminant levels (compliance timelines and parts of that rule are subject to ongoing regulatory and legal change). Stating that the Max 9000 “meets or exceeds all federal and state drinking water standards, including the latest EPA PFAS regulations” converts every regulatory change, and every violation Triton incurs, into a potential CIT warranty claim. Where treated water reaches the public, a false AquaMonitor reading implicates bodily-injury claims by residents. Those claims are tort claims that no contract with Triton can disclaim.

**Exposure assessment: HIGH.** The most likely outcome is that Triton’s contract terms govern, with CIT’s marketing figures incorporated as warranted specifications. CIT needs a negotiated Performance Warranty (Part IX) ready to *offer* as Triton’s specification language, rather than waiting to receive Triton’s version.

### B. California — Harmon Valley Agricultural Cooperative (\$2.4M)

**Short answer.** California’s consumer warranty statutes are unlikely to reach this sale directly. But the co-op structure creates other, genuine exposures that CIT’s standard approach does not address.

1.  **Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act** (Cal. Civ. Code § 1790 et seq.) applies to “consumer goods,” meaning products bought “for use primarily for personal, family, or household purposes” (§ 1791(a)). An industrial purification unit bought by a co-op for irrigation and processing is not a consumer good. The **Consumer Legal Remedies Act** (Civ. Code § 1750 et seq.) protects individuals buying for personal, family, or household purposes, so it also does not apply. **Magnuson-Moss** (15 U.S.C. § 2301) turns on whether the product type is “normally used” for personal, family, or household purposes, and a \$475,000 industrial system is not. BSK’s 2021 view that MMWA risk is marginal remains correct. *Where the line is:* these statutes attach by the *nature of the product and its use*, not by the buyer’s size or sophistication. They would come into play only if CIT sold units to, or for use by, individuals for household purposes, for example a co-op distributing small units to members’ residences. That is not this deal. We should put in the contract that the units are purchased for commercial and agricultural use.
2.  **The real California issues:**
    - **Who is the buyer?** If Harmon Valley buys *as agent* for its members, each member farm may be a contracting buyer or an intended third-party beneficiary, with its own warranty claims and consequential losses (crop loss, rejected produce, processing shutdowns). California did not enact UCC § 2-318 in uniform form, and it generally requires privity for *implied* warranty claims. But courts recognize an exception for **express warranties made in advertising and labels** that reach the remote purchaser. The brochure is addressed to agricultural operations and “cooperative purchasing programs,” so member farms that saw it could sue CIT directly on the guarantee. They would not be bound by any disclaimer they never saw. *Action:* contract with the co-op as principal. Include an express no-third-party-beneficiary clause. Have the co-op warrant its authority, flow the warranty terms down to members (or obtain member acknowledgments), and indemnify CIT for member claims beyond the contract remedies.
    - **Unfair Competition Law and False Advertising Law** (Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17200, 17500) apply to business-to-business advertising. Remedies are limited to restitution and injunction, but these claims sit outside the contract, and “unfair”/“fraudulent” business practice theories fit the withheld-warranty instruction uncomfortably well.
    - **Cal. Civ. Code § 1668** voids contract clauses that exempt a party from its own fraud, willful injury, or violation of law. CIT’s limitation clauses will not shield misrepresentation claims.
    - **Specific agricultural representations.** The brochure claims removal of “pesticide residues” and “nitrates” (deck), and the sales memo describes the product as “specifically designed” for “seasonal demand fluctuations and varying source water quality.” That is a § 2-315 fitness-for-purpose invitation. Agricultural source water (high TDS, nitrates, silica, iron) is also exactly the kind of feed water most likely to fall *outside* CIT’s published input parameters, which would void the SLW coverage while leaving the marketing guarantee intact.
    - **Express warranties without reliance** (*Keith v. Buchanan*): brochure specifications are presumptively part of the bargain.
3.  **Unconscionability (Cal. Com. Code § 2302; Civ. Code § 1670.5).** California courts scrutinize both procedural unconscionability (surprise, oppression) and substantive unconscionability. A disclaimer deliberately withheld until after delivery, against a co-op that “may lack the resources or expertise to closely review warranty terms” (as BSK noted), is a candidate for a procedural-unconscionability finding.

**Exposure assessment: MODERATE-HIGH**, driven by member-farm claims and misrepresentation theories rather than the consumer statutes.

### C. Illinois — Great Lakes Processing Corp. (\$4.2M)

**Short answer.** This is a standard UCC analysis (810 ILCS 5/2-313, 2-316, 2-719), but three Illinois-specific points and the FDA overlay raise the stakes.

1.  **The Illinois Consumer Fraud and Deceptive Business Practices Act** (815 ILCS 505/1 et seq.) defines “consumer” as any person who purchases merchandise “not for resale,” which covers a business buying equipment for its own use. The Act permits actual damages, attorney fees, and, in appropriate cases, punitive damages (815 ILCS 505/10a). Misrepresentations of product performance and regulatory compliance are core ICFA territory, and contractual disclaimers do not bar ICFA claims. Business plaintiffs sometimes must show a “consumer nexus,” which reduces but does not remove the risk.
2.  **Economic loss doctrine.** *Moorman Mfg. Co. v. National Tank Co.*, 91 Ill.2d 69 (1982) bars negligence and strict-liability recovery for purely economic loss. That channels Great Lakes’ claims into contract, which helps *if* the contract limits hold. But *Moorman* has exceptions for intentional misrepresentation and for negligent misrepresentation by those in the business of supplying information. The second exception is an argument Great Lakes could press against **AquaMonitor’s compliance reporting**.
3.  **Privity.** Illinois generally requires privity for implied warranty claims seeking economic loss (*Szajna v. General Motors Corp.*, 115 Ill.2d 294 (1986)). Great Lakes buys directly, so this does not help CIT here.
4.  **Failure of essential purpose.** Illinois decisions have tended to treat a separately stated consequential-damages exclusion as enforceable even where the repair remedy fails, applying a case-by-case, independent approach. But outcomes turn on unconscionability and the facts. See Part VIII.
5.  **The FDA overlay, and how it affects reliance.** Food processors must use water that is safe and of adequate sanitary quality (21 CFR 117.37(a)) and must maintain food-safety plans under FSMA preventive-controls rules. It cuts both ways:
    - *Against CIT:* Great Lakes’ reliance on the guarantee is concrete and documented. CIT’s materials say the system “ensures” FDA compliance and “reduces risk of product recalls,” and the sales memo says recall risks “are eliminated.” A water-quality failure can produce recall costs, destroyed inventory, and regulatory action. Those consequential damages dwarf the unit price; Redmond alone claims \$740,000 in spoiled product.
    - *For CIT:* FDA rules place the compliance obligation on the processor, which must verify its own water quality. That supports an argument that reasonable reliance on CIT’s equipment, and especially on AquaMonitor, *as a substitute for the processor’s own verification* was unreasonable. CIT’s contract should make that allocation explicit: AquaMonitor supports compliance and does not replace the customer’s regulatory sampling program.
6.  **LOI status.** An LOI has been signed. If it references the performance figures or the guarantee, those may already be framed as agreed terms. I need to review it.

**Exposure assessment: HIGH**, driven by consequential damages (recall and inventory loss), ICFA exposure, and the Enhanced configuration’s software-reliance claims.

## VII. ISSUE 4 — SOFTWARE WARRANTY GAP: AQUAMONITOR v3.2

### A. Short answer

This may well be our largest unaddressed exposure, as you suspected. The current framework has **three separate gaps**:

- 1.  the SLW’s express warranty does not clearly cover the software;

- 2.  the SLW’s *disclaimer and liability cap* may not clearly cover the software either, because “Product” is defined as hardware;

- 3.  the cloud subscription component is a service that Article 2 may not govern at all.

The marketing, meanwhile, makes affirmative accuracy and compliance promises about the software.

### B. Analysis

**1. Does Article 2 apply?** Courts apply a “predominant purpose” test to mixed goods-and-services transactions. Embedded software sold as part of a physical system is generally treated as goods (e.g., *Advent Systems Ltd. v. Unisys Corp.*, 925 F.2d 670 (3d Cir. 1991)). So on-unit AquaMonitor functionality in a Max 9000 purchase is likely governed by Article 2, including implied merchantability unless effectively disclaimed. However:

- the **12-month cloud subscription** (analytics, remote dashboard, “cloud-based data storage”);
- **over-the-air updates**; and
- the **“dedicated technical support line”**

are services and data hosting, governed by common-law contract principles (and by any license terms). *CIT has no license, EULA, or SaaS terms in the documents I reviewed.* Without them, CIT’s rights in the software (ownership, license scope, restrictions on reverse engineering, termination, what happens after the subscription ends) are undefined. So is its liability for cloud outages, data loss, and security incidents.

**2. Coverage gap: what CIT warrants versus what customers will expect.** SLW § 3.1 warrants freedom from “defects in materials and workmanship,” and § 2 defines “Product” by listing hardware (housings, pumps, control panels, valves, sensors, and so on). Software is not “material,” and coding errors are not naturally “workmanship.” A customer will reasonably expect, because the brochure says so, that AquaMonitor:

- provides “accurate, real-time data you can rely on for regulatory compliance”;
- “continuously verifies” that the system performs;
- generates “audit-ready” regulatory reports; and
- (deck) is “21 CFR Part 11 compliant.”

None of that is warranted in the SLW. All of it is warranted by the marketing.

**3. Protection gap: the disclaimer may not reach the software.** SLW § 5’s final paragraphs disclaim warranties regarding “the accuracy or reliability of any information obtained through the use of the Products,” which is helpful language. But it is keyed to “Products,” which are defined as hardware. A court construing the document against CIT as drafter could hold that the software, the cloud service, and the data are outside § 5 and § 6 altogether. At best the question is ambiguous, and ambiguity is resolved against the drafter.

**4. The false “all clear” scenario.** Suppose AquaMonitor reports compliant water while the system is out of specification, and a customer distributes contaminated water. CIT faces:

- 1.  **Contract/warranty claims by the buyer**: breach of the express accuracy warranty, and consequential damages including regulatory penalties, recalls, and remediation.

- 2.  **Tort claims by third parties**: residents, consumers of the customer’s food products, or pharmaceutical patients who suffered bodily injury. These plaintiffs are not bound by any contract term. Product-liability theories (design defect, failure to warn) apply to the integrated product.

- 3.  **Negligent misrepresentation** (the “supplier of information” exception to the economic loss rule).

- 4.  **Regulatory attention.**

This is not hypothetical. The claims file already shows sensor and firmware false readings: WC-2022-006 “false compliance readings”; WC-2023-021, five days of out-of-limit municipal water; WC-2024-056, an endotoxin false negative; WC-2024-071, an open claim where the customer cannot verify purity. AquaMonitor will *aggregate and certify* those readings, which magnifies the reliance.

**5. Why Ms. Dasgupta’s risk-mitigation argument cuts both ways.** She is right that real-time monitoring can catch degradation early. Operationally, that is a genuine benefit, and it would help CIT argue that a repair remedy can be performed promptly. But legally, monitoring software (i) creates **a continuous, time-stamped record of every moment the unit ran below 99.97%**, which is evidence of breach that customers will own; (ii) invites customers to reduce their own independent testing; and (iii) is itself a warranted feature that can fail. The risk-reducing potential is real only if the contract (a) defines what AquaMonitor does and does not do, (b) requires the customer to keep independent regulatory sampling, and (c) sets out how monitoring data is used to trigger and measure the Performance Warranty remedies.

**6. Cybersecurity.** The Enhanced configuration is a cloud-connected, remotely accessible, OTA-updated device installed at drinking-water utilities and FDA-regulated plants. Community water systems have federal risk-assessment obligations under America’s Water Infrastructure Act of 2018, and federal agencies have repeatedly warned of attacks on water-sector operational technology. Our materials promise “secure web and mobile interfaces” but no document allocates security responsibilities: patching, network segmentation, credentials, or incident notice. A breach that alters treatment or readings is a foreseeable claim. Current CGL coverage almost certainly excludes data and cyber events. The claims file also shows firmware updates that broke functions (WC-2023-043, alarm emails disabled by an update; WC-2024-009, firmware incompatible with a customer’s network security update). An OTA update that causes an outage or false reading would be a CIT-initiated change to a warranted product.

**7. Configuration inconsistency.** As noted in Part III.B, the sales memo says *both* configurations include AquaMonitor, while the brochure and quotation say only Enhanced does. Triton is buying 12 Base units. If reps told Triton, per the memo, that AquaMonitor is included, Triton may have a claim to software CIT did not intend to supply, or will rely on monitoring it does not have. This must be reconciled before any quote issues.

### C. Recommendation

Yes, CIT needs **separate software terms**: an AquaMonitor Software License and Subscription Agreement, incorporated into the purchase agreement and presented before purchase. It should cover:

- 1.  a limited software warranty (e.g., material conformance to published documentation for 90 days or the subscription term, with the remedy of correction, workaround, or refund of subscription fees);

- 2.  a clear statement that AquaMonitor is a **decision-support tool that does not replace the customer’s regulatory sampling, laboratory analysis, or compliance obligations**, with the customer acknowledging that duty;

- 3.  a sensor calibration and maintenance allocation;

- 4.  data accuracy disclaimers and uptime/SLA commitments for the cloud service;

- 22. OTA update terms, including the customer’s right to schedule updates for validated (pharma) systems;

- 6.  cybersecurity responsibilities and incident notification;

- 7.  data ownership and use;

- 8.  Part 11 language recast as “supports customer compliance when validated and configured by customer”; and

- 9.  a software-specific liability cap.

The SLW definition of “Product” should also be amended so the hardware disclaimer and cap expressly extend to embedded software, firmware, and data.

## VIII. ISSUE 5 — CLAIMS TREND, REDMOND, AND DURABILITY OF THE EXCLUSIVE REMEDY AND CONSEQUENTIAL-DAMAGES EXCLUSION

### A. Legal framework

§ 2-719(1) allows parties to make repair or replacement the exclusive remedy. § 2-719(2) provides that where “circumstances cause an exclusive or limited remedy to fail of its essential purpose, remedy may be had as provided in this Act.” A repair remedy typically fails when the seller (i) cannot fix the defect after reasonable attempts, (ii) cannot or will not repair within a reasonable time, or (iii) provides a remedy that cannot, by its nature, cure the type of loss contemplated. § 2-719(3) separately allows consequential damages to be excluded unless unconscionable.

**The split you identified.** When the repair remedy fails, does a separate consequential-damages exclusion survive?

- The *independent* approach enforces the exclusion as a separate risk allocation unless unconscionable. See *Chatlos Systems, Inc. v. National Cash Register Corp.*, 635 F.2d 1081 (3d Cir. 1980); *Kearney & Trecker Corp. v. Master Engraving Co.*, 107 N.J. 584 (1987). This is the majority, particularly between sophisticated commercial parties.
- The *dependent* approach holds that the exclusion falls with the failed remedy. See *RRX Industries, Inc. v. Lab-Con, Inc.*, 772 F.2d 543 (9th Cir. 1985), applying California law.
- Many courts take a case-by-case view focused on whether enforcing the exclusion would be unconscionable in light of the seller’s conduct.

The law in Missouri (the *Redmond* forum), Texas, Illinois, New York, Massachusetts, and Oregon on this precise point must be confirmed by jurisdiction-specific research before we rely on it. I have not done that research yet and flag it as a priority for outside counsel if engaged.

### B. Application to the Max 9000

**Structural strengths in the SLW.** § 6 expressly states that the limitations apply “regardless of whether any remedy . . . fails of its essential purpose” and are “independent of” the exclusive remedy. That is the right drafting. Many courts will honor it in a negotiated commercial contract. It is the SLW’s single best provision.

**Why I nonetheless rate the risk as high:**

1.  **Assent (again).** § 6 protects CIT only if it is part of the contract. See Part IV.C.3.
2.  **Remedy mismatch.** Repair or replacement addresses a *defective component*. The Total Performance Guarantee promises a *performance outcome*. When a unit produces 96.8% water for four months (Redmond), repair restores future performance but by its nature cannot address the period of non-conformity. Courts are receptive to the argument that a remedy which cannot address the very breach the seller promised against fails of its essential purpose. That is Redmond’s argument, and it is stronger for the Max 9000 because the guarantee is explicit.
3.  **CIT’s own words undercut the exclusion.** “Make it right,” “make customers whole,” and, in response to consequential-damages questions, “CIT is committed to making our customers whole” are evidence that the parties did *not* agree to exclude consequential damages. They support waiver, estoppel, and unconscionability arguments. They also support a § 2-316(1)-style argument that the promise of “whole” relief is itself part of the express warranty.
4.  **Operational capacity.** Resolution times are lengthening: about 28 days, then 35, then 47. Full-unit replacements took 78 and 85 days in FY2024. A pump is on an 8-week backorder. The Max 9000 is a new product with no installed-base spares pool, larger components, and a stated delivery lead time of 10–14 weeks. Replacing a failed Max 9000 unit could take a full production cycle, and a municipal or pharmaceutical customer cannot go without water for that long. “Failure to repair within a reasonable time” is the most commonly successful § 2-719(2) argument.
5.  **The recurring pressure-vessel defect.** Six structural failures across the legacy line, with a manufacturing investigation escalated, is the kind of pattern courts treat as a sign the seller *cannot* cure. If the Max 9000 shares vessel suppliers, designs, or weld processes, this must be resolved before launch, and any known issue must be disclosed internally to the Board.
6.  **The settlements already paid.** CIT paid \$680,000 in FY2024 on failure-of-essential-purpose arguments. That was a rational litigation choice, but plaintiffs’ counsel will learn of it, and it signals that CIT does not trust its own remedy clause.
7.  **Unconscionability optics.** The factors that tip the case-by-case analysis against sellers are all present: withheld terms, contradicting oral assurances, a guarantee marketed as “above and beyond any standard warranty,” and very large consequential losses foreseeably tied to public health and regulated production.

## X. RECOMMENDATIONS

These are written for a business audience. The goal is to **keep the Total Performance Guarantee as a selling proposition** while making it a defined, priced, contractual commitment instead of an open-ended promise. Each item notes whether it is legally necessary or risk-reducing best practice, which responds to Ms. Dasgupta’s fair request to separate the two.

### A. The core restructuring: from “promise” to “Performance Warranty”

**Legally necessary** if CIT intends to keep quantified guarantees. CIT should adopt a written **AquaPure Max 9000 Performance Warranty**, which Sales can continue to brand as the “Total Performance Guarantee,” containing:

1.  **Defined metrics.**
    - “99.97% removal” is measured for a *listed* set of contaminants (e.g., specified PFAS compounds, lead, arsenic, mercury, and microplastics above a stated particle size), by a specified test method, at a specified sampling point, averaged over a specified period.
    - Uptime is defined: 98.5% measured annually, excluding scheduled maintenance, customer-caused downtime, power or feed-water events, and force majeure.
    - Throughput is rated at specified feed-water conditions.
2.  **Conditions.** Feed water within published input parameters (with a site water analysis *before* sale, attached to the contract); CIT-certified installation; the maintenance schedule; genuine cartridges; and customer’s continued independent regulatory sampling.
3.  **Acceptance testing.** A commissioning performance test establishes baseline conformance. Passing it starts the warranty. Failing it triggers CIT’s cure obligation before final payment.
4.  **Cure period.** CIT gets a stated period, e.g., 30 days, to restore performance after notice, supported by AquaMonitor data where installed.
5.  **Stepped remedies.** First, repair, adjust, or replace components. If not cured within the cure period, performance credits (liquidated damages, e.g., a stated percentage of unit price per week or month of non-conformance, capped). If still not cured within a longer period, CIT’s option to replace the unit or buy it back (refund of price less depreciation). Liquidated damages are enforceable under § 2-718 if reasonable in light of anticipated harm. This ladder gives customers a remedy that *does* address the period of non-conformity, which is the strongest answer to a § 2-719(2) “failure of essential purpose” argument.
6.  **Aggregate cap and exclusion.** Total liability under the Performance Warranty capped (e.g., at the unit price or a percentage of it). A consequential-damages exclusion drafted as independent of the remedy and expressly acknowledged as surviving remedy failure. Consider *negotiated* carve-backs (e.g., a separate, capped sub-limit for documented substitute-water costs) for strategic accounts, because giving a little here is what makes the exclusion defensible.
7.  **Duration.** Performance warranty term stated (e.g., 3 years, aligned to parts). The “15-year design life” is described as a design parameter, not a warranty.
8.  **Regulatory statements.** Replace “your water will meet or exceed all EPA and state standards” with a statement of the *specified contaminant limits CIT warrants the system will achieve under the defined conditions*, and a statement that regulatory compliance remains the customer’s responsibility.

**Pricing and economics.** Because the remedies are defined, Finance can model the guarantee’s cost and set a reserve, and Stoneridge can present a bounded risk to the underwriter.

## XI. CONCLUSION

The core problem is not that CIT markets strong performance numbers. It is that CIT makes guarantees in one set of documents and tries to take them back in another document that the customer does not see until after the sale, and that does not even clearly cover the software CIT is promoting. Courts resolve that contradiction against the seller, and the claims history shows they already are. The remedy is achievable within Ms. Dasgupta’s timeline:

- define the guarantee;
- attach it, with the warranty and software terms, to every quote;
- get a signature before the deposit;
- retrain the sales team; and
- give the Board an accurate picture of the insurance position.

Done well, this lets CIT sell the Max 9000 on its numbers, with a guarantee that is honest, bounded, and defensible.
