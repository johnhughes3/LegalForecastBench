# I. Bottom Line

Pinnacle's motion is vulnerable on every one of its six grounds, and several of its most consequential arguments rest on quotations of the Logistics Services Agreement ("LSA") that do not match the executed contract. The three highest-value points for the opposition are:

- **1. The liability cap is misquoted.** Pinnacle's brief quotes Section 7.1 as a flat "fees paid in the prior 12 months" cap (\$1,860,000). The executed LSA caps liability at the greater of 12-month fees or \$2,000,000; carves out gross negligence and willful misconduct; and, through Section 7.2, excludes indemnification claims (§ 9.1) and claims for which Pinnacle must carry insurance (§ 6.1(b) requires full-replacement-value property coverage on Greenfield inventory). The brief omits all of this.

- **2. The consequential-damages waiver is misquoted.** The executed Section 7.3 expressly does not apply to "breach of Section 4.2" — the humidity and reporting obligation at the center of this case. Pinnacle's quotation drops that carve-out and adds language ("regardless of whether such damages were foreseeable") that is not in the contract.

- **3. Force majeure fails on the contract language and the facts.** The quoted Section 11.7 leaves out the condition that the affected party "notifies the other within 72 hours," along with the written-notice requirements. Pinnacle's own facility manager testified that Greenfield was never notified. The record also shows mechanical failures (refrigerant leaks and a failed compressor), a refusal to replace the unit because it was "not in the budget," and a total failure in January 2023, when there was no summer heat. The summer heat was only 2–4°F above average.

The mitigation and comparative-fault defenses leave out Sections 4.2, 4.5 and 7.4. Those sections make Pinnacle's reporting duty independent of Greenfield's inspection rights, and they state that a party has no duty to discover a breach the other party failed to disclose. Pinnacle's own facility manager testified that the November 2022–January 2023 reports were withheld on the Regional VP's instruction because "the numbers weren't good." Pinnacle's damages expert, Ellington, gives three different incremental-cost figures and three different salvage percentages. He admits he has no expertise in mold remediation and defers to our industrial hygienist on salvageability. His biography also differs between his declaration and his deposition. The record citations are unreliable throughout: the brief's SUMF paragraph numbers systematically do not match the filed SUMF, and several declaration citations point to paragraphs that say something else.

Recommendation: oppose on all grounds; file Rule 56(c)(2) evidentiary objections; file a Rule 702 motion to exclude Ellington's salvageability and mitigation opinions; and fix the handful of weaknesses in our own record (Section VI) before filing. Before quoting any contract language to the Court, confirm that the LSA we reviewed is the final executed version and check whether Pinnacle attached a different version to the Overstreet Declaration. The discrepancies are large enough that we should rule out a competing-version dispute before accusing Pinnacle of misquotation.

# III. Critical Issues (Dispositive)

## Issue 1 — The Liability Cap (§ 7.1) Is Misquoted and Does Not Apply as Pinnacle Claims

Pinnacle's quotation (MSJ at IV.B):

*"In no event shall Pinnacle's aggregate liability to Greenfield under this Agreement, whether in contract, tort, or otherwise, exceed the total fees paid by Greenfield to Pinnacle during the twelve (12) months preceding the date on which the claim first arose."*

Executed LSA § 7.1:

*"Pinnacle's aggregate liability under this Agreement shall not exceed the greater of (a) the total fees paid by Greenfield to Pinnacle during the twelve (12) months preceding the claim, or (b) \$2,000,000. This limitation shall not apply to claims arising from Pinnacle's gross negligence or willful misconduct."*

Weaknesses and errors:

- **\$2,000,000 floor omitted.** Even on Pinnacle's own theory, the cap can never be lower than \$2,000,000. The brief's \$1,860,000 figure is wrong as a matter of contract.

- **Gross negligence / willful misconduct carve-out omitted.** The second sentence of § 7.1 and § 7.2(b) both remove gross negligence and willful misconduct claims from the cap. The Holt testimony (Issue 9) supports both.

- **§ 7.2 exclusions omitted entirely.** (a) Pinnacle's indemnification obligations under § 9.1 are uncapped. § 9.1(a)–(b) covers "any and all Losses" arising from breach of §§ 4.1–4.6 and from contamination of Greenfield Inventory in Pinnacle's custody, including attorneys' fees. (d) The cap does not apply to "claims for which Pinnacle is required to maintain insurance under Article 6." § 6.1(b) requires property insurance "covering all goods and inventory stored at the Facility, including Greenfield Inventory, for not less than the full replacement value thereof." The \$4.7M inventory claim falls within § 7.2(d) on its face. The brief never mentions § 7.2 or Article 6.

- **Language added.** "Whether in contract, tort, or otherwise" and "first arose" do not appear in § 7.1. The tort language matters because Pinnacle uses it to extend the cap to Count II.

- **Inconsistent 12-month period and figures.** The MSJ uses February 2022–January 2023. SUMF ¶ 14 uses July 2022–June 2023, a period that runs after the February 10, 2023 demand. SUMF ¶ 15 concedes that Greenfield withheld the January and February 2023 fees, so "fees paid" in either window could not be twelve full payments of \$155,000. Ellington ¶ 28 describes \$1,860,000 as fees paid "over the term of the engagement," but the relationship lasted about 25 months (roughly \$3.9M at \$155K/month). Section 5.4 annual fee adjustments, and Okafor Decl. ¶ 2 ("subject to periodic adjustment"), could also change the figure. The cap arithmetic is internally contradictory and unsupported.

- **Unsupported "negotiated by counsel" assertion.** The brief says "both parties were represented by counsel during the negotiation of the LSA. SUMF ¶ 5." SUMF ¶ 5 describes the Warner Robins Facility. Nothing in the record supports the claim of negotiation with counsel.

**Response:**Opposition approach: Quote the full §§ 7.1–7.2 and Article 6. Argue that (i) the inventory and remediation claims are uncapped under § 7.2(d); (ii) all Losses within § 9.1 are uncapped under § 7.2(a); (iii) gross negligence and willful misconduct raise jury questions; and (iv) at an absolute minimum, the floor is \$2M. Georgia law construes limitation-of-liability clauses strictly against the party seeking protection. Confirm Georgia authority on strict construction of exculpatory clauses and on public-policy limits for gross negligence before citing.

## Issue 2 — The Consequential-Damages Waiver (§ 7.3) Is Misquoted and Carves Out § 4.2 Breaches

Pinnacle's quotation (MSJ at IV.C; SUMF ¶ 12): "Neither party shall be liable ... for any indirect, incidental, special, or consequential damages arising out of or related to this Agreement, regardless of the form of action, whether in contract, tort, or otherwise, and regardless of whether such damages were foreseeable."

*Executed LSA § 7.3: "Neither party shall be liable for any indirect, incidental, special, or consequential damages. This waiver shall not apply to claims arising from a party's gross negligence, willful misconduct, or breach of Section 4.2."*

- **The § 4.2 carve-out is outcome-determinative.** Greenfield's core claim is breach of § 4.2: humidity above 60%, and failure to provide monthly environmental compliance reports. Pinnacle's own SUMF concedes both elements (¶ 25 no reports for Nov. 2022–Jan. 2023; ¶ 36 readings of 72–85% RH; ¶ 37 months of sustained exceedance). The waiver does not reach these damages at all.

- **Invented text.** "Arising out of or related to," "regardless of the form of action," "tort, strict liability" and "regardless of whether ... foreseeable" are not in the executed § 7.3. The brief then argues that the "mutual nature" of the clause shows it is fair and unambiguous, which is an argument built on text that does not exist.

- **Characterization problems.** Even if the waiver applied, expedited replacement manufacturing is arguably a direct cost of replacing destroyed goods (cover). Our CIH treats it as a direct consequence (Venkatesh Rpt. § 6.3). Ellington's own declaration table concedes \$600,000–\$750,000 of expedited manufacturing as recoverable (Ellington Decl. ¶ 27), which contradicts the brief's claim that all \$1.15M is barred. Ellington's deposition statement that these costs are "consequential" (Dep. 46) is a legal conclusion, and he admits recoverability "would depend on the contractual terms and applicable law."

- **Misattributed expert support.** The brief cites Ellington Decl. ¶ 12 for the claim that expedited costs include replacement of panels at "the end of their useful shelf life." Neither ¶ 12 nor any other paragraph of the declaration says that. The brief cites Ellington ¶¶ 10–12 for lost-profit speculation, but the lost-profit opinions are at ¶¶ 23–24.

## Issue 4 — Mitigation / Inspection-Rights Theory Contradicts the Contract and the Record

Pinnacle's mitigation argument (MSJ IV.D) and its comparative-fault argument (IV.E) both depend on Greenfield's failure to use § 4.5. The brief leaves out three provisions that answer that argument directly:

- **§ 4.2:** Pinnacle's reporting obligation "is an affirmative reporting obligation and shall not be conditioned upon or limited by Greenfield's exercise of its inspection rights under Section 4.5."

- **§ 4.5:** Greenfield's inspection right "is in addition to, and not in lieu of, Pinnacle's affirmative obligations to provide reports and notifications."

- **§ 7.4 (Duty to Mitigate):** Mitigation requires only steps that are "commercially reasonable under the circumstances then known," and "\[n\]othing in this Section shall impose upon a Party the obligation to discover a breach that the other Party has concealed or failed to disclose in accordance with its reporting or notification obligations."

Record contradictions:

- **Deliberate withholding.** Holt testified that the reports were not sent "\[b\]ecause the numbers weren't good and I was told to hold off" by Okafor. Okafor told him in early November 2022, "No point in raising alarms before we have a solution," and in December, "Not yet, let's get through the holidays" (Holt Dep. 65–68, 99). Readings were "routinely in the upper 60s, sometimes in the 70s." This is concealment within the meaning of § 7.4.

- **Greenfield did follow up.** Santos emailed Holt in early December and again in January about the missing reports. Holt told him they were "coming soon" (Santos Dep. 33–38). This contradicts Ellington ¶ 20 ("Greenfield did not request the missing reports") and the brief's description of Greenfield as passive.

- **The reports Greenfield had showed compliance.** Every report Greenfield received, through October 2022, showed conditions within specification (Santos Dep. 33–38). Yet our CIH places initial colonization in June–August 2022, Holt recorded 78% RH on October 3, and Holt testified that building sensor data became "unreliable" whenever Unit 3 was down (Dep. 30). Holt could not say whether the October report disclosed the 78% reading (Dep. 58). The reports Greenfield relied on appear to have been inaccurate. That point strongly rebuts "Greenfield should have known" and supports bad faith.

- **"Access at all times" is false.** SUMF ¶ 23 and the brief say Greenfield "had access to environmental monitoring data at all times." § 4.5 requires 48 hours' written notice during business hours. Pinnacle itself claims the monitoring equipment had "calibration" problems (Okafor ¶ 7), and Holt had no reliable sensors during outages. Data that Pinnacle says was unreliable cannot have been available "at all times."

- **Santos visits were not inspections.** SUMF ¶ 30 concedes that Santos never gave 48-hour notice. His visits were logistics visits, not § 4.5 inspections (Santos Dep. 24–29). Santos has no HVAC training (Dep. 12–15). Pinnacle cannot treat these visits as missed inspections.

- **Visit timing.** The brief says Santos visited during the "period of alleged HVAC failures." With quarterly visits, the unit may well have been running on a patch when he was there, and the warehouse "always looked clean and organized."

- **Post-discovery "delay."** Pinnacle had custody of the goods. Ellington faults the four-day gap before the demand letter and the gap before the Feb. 15–17 inspection (Decl. ¶ 21), but Pinnacle controlled the facility and still had deployed no dehumidifiers when our CIH inspected (Venkatesh ¶ 3.7).

- **Remedy overstated.** Failure to mitigate reduces damages; it is not a complete bar, yet the brief asks the Court to "bar" recovery. Reasonableness of mitigation is classically a jury question.

- **Unfiled testimony.** The intro and IV.D rely on Hargrove's statement that Greenfield "trusted Pinnacle to do its job" (Hargrove Dep. 112:7-14; see also 115:3-9, 117:2-11). The SUMF does not cite those lines (SUMF cites Hargrove only at 12, 14, 55, 58, 72), and no Hargrove excerpts are in the materials we received. Confirm whether they were filed. If not, object under Rule 56(c)(1)(A) and (c)(2). Hargrove's cited testimony (SUMF ¶ 27) actually confirms that Greenfield reasonably relied on the contractual reports.

## Issue 6 — Comparative Fault Is Misapplied

- **O.C.G.A. § 51-12-33 does not apply to contract claims.** Georgia's apportionment statute governs tort actions for injury to person or property. It is not a defense to breach of contract (Count I) or to breach of the implied covenant (Count III). The brief asks for SJ on "all of its claims" on this ground.

- **No duty identified.** The LSA allocated monitoring to Pinnacle (§ 4.2 "install and maintain commercially reasonable monitoring equipment"; § 3.4 industry standard of care for moisture-sensitive components; Exhibit B ¶ 5). No contract term or duty required Greenfield to install its own sensors or to negotiate real-time data access.

- **Apportionment is for the jury.** Allocating percentages of fault is a jury function. The request that the Court "find as a matter of law" a 50%-or-greater share has no support in the record.

- **Unfiled support.** The claim that Greenfield chose not to deploy sensors rests on Hargrove Dep. 115:3-9 and 117:2-11, which are not in the SUMF and appear not to be in the record.

- **Authority problem.** S. Gen. Ins. Co. v. Holt, 262 Ga. 267 (1992) is, to our understanding, an insurer failure-to-settle / bad-faith case, not an apportionment case. Denton v. Eaton Corp. also needs verification. See Issue 12.

## Issue 9 — Evidence of Gross Negligence / Willful Misconduct (Removes Cap and Waiver)

Pinnacle does not address this carve-out in §§ 7.1, 7.2(b) and 7.3. The record supports a jury finding:

- Knowing refusal, for budget reasons, to replace a failing unit protecting about \$4.7M of moisture-sensitive inventory after the facility manager warned in writing about humidity risk to "Greenfield's panels" (Aug. 9 email).

- Deliberate suppression of three months of compliance reports because they would show non-compliance, with renewed instructions to keep withholding (Holt Dep. 65–68, 99).

- Holt telling Santos the reports were "coming soon" while they were being withheld (Santos Dep. 33–38).

- No notice of four separate HVAC events, including a complete failure with no climate control for at least 2.5–3 weeks (Holt Dep. 83–85), despite § 4.3 (24-hour notice) and Exhibit B ¶ 5.

- No dehumidification at any point, and no approval of Holt's January request to rent dehumidifiers (Holt Dep. 83).

The same evidence supports Count III (implied covenant), bad-faith litigation expenses under O.C.G.A. § 13-6-11, and potentially a fraudulent-concealment theory. Gross negligence and willfulness are ordinarily jury questions.

## Issue 10 — Gaps: Claims and Contract Provisions Pinnacle Does Not Address

- **No argument on breach.** Pinnacle concedes the HVAC issues ("does not dispute") and never argues that it complied with § 4.2. Its SUMF concedes the humidity exceedances and the missing reports. At most it seeks partial relief on damages; there is no basis for judgment on Count I.

- **Counts II and III.** The brief asks for judgment on all three counts but offers no element-by-element argument on negligence or the implied covenant. The good-faith claim is supported by the concealment evidence. Note: Pinnacle has not raised the economic loss rule or duplication between tort and contract claims; expect it on reply and be ready to show an independent duty (bailment, § 3.4 standard of care).

- **Ignored LSA provisions.** § 3.4 (industry standard of care for moisture-sensitive components); § 4.3 (backup climate control; 24-hour HVAC-failure notice; preventive maintenance records); § 4.4 (air-circulation spacing); § 9.1 (indemnification including attorneys' fees; uncapped per § 7.2(a)); § 10.2(c) (HVAC adequacy warranty); § 6.1(b) (replacement-value insurance); Exhibit B ¶¶ 5–7 (24-hour excursion notice; service credits; non-exclusive remedies). All of these are unaddressed.

- **Attorneys' fees.** § 9.1 expressly includes attorneys' fees for breach of §§ 4.1–4.6. The brief does not address Greenfield's contractual fee claim. Note the risk that a court reads § 9.1 as limited to third-party claims; research Georgia law on first-party indemnification before relying on it heavily.

# V. Medium- and Low-Priority Issues

## Issue 11 — Damages Challenges Raise Fact Disputes, Not Grounds for SJ

- **Overhead allocation.** Whether fixed overhead is excluded depends on the proper measure of damages for destroyed finished goods (market value or replacement cost versus variable cost). § 6.1(b) contemplates "full replacement value." Greenfield actually incurred replacement costs. Ellington's math is internally consistent in the declaration (\$1,680 + \$860.54 = \$2,540.54; 1,850 × \$1,680 = \$3,108,000), but his three different figures undermine reliability. At most this is a dispute over the measure of damages for trial.

- **Salvage.** Venkatesh (with IICRC S520 and EPA support) says no panels were salvageable. That is a direct expert conflict, and summary judgment cannot resolve it.

- **Remediation (\$385K).** The brief says remediation should fall with salvage; Ellington "do\[es\] not directly challenge the reasonableness" of the rates (Decl. ¶ 26). His reduced figures (\$120K–\$160K) depend entirely on his mitigation scenario.

- **Lost profits (\$890K).** Waiver argument fails (Issue 2). "Reasonable certainty" is a fact question. Greenfield has three identified builder contracts (Tri-County, Peach State, Heritage Communities). The brief's claim that Greenfield's estimates rely on "historical averages and assumptions about customer retention" has no record citation.

- **Linden fees (\$175K).** The brief cites O.C.G.A. § 13-6-5, which is Georgia's mitigation statute. The litigation-expense statute is § 13-6-11, and bad faith under it is supported by the concealment evidence. Fees may also be recoverable as "Losses" under § 9.1. Pre-suit investigation of cause is arguably a direct cost. (See also Issue 12 on the mitigation cite to § 13-6-8.)
