# I. Questions Presented

1.  Which state’s law will govern the effort by the *Delgado* plaintiffs and the Illinois EPA to disregard Pryor Manufacturing LLC’s separate existence and impose Pryor’s liabilities on Cascade Industrial Holdings, Inc.?

2.  How do the veil-piercing standards of Delaware, Ohio, Texas, and Illinois apply to the documented Cascade–Pryor relationship, and what is Cascade’s calibrated risk under each?

3.  Independent of veil piercing, is Cascade exposed to direct liability (a) as designer of the HP-4400 under Texas products-liability law and (b) as an “operator” of the Canton Facility under *United States v. Bestfoods*?

4.  How do insurance, Pryor’s balance sheet, and procedural posture affect realistic exposure, and what remediation should the Board authorize now?

# II. Short Answers and Executive Summary

**1. Choice of law is less of a lever than the preliminary assessment suggests — and Delaware law is very unlikely to apply.** The question in both proceedings is whether the separate existence of *Pryor* (an Ohio LLC) should be disregarded. Texas’s Business Organizations Code directs that the law of an entity’s jurisdiction of formation governs its internal affairs, expressly including the liability of an owner or member for the entity’s obligations (Tex. Bus. Orgs. Code §§ 1.101–1.105). Illinois conflicts practice likewise looks to the law of the state of organization of the entity whose veil is at issue. Both routes point to **Ohio**, not Delaware. Delaware law would matter only if someone sought to reach *Cascade’s* shareholders, which no one is attempting. We recommend abandoning the “Delaware first” position in the November 22 preliminary assessment and leading with Ohio law, with Texas law as the fallback the court may apply.

**2. The veil-piercing facts are poor under any test; the outcome turns on the “wrong” element.** On the control/unity prong, the record is about as unfavorable as it gets: no independent governance ever, dual officers, a \$50,000 capex threshold, Cascade branding on the product and the facility, unpriced shared services, a sweep agreement that lets Cascade take all cash with no reserve, and \$41.2 million of undocumented “debt.” Cascade’s defensible ground is the second element. Ohio (*Dombroski v. WellPoint*, 2008) requires that control be used to commit **fraud, an illegal act, or a similarly unlawful act**, which is a meaningful hurdle for a product-defect claim. Texas tort veil piercing is more forgiving to plaintiffs, and the Texas single business enterprise (SBE) theory is weak after *SSP Partners*. **Our ratings:** Ohio law, *Delgado* = Moderate. Texas law, *Delgado* (alter ego) = Moderate-High. Texas SBE = Low. IEPA alter ego (any law) = Moderate-High, because RCRA violations are themselves “illegal acts.”

**3. Direct liability is the more serious exposure, and it does not depend on choice of law or on piercing.** Cascade’s engineering group designed the HP-4400 and specified the interlock (EDS-HP4400-Rev3). Cascade’s VP of Engineering approved the design and, on July 19, 2022, overruled a written safety objection that proposed a costed alternative design (\$2,800/unit, about 8 weeks). He cited delivery commitments the CEO had prioritized. Texas defines “manufacturer” to include a person who *designs* a product (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 82.001(4)). **Direct design liability risk: High.** **Exemplary damages risk: Moderate-High.** In the IEPA matter, the Whitfield Memorandum is a facility-specific directive on how hazardous waste would be stored, issued by a parent officer who holds no Pryor office. That is close to the fact pattern *Bestfoods* identified as supporting direct operator liability. **Operator risk on the merits: High.** This is subject to the jurisdictional defense noted next.

**4. The IEPA action has a threshold jurisdictional defect that the preliminary assessment did not identify.** The Illinois EPA seeks penalties for *storage* at a facility in **Canton, Ohio**, under Illinois permit provisions (415 ILCS 5/21(d)). Ohio, not Illinois, is the RCRA-authorized state for that facility. Illinois’s only apparent hook is the manifested shipments received by a TSDF in Peoria (Count II). Counts I, III and IV, to the extent they rest on Ohio storage, should be challenged for lack of jurisdiction and authority before any merits positions are taken. **Urgent:** the Complaint states a 45-day answer period. If service occurred on or near October 3, 2024, that deadline may already have run. Default status must be confirmed immediately.

**5. Insurance and finances.** Pryor’s combined Ridgeway Mutual limits are \$20 million per occurrence against an \$18.5 million demand. The \$2.4 million IEPA penalty is uninsured. The risk memo’s assumption that direct design claims fall outside Cascade’s additional-insured status is not obvious, because “arising out of” is typically read broadly. The larger risk is that **punitive damages may be uninsurable if Ohio law governs the policies** (Ohio R.C. 3937.182). The record also contains several numerical errors that opponents are already exploiting (Part III.C). For example, the Petition’s “\$49.5 million” annual extraction double-counts \$17.9 million. And the widely repeated “\$4.2 million equity / 9.81:1” figure ignores Pryor’s \$21.7 million of total member’s equity.

**6. Remediation should be forward-looking, sequenced, and litigation-aware.** Priorities:

- **Safety first:** an HP-4400 field-safety and retrofit program, together with post-sale warnings.

- **Separate counsel:** separate counsel for Pryor and, potentially, for Orvis.

- **Recapitalization:** recapitalize Pryor with equity, not debt, and amend the sweep agreement to impose a minimum reserve.

- **Governance:** create an independent Pryor board of managers.

- **Documentation:** document intercompany debt and services on arm’s-length terms.

- **Separateness:** separate branding and environmental authority.

None of these steps cures 2022–2024 conduct. Each should be implemented under counsel’s supervision so that it does not create admissions or spoliation risk.

# IV. Choice of Law

## A. Framework in the Texas Forum

Texas generally resolves conflicts under the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws “most significant relationship” test (*Hughes Wood Prods., Inc. v. Wagner*, 18 S.W.3d 202 (Tex. 2000); *Torrington Co. v. Stutzman*, 46 S.W.3d 829 (Tex. 2000)). The analysis is conducted **issue by issue**. For veil piercing, however, Texas has a statutory rule that largely displaces the common-law inquiry:

- **Tex. Bus. Orgs. Code § 1.102:** the law of the jurisdiction of formation governs a foreign entity.

- **§ 1.104:** that law governs the entity’s formation and internal affairs.

- **§ 1.105:** “internal affairs” include “the liability of an owner, member, or managerial official for an obligation of the entity.”

Whether Cascade, as Pryor’s sole member, is liable for Pryor’s obligations is therefore governed by **Ohio law**, as Pryor’s jurisdiction of formation. This is consistent with Restatement § 307 and the internal-affairs doctrine. Federal courts applying Texas law have generally followed the state-of-organization rule for veil piercing. *(Counsel should compile and cite-check current Texas appellate authority applying §§ 1.104–1.105 to veil piercing before briefing.)*

## B. Why the “Delaware First” Strategy Should Be Abandoned

The preliminary assessment recommends arguing for Delaware law because Cascade is a Delaware corporation. We disagree:

- **Wrong entity.** The internal-affairs doctrine applies the law of the entity *whose* veil is to be pierced (Pryor), not the law of the parent seeking protection.

- **Irrelevant target.** Delaware law would govern only an effort to reach Cascade’s stockholders.

- **Credibility cost.** Advancing an untenable position risks credibility at the threshold of the case, and the payoff is small. Ohio’s post-*Dombroski* standard is itself among the more demanding in the country, requiring fraud, an illegal act, or a similarly unlawful act.

We recommend briefing Ohio law as the primary position. Delaware’s standard (discussed in Part V.A) can be noted only as confirming a broad interstate consensus that veil piercing is exceptional.

## C. Plaintiffs’ Counter-Arguments and Our Response

Plaintiffs will make three arguments:

5.  Veil piercing in a *tort* case is a remedial question on which the place of injury (Texas) has the dominant interest.

6.  § 1.105 addresses internal affairs, not liability to involuntary third-party tort creditors.

7.  Texas public policy (Texas residents injured in Texas) should override.

The statutory text answers the first two points directly, because it expressly covers “liability of an owner \[or\] member for an obligation of the entity” without distinguishing contract from tort. On the third, a public-policy override requires a fundamental conflict, and Ohio law is not repugnant to Texas policy. Texas itself restricts veil piercing by statute (§§ 21.223–21.224, extended to LLCs by § 101.002).

**Realistic assessment:** we estimate a 60–70% probability that the court applies Ohio law to the alter-ego claim. The marginal benefit over Texas law is real but not dispositive, because (i) the SBE theory is weak under Texas law anyway, and (ii) the direct-liability claims will be governed by Texas tort law regardless.

# V. Jurisdiction-by-Jurisdiction Veil-Piercing Analysis

## A. Delaware

**Standard.** Delaware disregards the entity only in exceptional circumstances. It requires a showing that the entities operated as a single economic entity *and* an overall element of injustice or unfairness, typically fraud or something like it (*Wallace ex rel. Cencom Cable Income Partners II v. Wood*, 752 A.2d 1175 (Del. Ch. 1999); *Mobil Oil Corp. v. Linear Films, Inc.*, 718 F. Supp. 260 (D. Del. 1989)). Relevant factors include:

- undercapitalization;

- solvency;

- observance of formalities;

- siphoning of funds by the dominant owner; and

- whether the entity is a façade.

Mere domination, or the ordinary incidents of 100% ownership, is not enough.

**Application.** Even under Delaware law the single-economic-entity prong would likely be satisfied here. The defense would rest on the injustice prong: Pryor is solvent, insured to \$20 million, and profitable, and the injury did not result from the corporate form being used to shield Cascade from a known creditor. The systematic cash sweep weighs against Cascade because it is a “siphoning” factor.

**Relevance.** As explained in Part IV.B, Delaware law is unlikely to govern. **Risk if applied: Low-Moderate.**

## B. Ohio (Probable Governing Law)

**Standard.** Ohio applies the three-part test of *Belvedere Condominium Unit Owners’ Ass’n v. R.E. Roark Cos.*, 67 Ohio St.3d 274 (1993), as narrowed by *Dombroski v. WellPoint, Inc.*, 119 Ohio St.3d 506, 2008-Ohio-4827. The claimant must prove:

8.  control over the entity so complete that it had no separate mind, will, or existence of its own;

9.  that control was exercised to commit **fraud, an illegal act, or a similarly unlawful act** against the claimant; and

10. injury or unjust loss resulted.

*Dombroski* expressly rejected the looser “unjust or inequitable” formulation. **The preliminary assessment’s description of the second prong as “wrongful or unjust act” reflects pre-2008 law and should be corrected.** Ohio courts apply the same test to LLCs.

**Ohio Revised LLC Act.** Ohio’s Revised Limited Liability Company Act, R.C. Chapter 1706 (effective January 1, 2022), provides that a member is not liable for the LLC’s obligations solely by reason of being a member. It also provides that the failure to observe formalities is not a ground for imposing member liability. *(Confirm the pin cite, R.C. 1706.26, and any saving language for common-law piercing.)* Two consequences follow:

- It blunts the “no meetings, no minutes, no resolutions” facts. Ohio’s LLC statute never required them, and an operating agreement vesting all management in a sole member is expressly permitted.

- It does **not** neutralize undercapitalization, siphoning, commingled identity, or domination of specific decisions. Those facts go to the substance of the control prong, not to formalities.

**Application — Prong 1 (control): likely satisfied.** Cascade exercised operational control over the specific decisions at issue, including design, interlock specification, the environmental budget, and waste configuration. Cascade also sets Pryor’s fees and distributions unilaterally and can take all of Pryor’s cash with no reserve (Agreement §§ 2.2, 2.4, 5.3). Pryor’s “representations” concede that there is no independent governance and that the Agreement was not negotiated at arm’s length (§§ 6.1(e), 6.3). Cascade’s best points on this prong are limited:

- Pryor employs 312 people, runs two plants, and holds its own bank account and Ohio good standing.

- Centralized treasury, shared services, and common branding are commonplace within corporate groups.

**Application — Prong 2 (fraud/illegal act): the key battleground.**

- **Delgado:** a design-defect tort is not fraud, and negligent design is not an “illegal act” in the *Dombroski* sense. Plaintiffs will argue that knowingly shipping a press alleged to be non-compliant with OSHA 1910.217 was an unlawful act. Two responses undercut that argument:

<!-- -->

- 1910.217 regulates **employers**, not manufacturers.

- By its terms (§ 1910.217(a)(5)), 1910.217 does **not apply to hydraulic power presses**. The Orvis chain’s reference to a “full-revolution clutch” on a hydraulic press is technically anomalous and must be run to ground with engineering experts. The governing consensus standard is likely ANSI B11.2.

<!-- -->

- **Undercapitalization and siphoning theory:** plaintiffs will also argue that the sweep structure and distributions were an unlawful stripping of a tort-exposed subsidiary. That argument is weakened by Pryor’s solvency (\$21.7 million equity, 2.64x current ratio) and \$20 million of insurance.

- **IEPA:** this prong is much easier for the agency, because the alleged conduct is a statutory violation (unpermitted storage and false manifests), squarely an “illegal act.”

**Application — Prong 3 (causation/unjust loss).** In *Delgado*, plaintiffs must show that the control caused the injury. The design-control facts supply a direct causal link. The financial facts do so only if Pryor cannot pay. The IEPA pleads causation expressly: extraction left Pryor unable to fund compliance (¶ 62).

**Risk: Delgado — Moderate. IEPA — Moderate-High.**

## C. Texas

**1. Traditional alter ego.** Under *Castleberry v. Branscum*, 721 S.W.2d 270 (Tex. 1986), a court may disregard the corporate fiction where the entity is the alter ego of its owner, meaning there is such unity that separateness has ceased, *and* holding only the entity liable would result in injustice. For contract claims, the Legislature superseded *Castleberry*’s constructive-fraud standard: Tex. Bus. Orgs. Code § 21.223(b) requires **actual fraud primarily for the direct personal benefit** of the owner, and § 101.002 extends this to LLCs.

**Tort claims.** The preliminary assessment implies § 21.223 governs here. It likely does not. By its terms § 21.223 addresses contractual obligations and matters relating to them, and Texas courts have generally declined to apply its actual-fraud requirement to tort claims. For torts, the *Castleberry* formulation continues to apply, although Texas courts still describe veil piercing as exceptional and require that the entity have been used as a sham or to work an injustice. Under Texas law the *Delgado* alter-ego claim therefore faces a **lower** bar than under Ohio law. This is the core reason to press the choice-of-law motion.

**Application.** The unity factors Texas courts consider are satisfied on this record:

- common officers;

- parent payment of subsidiary expenses;

- inadequate capitalization;

- the subsidiary being treated as a division;

- use of the parent’s address and letterhead; and

- the parent directing subsidiary operations.

Cascade’s injustice-prong defense is that Pryor is solvent and insured. Plaintiffs will reply with the \$105 million cumulative net extraction, the absence of any reserve, and the risk that a verdict exceeds limits. **Risk: Moderate-High.**

**2. Single business enterprise.** In *SSP Partners v. Gladstrong Investments (USA) Corp.*, 275 S.W.3d 444 (Tex. 2008), the Supreme Court of Texas held that the SBE theory, as a basis for imposing one entity’s obligations on another without the fraud showing required by statute, **will not support the imposition of liability**. The court reasoned that the SBE factors describe ordinary features of affiliated groups. The preliminary assessment understates the holding by calling it mere skepticism. Most intermediate courts since then treat SBE as unavailable as an independent theory. Plaintiffs effectively concede the point by pleading it “for appellate review” (Pet. ¶ 42). **Recommendation:** move to dismiss the Fifth Cause of Action under Tex. R. Civ. P. 91a, or seek partial summary judgment. **Risk: Low.**

**3. Jurisdictional alter ego is a different and stricter test.** Imputing Pryor’s contacts to Cascade *for personal jurisdiction* requires a showing that the parent controls the internal business operations and affairs of the subsidiary beyond the norm of ownership, amounting to fusion (*PHC-Minden, L.P. v. Kimberly-Clark Corp.*, 235 S.W.3d 163 (Tex. 2007)). The more significant jurisdictional risk is **Cascade’s own conduct**: it designed a product for national distribution through authorized distributors, including in Texas. Under *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court*, 592 U.S. 351 (2021), and Texas “stream-of-commerce-plus” decisions (e.g., *Luciano v. SprayFoamPolymers.com, LLC*, 625 S.W.3d 1 (Tex. 2021)), a special appearance has modest prospects. It would also invite jurisdictional discovery into precisely the control facts plaintiffs need. **We recommend against a special appearance unless Birchfield identifies facts not in this record.** If one is filed, it must be filed **before any other plea**, including the answer and any choice-of-law motion, or it is waived (Tex. R. Civ. P. 120a).

## D. Illinois

**Standard.** Illinois pierces where there is (1) such unity of interest and ownership that separate personalities no longer exist, and (2) adherence to the fiction would sanction a fraud or promote injustice (*Fontana v. TLD Builders, Inc.*, 362 Ill. App. 3d 491 (2d Dist. 2005)). Illinois courts consider factors including:

- inadequate capitalization;

- failure to observe formalities;

- insolvency;

- siphoning;

- non-functioning officers; and

- use of the entity as a façade.

The “promote injustice” prong is broader than Ohio’s *Dombroski* prong.

**Choice of law within Illinois.** Illinois courts generally apply the law of the state of organization to veil piercing, which again points to Ohio for Pryor. The IEPA pleads a generic alter-ego test and does not engage choice of law. Cascade should insist on Ohio law.

**Application.** Under either the Illinois or the Ohio standard, the IEPA’s alter-ego theory is strong on the merits. The alleged wrong is itself illegal. Causation is plausibly pleaded: the budget denial saved \$160,000 a year while \$31.6 million was swept. And undercapitalization relative to compliance needs is the classic injustice. **Risk (if jurisdiction exists): Moderate-High.**

# VI. Direct Liability Theories (No Veil Piercing Required)

## A. Cascade as Designer of the HP-4400 (Texas Law)

**Statutory status.** Chapter 82 defines “manufacturer” to include a person who “designs, formulates, constructs, rebuilds, fabricates, produces, compounds, processes, or assembles any product” and places it in the stream of commerce (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 82.001(4)). The record establishes that Cascade’s engineering team designed the HP-4400 and specified the interlock. Pryor’s own GM wrote that Pryor “did not participate in the interlock design phase.” Cascade’s best counter-argument is that it did not itself place the product into the stream of commerce, because Pryor manufactured and sold it. That argument is weakened by several facts:

- the Cascade logo on the nameplate;

- Cascade letterhead on customer contracts;

- Cascade processing of warranty claims; and

- Choi’s direction to “proceed” with production.

These facts also support an apparent-manufacturer argument (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 400; Restatement (Third) of Torts: Prods. Liab. § 14).

**Design defect elements (§ 82.005).** Plaintiffs must prove a safer alternative design that would have prevented or significantly reduced the risk of injury without substantially impairing utility, and that was economically and technologically feasible when the product left the manufacturer’s control. They must also prove producing cause. The Orvis chain is close to a ready-made exhibit on these elements:

- a redundant relay monitoring circuit;

- priced by Cascade itself at \$4,200/unit, and by Pryor at \$2,800/unit, on a \$387,000 machine (about 0.7–1.1% of price);

- achievable in 8–16 weeks; and

- the “existing relay architecture from the HP-3800” as a basis for the redesign.

Defense themes include:

- the interlock track record (more than 200 units without incident);

- SVP-400 validation up to 45 spm;

- Choi’s documented concern that a redundant circuit could introduce new PLC handshake failure modes;

- the non-applicability of 1910.217 to hydraulic presses;

- Garza’s maintenance and workplace-safety responsibility; and

- the possibility of misuse, bypass, or modification of the guard in the 20 months after sale, which requires immediate forensic inspection of the unit.

**Negligence.** Cascade owed a duty of reasonable care in design. The same facts support breach. A parent that affirmatively undertakes design of a subsidiary’s product is not shielded by the parent-subsidiary relationship. Liability attaches for its own conduct.

**Exemplary damages.** Gross negligence requires proof by clear and convincing evidence of objective extreme risk plus subjective awareness and conscious indifference (§§ 41.001(11), 41.003). A corporation is liable for exemplary damages for acts of a vice-principal, and Choi (a corporate VP with authority over design) likely qualifies. Plaintiffs’ most dangerous evidence consists of four points:

- written notice of an amputation hazard;

- a costed fix;

- a decision justified partly by schedule pressure attributed to the CEO; and

- silence from the legal department after being copied.

**Cap.** Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 41.008(b) limits exemplary damages to the greater of (i) twice economic damages plus non-economic damages up to \$750,000, or (ii) \$200,000. On the pleaded \$11.0 million in economic damages, the cap is about **\$22.0 million plus up to \$750,000**, far above the \$7.5 million bucket pleaded. The cap does not apply to certain felonies (§ 41.008(c)), and none appears implicated. **Risk: Moderate-High.**

**Proportionate responsibility.** Chapter 33 permits submission of Garza (and Lone Star, if appropriate) for a percentage of responsibility. If Garza is a workers’-compensation subscriber, the exclusive-remedy bar (Tex. Lab. Code § 408.001) likely defeats plaintiffs’ direct claim against it. Garza can nonetheless be designated as a responsible third party.

**Individual exposure.** Texas holds corporate agents personally liable for their own torts (*Leitch v. Hornsby*, 935 S.W.2d 114 (Tex. 1996)). Choi, and potentially Dalworth, could be added as defendants. The D&O policy’s subsidiary-product exclusion may leave them uninsured for indemnity, which creates conflicts that must be managed now (Part VII).

**Interaction with veil piercing.** Direct liability makes the veil-piercing fight largely academic as to *liability* in *Delgado*. It remains important for (i) the IEPA matter, (ii) future HP-4400 claimants whose units may not trace to Choi’s decision in the same way, and (iii) discovery scope and jury narrative. **The preliminary assessment’s question whether \*Bestfoods\* oversight defenses carry over to products liability is answered largely in the negative.** *Bestfoods* protects a parent’s *oversight*, and it confirms direct liability when the parent itself conducts the harm-producing activity. Designing the product is that activity.

## B. Cascade as “Operator” of the Canton Facility (IEPA Count III)

**Threshold defense — jurisdiction and authority.** The Illinois Act and Illinois’s authorized RCRA program govern hazardous-waste activity within Illinois. Ohio EPA administers the authorized program for Ohio facilities (R.C. Chapter 3734). Several consequences follow:

- The Complaint’s storage counts invoke 415 ILCS 5/21(d), which requires a permit “granted by the Agency,” for storage in Canton, Ohio.

- Neither RCRA § 3006 nor the Illinois Act appears to authorize Illinois to penalize storage at an Ohio facility.

- The only Illinois nexus pleaded is the receipt of manifested shipments in Peoria.

**Recommendations:**

11. Challenge Counts I, III and IV to the extent predicated on storage, for lack of jurisdiction.

12. Challenge the Agency’s authority to impose a civil penalty administratively. Under the Illinois Act, penalties are generally imposed by the Pollution Control Board or a circuit court on complaint by the Attorney General or a State’s Attorney (see 415 ILCS 5/31, 5/42), not by the Agency itself. *Illinois environmental counsel should confirm the procedural posture.*

13. Assess and prepare for derivative exposure: Ohio EPA or U.S. EPA may pursue the storage violations. Positions taken now must be consistent with that likely next proceeding.

**Merits — the \*Bestfoods\* standard.** *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51 (1998), distinguishes derivative liability (veil piercing) from direct “operator” liability. A parent operates a facility when it manages, directs, or conducts operations *specifically related to pollution*, such as the leakage or disposal of hazardous waste or decisions about compliance with environmental regulations. The Court also adopted a presumption that dual officers act on behalf of the subsidiary when acting in their subsidiary capacity. It singled out as potentially decisive the involvement of a *parent agent who held no position with the subsidiary* in the facility’s environmental affairs. Although *Bestfoods* construed CERCLA, courts and agencies apply its operator analysis to RCRA and to parallel state statutes. Applied to each Cascade actor:

- **Whitfield:** holds no Pryor office and issued a facility-specific storage directive. This is the paradigm identified in *Bestfoods*. **Strong for the Agency.**

- **Yun:** a Pryor authorized officer. Cascade can invoke the dual-officer presumption and argue that the budget denial was a Pryor act, and that budget-setting is classic parental oversight. The Agency will respond that Yun acted through “Cascade’s internal budget approval process” (Complaint ¶ 37), as Cascade CFO. **Mixed.**

- **The \$50,000 capex threshold and centralized environmental department:** general policy-setting, which is not alone operator conduct. Combined with the above, it supports the claim. **Supportive.**

**Risk on the merits: High** (if jurisdiction exists).

## C. Additional Theories to Anticipate

- **Fraudulent transfer.** The Uniform Voidable Transactions Act as adopted in Ohio (R.C. Chapter 1336) and in Texas (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code Ch. 24) reaches transfers made without reasonably equivalent value while the debtor was engaging in business with unreasonably small assets, or intended to incur debts beyond its ability to pay. Sweeps and distributions made after July 2022 (knowledge of the interlock risk) and after April 2023 (knowledge of Canton non-compliance) are the most exposed. Pryor’s book solvency is a defense, but contingent liabilities count.

- **Recharacterization and equitable subordination** of the \$41.2 million “debt” in any Pryor insolvency.

- **Responsible corporate officer** doctrine for individual environmental exposure (Whitfield, Yun).

- **Post-sale duty to warn / retrofit.** Texas generally does not impose a common-law post-sale duty to recall. However, continued sale of HP-4400s after the Delgado incident without a fix would be powerful exemplary-damages evidence in future cases.
