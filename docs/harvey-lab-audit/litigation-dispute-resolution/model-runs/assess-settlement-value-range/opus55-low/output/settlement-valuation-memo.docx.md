Why settle now: (1) the October 2019 Reedley/Mitchum emails make a majority-fault finding against Greenfield more likely than not and put punitive damages squarely before the jury; (2) punitive damages cannot be insured in Ohio, so trial converts a fully insured risk into an uninsured one for the Company; (3) a settlement inside the \$10M Ridgeline primary layer costs the Company nothing out of pocket, because the \$250,000 SIR is already exhausted; and (4) a public trial built around the internal emails is the main threat to the Halcyon contract. That last factor stays inside this memo and must not be mentioned in mediation.

Three corrections to the current defense position that the Committee should know before setting authority:

- **Non-economic cap.** The defense mediation brief argues that Mr. Holt's non-economic damages are capped at \$350,000 under R.C. 2315.18. That is wrong on these facts. The statute removes the cap for "permanent and substantial physical deformity, loss of use of a limb, or loss of a bodily organ system" (R.C. 2315.18(B)(3)). A traumatic below-elbow amputation of the dominant arm falls squarely inside that exception. The brief's entire \$1.8M–\$2.5M valuation depends on the cap. Before a retired judge, that argument will cost us credibility and should be removed.

- **Dr. Cho's arithmetic.** The defense economist's \$1,687,200 total leaves out her own fringe-benefit loss. Her summary table labels \$563,700 as "Future Lost Earning Capacity incl. Fringe Benefits," but that is the wage figure alone. Her fringe stream (\$7,788/yr × 24 yrs at 3.5%) has a present value of about \$125,000, which puts her corrected total at about \$1.81M. Plaintiff's counsel will find this.

- **Standards compliance.** Kwan Opinion 1 says the GP-7500 complied with ANSI B11.2-2013. Greenfield's own lead design engineer wrote in 2019 that it did not (GF-DOC-004471–73, -004476–78). Dr. Kwan says a single-channel interlock is allowed "when supported by an appropriate risk assessment," but the VP of Engineering wrote "I do not think we need a separate risk assessment at this time" (GF-DOC-004479). Standards compliance is a weak defense in this record.

# III. Liability Assessment

## A. Design Defect — Plaintiffs Are Likely to Establish It

We rate the probability of a design-defect finding at roughly 80–85%. The drivers:

- **Internal admissions.** Reedley's Oct. 7 and Oct. 11, 2019 emails say the single-valve architecture "does not meet" ANSI B11.2-2013 control-reliability requirements. He calls those provisions "not merely advisory." He predicts "a crush amputation." He warns that fixing the problem after an incident "could be seen as an acknowledgment that the original design was deficient." A jury will read these as admissions by Greenfield's own engineer. They also directly contradict the brief's argument that the redundancy language is a "should," not a "shall."

- **Risk-utility (R.C. 2307.75(B)–(F)).** The alternative design was cheap (≈0.27% of price), simple, and later adopted. Under Fed. R. Evid. 407, the post-incident ECO is generally inadmissible to prove defect. But the 2019 emails are pre-incident and fully admissible, and they establish feasibility and cost on their own. If Greenfield disputes feasibility, or keeps describing the change as "routine continuous improvement" (as the defense brief does), it risks opening the door to the ECO for feasibility or impeachment. We recommend conceding feasibility.

- **Defense expert gap.** Dr. Kwan does not analyze the valve failure, the field reports, or the emails, even though he lists them as materials reviewed. That leaves him open to a "no root-cause analysis" attack, and possibly to a Daubert challenge by Plaintiffs.

## B. Causation and the Apex Modification — Our Strongest Issue, But Not a Complete Defense

The guard swap is real and helps us. Briggs admits he removed the OEM guard for production convenience. He did not contact Greenfield or ShieldTech, "eyeballed" the gap, and gave Holt only an informal orientation. Kwan found the ShieldTech guard was not wired to the guard-in-place sensor, so the press could cycle with no guard at all. These facts support a large allocation to Apex. They are unlikely to produce a "sole proximate cause" defense verdict, for these reasons:

- **Unanswered workflow question.** Kwan says the 1" OEM gap blocked hand entry "during all phases of the press cycle." If so, the jury will ask how operators loaded and unloaded parts, and Vasquez says the guard was built to allow retrieval. We need a clear answer from Greenfield engineering on how parts are loaded and removed with the OEM guard installed, with exemplar demonstration, before trial. As the record stands, this is the pivot point of the causation fight.

- **Foreseeability of the modification.** Mitchum's Oct. 9, 2019 email lists "improved guarding options" as a Phase 2 item. Briggs testified that the OEM guard would not accept larger brackets. Plaintiffs will argue that customer guard substitution was foreseeable and that Greenfield knew its guard was a pain point. That undercuts any alteration or modification defense.

- **Post-sale warning.** Greenfield sent no bulletin to its roughly 1,850 customers, including Apex, despite Reedley's written recommendation. Briggs says he never received one. This supports a separate post-marketing warning theory. Note that the defense brief cites R.C. 2307.76(A)(2) as the modification defense. That subsection appears to be the post-marketing warning provision, which cuts against us. The citation should be verified and corrected.

- **Kwan misquotes Briggs.** Kwan's report says Briggs "read the Owner's Manual warning" and said Apex "needed to get the job done." The excerpted transcript says the opposite: Briggs "may have glanced at it" and "can't say for certain." Unless that testimony appears elsewhere in the 94-page transcript, Kwan will be impeached on it. Verify before mediation.

- **Brewer is distinguishable.** The defense brief rests heavily on Brewer. The comparables analysis shows Brewer had no evidence of prior notice, no internal documents, and no amputation. The Daubert motion is the one real swing factor: if the Vasquez "hazard even with OEM guard" opinion is excluded, causation tilts meaningfully toward us. Our honest estimate is that the motion is more likely to be denied, or granted only in part, than granted in full (about 25–30% chance of full exclusion).

Holt's own fault will be small. He did not modify the guard, used the two-hand control, and retrieved the part in the ordinary way. Our assessment of 15–25% for Greenfield is not credible to a jury that sees the emails. We also need to confirm that the non-party allocation to Apex under R.C. 2307.23 was properly pleaded as an affirmative defense and preserved.

**The 50% threshold.** The 50% line matters a great deal. Under R.C. 2307.22, a defendant found more than 50% at fault is jointly and severally liable for all economic damages, which means Greenfield would also absorb Apex's share, because Apex is immune as a complying workers' compensation employer. At 50% or below, Greenfield pays only its proportionate share. Non-economic damages are several-only in either case. The punitive cap is also tied to compensatory damages awarded against Greenfield, so a lower allocation shrinks punitive exposure too.

**Contribution from Apex is unlikely.** The defense brief "reserves" contribution against Apex under R.C. 2307.25. Workers' compensation immunity generally bars contribution claims against a complying employer unless there is a contractual indemnity. We have seen no indemnity provision in the Apex purchase documents. The Committee should not count on any recovery from Apex, and the brief should not imply that recovery is available.

## B. Non-Economic Damages — Uncapped for Mr. Holt

Because the R.C. 2315.18(B)(3) exception applies, a jury may award whatever the evidence supports, subject only to remittitur. Consider the profile: a 38-year-old father, dominant-arm amputation, phantom-limb pain, visible disfigurement, about 40 years of remaining life, and a sympathetic narrative ("coached Little League"). In the Southern District of Ohio, we assess a realistic jury range of \$2.5M–\$6.0M for Mr. Holt, most likely around \$4.0M. That range is consistent with Garza (\$6.4M gross settlement: same injury, same district, employer modification) and Novak (\$8.7M verdict; above-elbow amputation).

Jennifer Holt's consortium claim is her own claim and does not arise from her own physical injury. The better reading is that the general cap applies to it: the greater of \$250,000 or three times her economic loss, with a \$350,000 maximum per plaintiff. This point is not free from doubt. We value the claim at \$100,000–\$350,000, most likely \$250,000. The defense brief's \$50,000–\$100,000 is low but defensible as an opening position.

# V. Punitive Damages and Insurance

## A. Likelihood of Submission and Award

To obtain punitive damages against a manufacturer in a product-liability action, Plaintiffs must show by clear and convincing evidence that Greenfield acted with flagrant or conscious disregard for the safety of persons likely to be harmed (R.C. 2315.21; R.C. 2307.80). The factors in R.C. 2307.80 include the likelihood and seriousness of harm, the manufacturer's awareness of the risk, and its conduct after learning of the risk. On this record, summary judgment on punitive damages is unlikely. What the jury will see:

- Reedley specifically predicted the injury that occurred ("a crush amputation").

- By October 2019, Greenfield already knew of all three injury reports (the emails list the 2018 laceration, the 2018 bruised hand, and the 2019 fractured wrist). Mitchum deferred the fix anyway.

- Mitchum declined a risk assessment and deferred the \$385 fix mainly for budget-cycle and delivery reasons. The fix stayed deferred for almost four years, and no retrofit bulletin ever went to existing customers.

Mitigating points: the "Phase 2" delay can be attributed partly to supply-chain disruption; there were no prior amputations; the failure rate was below 0.4%; and Mitchum's emails are measured and acknowledge the reports. Plaintiffs have also overstated their case. Their brief's quotations from the emails (e.g., "We got lucky") do not appear in the produced text. Vasquez misquotes Reedley and Mitchum and says more operators were injured after October 2019, but the emails show all three injuries came before that. These points are good impeachment material. However, the corrected record is, if anything, worse for Greenfield on the knowledge question, so we should use them carefully.

Our estimates: if Greenfield is found more than 50% at fault, about a 35% chance of a punitive award; if 50% or less, about 15%. A likely award would be 0.5×–1.0× compensatory damages. The statutory cap is 2× the compensatory damages awarded against Greenfield (R.C. 2315.21(D)(2)(a)). Attorney fees can be added to a punitive award. Seeking bifurcation (R.C. 2315.21(B), or Fed. R. Civ. P. 42(b) if the court treats the Ohio rule as procedural) is essential if the case goes to trial.

## B. Punitive Damages Are Uninsured

Both the Ridgeline and Beacon policies exclude punitive damages. Ohio public policy, including R.C. 3937.182(B), generally bars insurance coverage for punitive damages. Every punitive dollar would therefore come from the Company. Coverage counsel's written opinion is still outstanding and should be delivered before March 14. Plaintiffs' brief frames the demand as being "within" the \$35M tower, which obscures the fact that the tower does not cover the exposure that matters most to the Company.

## C. Carrier Dynamics and Counsel Conflict

- **Put Ridgeline on notice.** Ridgeline funds any settlement and controls consent. Because the Company's uninsured punitive exposure is the main reason to settle, the Company's interest in settling is stronger than the carrier's. We recommend the Company send Ridgeline, with a copy to Beacon, a written request to settle within limits in the target range. The letter should cite the uninsured punitive exposure and the adverse-verdict scenarios in § VI. This protects the Company's rights if Ridgeline declines a reasonable within-limits settlement and a larger verdict follows.

- **Independent coverage counsel.** Hargrove, Foss & Delaney serves as both defense counsel and coverage counsel. Punitive-damages coverage is the kind of issue on which the Company's interests and the carrier's may diverge. We recommend the Committee retain independent coverage counsel to advise on the punitive exclusion and the settlement-demand letter. This recommendation is made in candor, not reluctance.

- **Prejudgment interest.** R.C. 1343.03(C) allows prejudgment interest on a tort judgment when a party failed to make a good-faith effort to settle. A token offer at mediation creates this risk, and it is one more reason the opening offer must be credible.

## D. Tactics and Guardrails

- **What not to argue.** Do not argue the non-economic cap, do not argue that the ANSI redundancy requirement is a mere recommendation, and do not deny that Greenfield knew of the re-cycling reports. The mediator will have read the emails.

- **Where to push.** Emphasize the undisputed Briggs facts (convenience swap, no measurement, defeated interlock sensor), the several-liability consequence of a Greenfield allocation of 50% or less, the Daubert motion, and our economic points on the discount rate, residual earnings, and prosthetics.

- **Bridging tools.** Offer to fund part of the settlement as a structured annuity for prosthetics and medical care. That lowers our cost and gives the Holts a guaranteed income stream. - **Confidentiality discipline.** Say nothing, directly or indirectly, about the Halcyon contract, the Board's timing preference, or reputational concerns. These facts would give Plaintiffs obvious leverage, as the GC memo warns. The Company representative should be briefed on this point specifically.

- **Walk-away posture.** If there is no deal above \$5.5M, do not let the session end. Accept a mediator's proposal process or schedule a follow-up session after the Daubert rulings. A favorable ruling lowers the value; an unfavorable one raises it by roughly \$0.75M–\$1.5M.
