# I. Bottom Line

We reviewed all 312 entries in Thornfield's privilege log. Each entry was checked against the org chart, the CLM engagement letter, the two common interest agreements (CIAs) and the Rule 26(a)(2) expert disclosures. For the 49 entries that came with sample documents, we also read the documents. The log should not be served or certified in its current form. There are four main problems:

- About 1 in 10 entries claim privilege that cannot be supported. 27 entries should be withdrawn and produced, and 5 more have been waived by disclosure to outsiders. These entries follow patterns that plaintiffs' counsel will spot quickly: Margaret Langford's communications from before she became General Counsel, Teresa Molina's communications (she is not a lawyer), ordinary-course Graystone reports, CLM marketing emails from before the engagement, and emails between business people with no lawyer involved.

- Three withheld documents were given to outsiders: NJDEP, which is an opposing party (#128); Ridgeline, the insurance broker (#102); and Graystone/Dr. Reese (#78). In each case the log hides the disclosure by describing the underlying privileged email rather than the forward that was actually logged. Two further documents were shared with Garfield before the Garfield CIA was signed (#85, \#91).

- Several log descriptions are affirmatively inaccurate. Examples: describing non-lawyer emails as "communication with counsel," routine audit reports as "prepared in anticipation of litigation," and a press release as "attorney work product." Serving these descriptions creates Rule 26(g) certification risk and could support an argument that privilege was waived for the entire log.

- Metadata is unreliable. In 26 entries the date, author, recipient, CC or subject is wrong (some dates are impossible, such as "March 32"), or the description is generic ("Re: Status," "Legal communication"). Most of these documents are privileged in substance, but the entries as written do not meet Rule 26(b)(5)(A).

# II. Governing Framework

- Attorney-client privilege (federal common law, FRE 501; Upjohn). The communication must be (1) between privileged persons (client, lawyer, and their agents), (2) made in confidence, and (3) made to obtain or provide legal assistance. For in-house counsel, and for emails that mix business and legal content, the Third Circuit looks at whether the primary purpose was legal advice. Having a lawyer's name on a communication, or copying a lawyer, is not enough.

- Work product (Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(3)). The material must have been prepared "in anticipation of litigation." Documents that would have been prepared in substantially the same form anyway, in the ordinary course of business, are not protected. On this record, litigation was reasonably anticipated no earlier than the CLM engagement (January 6, 2020), and the threatened claims are clearly established by April 22, 2020, when the Pacific Mutual CIA recited them.

- Common interest (In re Teleglobe, 493 F.3d 345 (3d Cir. 2007)). Protection applies only to communications between counsel that further a shared legal interest. Here it is also limited by the terms of the agreements. The Garfield CIA is effective August 3, 2021, is expressly non-retroactive (§§2.4, 5.4), and recites that there was no earlier agreement (§7.2). The Pacific Mutual CIA is effective April 22, 2020, excludes coverage disputes (§§2.5, 7.2), and excludes Ridgeline and Graystone (§§1.5, 7.3).

- Testifying experts (Rule 26(a)(2)(B)(ii), 26(b)(4)(B)–(C)). Facts and data "considered" by a testifying expert must be disclosed. Drafts of the expert's report, and most communications between counsel and the expert, remain protected.

- Waiver (FRE 502). An intentional disclosure waives privilege for the disclosed material. Under 502(a), it extends to undisclosed material on the same subject only if fairness requires. An inadvertent disclosure is not a waiver under 502(b) if reasonable precautions were taken and the error was promptly corrected.

- Log adequacy (Rule 26(b)(5)(A)). Each entry must describe the withheld material well enough that the other side can assess the claim. Conclusory descriptions and wrong metadata can support a finding of waiver.

# V. Clawback and Waiver-Containment Candidates

The enclosed workbook lists 10 candidate items, ranked by priority. For each one, it analyzes whether FRE 502(b) clawback is available (inadvertence, reasonable precautions, prompt correction) and the risk of subject-matter waiver under FRE 502(a). The main conclusions are:

- Clawback is not realistically available for \#128 (NJDEP) or \#102 (Ridgeline). The GC disclosed both documents deliberately. The goal should be to limit the waiver to those documents: do not use them affirmatively, and prepare to oppose subject-matter waiver.

- \#78 (the forward to Graystone) is the strongest candidate for a 502(b) argument. The employee who forwarded it could not waive privilege for the company, and counsel had told him in writing not to share it. But the forward went undetected for years, and Reese's later designation as a testifying expert may make the email discoverable regardless, if he considered it. Send a sequestration demand to Graystone now.

- \#85 and \#91 (shared with Garfield before the CIA) can be defended only with an informal common-interest argument under Teleglobe. The Garfield CIA's own recitals seriously undercut that argument.

- \#177, \#44, \#119, \#156 and the Reese expert file (#199, with drafts in \#155 and \#246) need a redaction and segregation protocol for production.
