# Executive assessment

The log is materially overinclusive. The strongest systemic defects are (1) claims for communications involving Margaret Langford before March 15, 2019, when she was a business/regulatory executive and not Thornfield’s lawyer; (2) claims for ordinary-course environmental audits, sampling and operating/business records prepared by Graystone under its routine services agreement; (3) claims for lobbying and regulatory-affairs communications by Teresa Molina, a nonlawyer; and (4) communications shared outside Thornfield’s direct counsel circle, including a likely waiver to Graystone, high-risk pre-JDA exchanges with Garfield’s counsel, an insurance broker, and NJDEP. Labels such as “privileged,” “confidential,” or “work product” do not cure these defects.

At the same time, the descriptions for most post-engagement counsel communications, internal legal advice, legal research, and litigation strategy are facially supportable. Several need document-level narrowing because ordinary business facts, source data, attachments, raw financial records, filed pleadings, and testifying-expert materials are not protected merely because they appear in a legal communication or litigation file.

No production history or evidence that a disclosure was inadvertent was supplied. Accordingly, this review identifies conditional Rule 502 clawback candidates if a defensibly privileged item was inadvertently produced to an adversary; it does not identify a confirmed clawback event. Known voluntary third-party disclosures should not be presented as inadvertent clawback claims on the current record.

# Key legal and factual issues requiring immediate attention

**Pre-GC Langford period (Entries 1, 3–6, 8–10; sample-docs 003, 005, 009).** The organization chart expressly states that Langford served as VP of Regulatory Affairs through March 14, 2019, in a business/operational role, provided no legal advice, and was not in the legal department. Her law license or legal-sounding subject lines do not create ACP. These sampled messages are operational compliance, schedules, audits, and budget action items.

**Outside counsel pre-engagement (Entries 2, 7, 11; sample-docs 007, 011).** CLM’s January 6, 2020 engagement letter expressly states no attorney-client relationship or legal advice before that date. The Nov.–Dec. 2019 materials are capabilities/engagement terms. Entry 2 (2017) is even less supported; any WP claim requires proof of reasonably anticipated litigation and preparation because of litigation, not a generic “potential exposure” label.

**Graystone ordinary-course records (Entries 33, 58, 96; sample-docs 033, 058, 096, 128).** The reports/checklists were created under the September 1, 2018 MSA for routine annual/quarterly compliance and sampling. They predate the litigation hold and were not prepared at counsel’s direction. The reports say they are ordinary compliance materials; underlying environmental results are independently discoverable. Confidentiality is not privilege.

**Molina / government relations (Entries 31, 55, 89; sample-docs 031, 055, 089, 134, 141, 203).** Molina is expressly a nonlawyer VP of Government Relations, is not legal department, and has never given legal advice. Her communications concern lobbying, agency meetings, public-policy positioning, legislative strategy, and factual meeting reports. They are not ACP.

**Graystone disclosure (Entry 78; sample-doc-078).** Pruitt forwarded the Marsh/Kapadia legal exchange to Dr. Reese on June 14, 2021 and instructed him to incorporate the legal team’s views into technical modeling. Graystone was retained under a routine consulting MSA, no common-interest agreement covered Graystone, and the forwarded counsel email itself warns against third-party disclosure. The underlying exchange may have been privileged before forwarding, but the transmitted copy presents a substantial waiver problem. Do not call it an inadvertent disclosure without evidence.

**Garfield pre-JDA disclosures (Entries 85 and 91; sample-docs 085 and 091).** The Thornfield–Garfield agreement is effective August 3, 2021 and expressly nonretroactive. The March 15 and May 2, 2021 samples were shared with Garfield counsel before execution; the May 2 email says the formal arrangement is still to come. The later agreement does not retroactively cover them. A common-interest doctrine may nevertheless apply without a signed agreement if a then-existing common legal defense purpose and confidentiality are established. The liability allocation discussion creates an adversity concern; these claims are high-risk/conditional, not automatically waived.

**Broker and NJDEP disclosure (sample-docs 102 and 119).** Sample 102 forwards outside counsel’s litigation assessment to Ridgeline Risk Partners, an insurance broker with no common-interest agreement. Sample 119 intentionally sends Langford’s legal-risk memorandum to NJDEP, a regulator/co-plaintiff, for settlement dialogue, notwithstanding a warning in the memo not to disclose it. These are likely waivers, not Rule 502(b) inadvertent disclosures; do not pursue clawback absent facts showing mistake.

**Testifying expert / report (expert disclosures and sample-doc-199).** Reese was designated as a testifying expert under Rule 26(a)(2)(B). The March 22, 2022 technical report says it was prepared at CLM’s direction but also states Reese was designated testifying expert in 2024. Testifying-expert opinions, report and facts/data considered are discoverable under Rule 26(a)(2)(B) and (b)(4)(A); attorney-expert communications receive only Rule 26(b)(4)(C)’s limited protection. The 2018–2021 routine Graystone materials are not converted into WP by later expert designation. Reconcile the report’s date/chronology and all disclosure history.

**Mixed business emails (Entry 44; sample-docs 044, 156, 158).** The sampled Sandra Choi emails are long operational updates on production, vendors, staffing, budgets, maintenance and contracts, ending with a legal question. A short request for legal advice does not cloak the entire business email. Produce the business material and consider only a narrow redaction of the actual legal request if justified; there is no attorney response in the sample.

**Clawback scope (Rule 502).** Rule 502(b) relief applies only to inadvertent disclosure where reasonable steps were taken to prevent disclosure and prompt reasonable steps were taken to rectify it. The supplied evidence shows intentional sharing in several examples, not inadvertence. The spreadsheet therefore separates potential candidates from exclusions and requires production-history verification before any clawback notice.

# Standards applied / limitations

Attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications made for the purpose of obtaining or providing legal advice, not the underlying facts. Corporate communications are assessed by function and purpose (Upjohn Co. v. United States, 449 U.S. 383 (1981)). Work product protects materials prepared because of anticipated litigation, not records created in the ordinary course (Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(3); Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U.S. 495 (1947)). Common interest is a nonwaiver principle for otherwise privileged communications shared to further a common legal interest; it does not create privilege and does not ordinarily cover business interests (In re Teleglobe Communications Corp., 493 F.3d 345 (3d Cir. 2007)). Agreements are evidence of intent/scope but do not create privilege, cure disclosure to outsiders, or operate retroactively where they say otherwise. Testifying expert discovery is governed by Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(a)(2) and 26(b)(4).
