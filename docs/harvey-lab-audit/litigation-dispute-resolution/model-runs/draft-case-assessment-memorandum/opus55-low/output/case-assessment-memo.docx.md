# 1. Executive Summary

We represent Defendant Greenleaf Organics, Inc. ("Greenleaf"). Cascade Distribution Holdings, LLC ("Cascade") sues for breach of contract, tortious interference, trade secret misappropriation (OUTSA and DTSA), and unjust enrichment, and claims \$22,521,312.50 in compensatory damages plus up to \$9.6 million in exemplary damages, punitive damages, fees, and an injunction. This memo reviews the complaint, the March 15, 2019 Exclusive Distribution Agreement (the "Agreement"), the September 8, 2023 termination letter, Cascade's September 15, 2023 rejection letter, Cascade's IT forensic memo, Tyler Jantzen's 2017 Cascade employment agreement, the Bridger Valuation Group preliminary damages report, and the internal Greenleaf email set that Greenleaf's General Counsel forwarded to us.

Bottom line:

- **Liability on contract:** Greenleaf's contract liability is close to certain. The September 8 letter does not meet the Agreement's own requirements for a valid termination. Section 9.2 says a notice that fails those requirements is "void and of no effect." The letter also cannot work as a non-renewal notice under Section 3.2, because that section requires a notice to say expressly that it is one. So the Agreement renewed automatically through March 14, 2025. Greenleaf then stopped shipping on October 2, 2023, sold directly to retailers in Cascade's exclusive Territory (Section 2.2), and hired a Covered Employee in breach of Section 11.3. We see no credible defense to breach on these facts. The fight is over damages.

- **Trade secret exposure:** This is our most serious risk. Cascade's forensic evidence shows Jantzen emailed the pricing model, the 1,847-account master list, and route data to his personal Gmail on October 15, 2023. Greenleaf's own documents are damaging. On November 15, 2023, the VP of Sales wrote that Jantzen's pricing "looks suspiciously similar" to Cascade's and that retailers called the tiers "identical." The CEO replied, "Don't put stuff like that in email." Jantzen's October 20, 2023 email ("I didn't bring any Cascade files with me") is contradicted by the forensic record. It was also sent from a Greenleaf account 11 days before his last day at Cascade. None of these three emails is privileged.

- **Damages:** Bridger's \$22.5M figure is inflated and can be attacked on several fronts. (a) It claims both the termination fee and full-term lost profits, which are legally inconsistent remedies. (b) It projects volume from a Year 4 peak that Cascade's actual Year 5 run-rate does not support. (c) It stacks reliance-type infrastructure losses on top of expectation damages. (d) It measures trade secret 'loss' by development cost and the acquisition cost of every account, which overlaps with lost profits. Our preliminary view is that a realistic range of compensatory recovery is roughly \$3M–\$14M. The upside risk (willful exemplary damages, punitive damages, fee-shifting) is about \$20M–\$30M in a bad trial outcome.

- **Recommended course:** (1) Immediately widen the litigation hold and preserve Jantzen's personal Gmail and devices. (2) Commission an independent forensic sweep of Greenleaf systems and quarantine any Cascade material. (3) Retain separate counsel for Jantzen. (4) Treat Mr. Hyun-Park as a fact witness who should not direct the defense. (5) File a targeted Rule 12 motion against the tort, unjust enrichment, and punitive damages claims. (6) Retain a rebuttal damages expert. (7) Seriously consider an early, well-supported resolution before depositions of Ellison, Brennan, and Jantzen.

# 4. Claim-by-Claim Assessment

## 4.1 First Cause of Action — Breach of Contract

Assessment: HIGH liability risk (we estimate an 85–90% likelihood that Cascade establishes breach). The real contest is over damages and the application of Section 16.

**(a) The termination was void.**

Section 9.2 requires a convenience notice to (i) expressly reference §9.2, (ii) specify an effective date no earlier than 12 months after the notice, and (iii) acknowledge the obligation to pay the Termination Fee. It then states that failing to comply "shall render any purported termination for convenience void and of no effect, and this Agreement shall continue in full force and effect." The September 8 letter satisfies (i) only. It was effective "immediately." It did not mention the fee, and it said Greenleaf considered "all other financial obligations … fully addressed." Section 17.12 (no construction against the drafter) and the arm's-length recital in §16.3 leave us no ambiguity argument.

**(b) Automatic renewal through March 14, 2025.**

Section 3.2 requires a non-renewal notice to "expressly state that it is a notice of non-renewal under this Section 3.2" and to be delivered under §17.6. The September 8 letter does neither. It is very unlikely a court will treat the letter as a non-renewal notice by implication. Note that the complaint's own discussion of this point (¶41) is internally muddled: it says the letter was 'eight days before' the deadline, which cuts against Cascade. The express-statement requirement in §3.2 still resolves the point in Cascade's favor. Timing: Cascade's rejection letter arrived on September 15, one day before the deadline, and pointedly noted that no non-renewal notice had been received. There was a one-day window. Greenleaf did not use it.

**(c) Additional breaches (clear on the record).**

- **Supply —** Cessation of all shipments on October 2, 2023, and rejection of orders through January 2024 (§7.1). This is a repudiation.

- **Exclusivity —** Direct distribution to retailers in the Territory (§2.2). The complaint does not list this as a separate breach in ¶87, but it pleads the facts (¶¶44, 99). Expect an amendment.

- **Non-solicitation —** Jantzen was a "Covered Employee" (§11.2: sales/account managers materially involved within the prior 12 months). Section 11.3 bars soliciting or hiring such an employee "regardless of whether the Covered Employee initiates the contact." Neither §11.4 exception applies: he resigned, and there was no general job posting. The complaint misquotes §11.3 (it uses an older-style 'involved in the performance' formulation), but the actual text is broader and worse for us. Section 11.5 allows injunctive relief without bond.

- **Confidentiality —** The complaint's theory under §12 is weaker than it looks (see §5.3 below). Also, the complaint cites '§12.4' for return/destruction; that obligation is actually in §12.2, and §12.4 covers equitable relief.

## 4.2 Second Cause of Action — Tortious Interference with Business Relationships

Assessment: LOW-TO-MODERATE risk. Good candidate for a motion to dismiss.

- **OUTSA displacement:** ORS 646.473 displaces conflicting tort and restitutionary remedies based on misappropriation of a trade secret. The 'improper means' pleaded (¶¶100(a), 103) is mainly the alleged misappropriation. To the extent the claim rests on that, it is displaced.

- **Remaining improper-means theory:** What remains is breach of contract (§11.3) and competition for retail accounts. Under Oregon's intentional-interference standard, a breach of contract is generally not an 'improper means' by itself, and Greenleaf had an economic interest in selling its own products to retailers. Cascade must also plead damage beyond the contract loss and a relationship with a third party. The retailers buy Greenleaf products, so Greenleaf is hardly a stranger to those relationships.

- **Punitive damages:** Oregon requires leave of court before punitive damages may be pleaded (ORS 31.725), and D. Or. judges have split on whether that rule applies in federal court. It must also be proved by clear and convincing evidence (ORS 31.730). Move to strike ¶106 and prayer (b), subject to research on current D. Or. authority.

- **Unpleaded interference-with-contract claim:** This is a latent risk. Jantzen's employment agreement contains a customer non-solicit (§8.2) and a non-compete that covers 'suppliers' of Cascade (§7.1). Cascade has not yet pleaded that Greenleaf interfered with the Jantzen contract, but the Oct. 20 email and Ellison's recruitment make that claim easy to add. (Enforceability questions: Washington RCW 49.62 salary thresholds and notice requirements; Oregon ORS 653.295 if Oregon law governs an Oregon-based employee. Research required.)

## 4.3 Third Cause of Action — Trade Secret Misappropriation (OUTSA / DTSA)

Assessment: MODERATE-TO-HIGH risk (we estimate a 60–75% likelihood of a misappropriation finding against Greenleaf). This is the claim most likely to drive fees, exemplary damages, and injunctive relief, and the one that most damages Greenleaf's credibility with a jury.

**(a) Trade secret status.**

The pricing model and master account list (labeled 'Confidential — Restricted,' with 3+ years of purchase history, contract terms, and profitability data) are likely protectable. Jantzen's claim that he used 'publicly available retailer directories' may cover names and addresses, but it does not cover purchase histories, contract terms, and margin structures. Cascade's secrecy measures (classification policy, confidentiality agreements, access logging, a contractual ban on personal-email transfer) look reasonable. The route optimization PDF ('Confidential — Internal') is the weakest item: it is a Q3 snapshot, and it has little obvious value to a manufacturer building its own logistics.

**(b) Acquisition by improper means (Jantzen).**

The forensic record is strong. It shows a sharp spike in access, the after-hours email, the 'Files' subject line with no body text, a check of Gmail five minutes later, and a direct violation of his employment agreement's §6.5. Possible attacks: the complaint says the laptop was reviewed 'prior to its return on October 30,' but the forensic memo says it was returned October 31 and reviewed starting November 2. The October 14 save of the account file changed its metadata, and 'content changes … could not be determined.' These are cross-examination points, not defenses.

**(c) Greenleaf's knowledge and use (the key question).**

There is no direct evidence yet that the files reached Greenleaf systems. The circumstantial case is strong, however:

- Ellison's 'whole playbook' / 'plug and play Cascade's network' email (Aug. 10), sent before the transfer. Cascade will argue this shows Greenleaf expected or encouraged misappropriation.

- Jantzen was doing Greenleaf work, on a Greenleaf email account, by October 20, before his Cascade departure. That supports aiding a breach of the duty of loyalty and imputes his knowledge to Greenleaf.

- Brennan's November 15 email: pricing tiers 'identical'; a sophisticated model ready 'within weeks' when building one normally takes months. This is not privileged, and it comes from a 20-year distribution veteran who is Greenleaf's own VP of Sales.

- Ellison's November 18 reply ('Don't put stuff like that in email') invites a consciousness-of-guilt argument. It also suggests Greenleaf took no remedial action after an internal red flag, which supports 'willful and malicious' conduct and defeats any argument that Greenleaf acquired the information innocently.

- Jantzen's false exculpatory statement ('I didn't bring any Cascade files') will damage his credibility and, by extension, Greenleaf's.

**(d) Remedies exposure.**

Actual loss and/or unjust enrichment, or a reasonable royalty (18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)(3)(B); ORS 646.465). Exemplary damages up to 2x for willful and malicious misappropriation (18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)(3)(C); OUTSA's exemplary-damages provision). Attorney fees (18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)(3)(D); OUTSA fee provision, which we believe is ORS 646.467; the complaint cites ORS 646.465(2)–(3) and should be checked). Injunction (ORS 646.463; § 1836(b)(3)(A)). Note an inconsistency between Cascade's documents: the complaint says exemplary damages could add \$9.6M (2x \$4.8M), while Bridger caveat (c) says 'up to an additional \$4.8M.' The statutes cap exemplary damages at twice the award, so the complaint's figure is the correct maximum.

**(e) Injunction posture.**

Cascade has not sought a TRO or preliminary injunction, even though it had the forensic findings in November 2023. That five-month delay undercuts its claim of irreparable harm. Even so, a proactive remediation program (§9) is the best way to defeat injunctive relief and reduce any finding of 'willful' conduct going forward.

## 4.4 Fourth Cause of Action — Unjust Enrichment

Assessment: LOW risk. Where a valid express contract governs the subject matter, Oregon law generally bars a quasi-contract claim. Cascade pleads this claim in the alternative (¶127), but the Agreement's validity is undisputed. To the extent the claim rests on trade secrets, OUTSA displaces it (ORS 646.473). The \$22.5M restitution demand simply repeats the contract and trade secret damages; it is not a separate measure of Greenleaf's benefit. Move to dismiss, or at least expect it to be limited to duplicate relief.

## 4.5 Section 16 Limitation of Liability — Pivotal Issue

Section 16.2 excludes consequential damages, 'including but not limited to lost profits, loss of business, loss of goodwill.' It carves out 'willful misconduct or misappropriation of Confidential Information.' Every dollar of Cascade's lost-profit theory depends on one of those exceptions, or on characterizing lost profits under a distribution contract as direct damages.

- **Misappropriation exception — a good argument for us:** 'Confidential Information' is defined as information 'disclosed by one Party to the other Party in connection with this Agreement.' Cascade's internal pricing model, customer database, and route analyses were never disclosed to Greenleaf under the Agreement. They were allegedly taken by an employee. There is a strong textual argument that neither Section 12 nor the misappropriation carve-out is triggered. (Cascade will reply that Greenleaf is bound by §12 regardless, and that the definition's 'without limiting' list covers these categories.)

- **Willful misconduct exception — difficult:** This is the harder fight. Ellison's 'I don't want to pay the termination fee — we'll fight about it if we have to' and 'the sooner we cut Cascade out, the sooner we start capturing their margin' are strong evidence of a deliberate, knowing breach. Our best arguments: (i) 'willful misconduct' in a limitation clause should mean something more than an intentional breach of contract; otherwise every deliberate termination dispute would fall outside the clause and make §16.3's 'essential element of the bargain' meaningless. (ii) Greenleaf acted on a good-faith, if mistaken, view of its exit rights. That argument is weakened because Hyun-Park's advice spelled out the 12-month and fee requirements, and it would put privilege at issue (§7). Our estimate: 55–65% likelihood a court finds the willful-misconduct exception applies to the termination-related damages.

- **Direct vs. consequential characterization:** Many courts treat lost profits on the contract itself as direct, 'general' damages. Here, however, §16.2 names lost profits expressly, which helps us. Research is needed on Oregon authority.

- **§16.1 cap:** The cap equals purchases in the two preceding contract years (Years 3–4 = \$71.6M). It is not a practical constraint.

- **§15.4 fee-shifting:** Section 15.4 gives the prevailing party its fees, expert fees, and costs. Cascade will likely be the prevailing party on the contract claim, so fee exposure on the contract claim exists independent of the trade secret statutes. ORS 20.096 makes the clause reciprocal in any event.

## 4.6 Conditions Precedent — Section 15

Section 15.1 requires good-faith negotiations between senior executives: a meeting within 15 days of written notice and at least 30 days of negotiation. Section 15.3 allows litigation only if the dispute is 'not resolved through mediation within sixty (60) days of the commencement of the mediation process.' The complaint does not allege compliance with §15.1. If the 'commencement' of mediation was the March 15, 2024 session, suit was premature until about May 14, 2024. If it was the February 1 demand, the April 12 filing was timely (71 days). This point supports a stay or leverage in negotiating the schedule, but it is curable and courts rarely dismiss on it. Note also that §15.5 exempts injunctive claims under Sections 11 and 12 from these prerequisites.

## 9.5 Resolution strategy

The combination of near-certain contract liability, the Brennan and Ellison emails, and two-way fee exposure makes this case a poor candidate for trial. Our preliminary recommendation is to (i) complete the forensic sweep and remediation first, so that Greenleaf can credibly represent that no Cascade material remains in use; (ii) win what we can on the Rule 12 motion; and (iii) re-engage mediator Polzin (or a magistrate judge settlement conference) before Ellison, Brennan, and Jantzen are deposed.
