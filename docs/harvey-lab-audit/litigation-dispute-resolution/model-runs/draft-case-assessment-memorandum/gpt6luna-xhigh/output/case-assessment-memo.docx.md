# 3. Liability analysis

## A. Breach of contract — strongest claim

The Agreement requires strict compliance with the termination mechanics. Section 9.2 requires 12 months’ advance written notice; the notice must state an anticipated termination date no earlier than 12 months after notice and acknowledge the fee obligation; noncompliance makes the purported termination void. Greenleaf’s Sept. 8 letter expressly cites §9.2 but says “effective immediately,” supplies no compliant future date, and neither acknowledges nor tenders the fee. It is a particularly strong breach record because the letter and agreement themselves establish the mismatch. Cascade’s Sept. 15 response promptly preserved its position.

Greenleaf’s later conduct—stopping shipments, rejecting orders, and direct sales—supports repudiation and breach of the exclusivity promise in §2.2. The direct-to-retail channel is not within the limited website e-commerce or incidental event exceptions (§2.4), if the alleged scope is proved. Section 7.1 requires commercially reasonable supply efforts “subject to product availability,” rather than an absolute fill-every-order covenant; obtain availability, production, inventory, rejected-order, and payment records to establish that Greenleaf could and should have filled Cascade orders.

Automatic renewal is a strong but litigable issue. Section 3.2 requires a notice that expressly states it is a notice of non-renewal under §3.2, delivered under §17.6, at least 180 days before term end. Greenleaf’s Sept. 8 letter described an immediate §9.2 convenience termination, not non-renewal. It was timely in date (eight days before Sept. 16), however, so Greenleaf may argue it communicated an intent to end the relationship. The express-content requirement and different legal effects favor Cascade. If no compliant notice, the term renewed through Mar. 14, 2025; no later non-renewal notice appears in this record.

Year 2 shortfall is Greenleaf’s main contract defense. Section 5.3 defines failure to meet the minimum as a material breach subject to §9.1. Section 14 expressly lists pandemics and supply-chain disruptions, but Cascade must prove the event caused the shortfall and that it gave prompt written notice and used commercially reasonable mitigation (§14.2). Greenleaf’s alleged 2021 breach notice matters, but a cause termination required a detailed notice, 30-day cure period, and then a 60-day termination notice (§9.1); the Sept. 8, 2023 letter instead elected §9.2 and did not satisfy §9.1. The two-plus years of continued performance and counsel’s contemporaneous waiver analysis are favorable to Cascade. The contract’s written anti-waiver clause (§17.4) helps Greenleaf argue no waiver absent writing, but does not eliminate arguments based on election, estoppel, force majeure, and failure to follow the cause procedure.

## B. Employee solicitation / non-hire — strong contractual claim

Section 11.2 defines a Covered Employee by material involvement during the preceding 12 months; Jantzen directly managed Greenleaf-related accounts, pricing, and routes. Section 11.3 prohibits solicitation, recruitment, hiring, or engagement during the term and 18 months thereafter, even if the employee initiates contact. The agreement’s exceptions (termination without cause or a truly general, non-targeted posting) do not fit the current facts: Ellison initiated contact, pursued him, and discussed a role. Jantzen was still employed by Cascade when recruited and hired during the initial term. No written consent is shown. Section 11.5 expressly provides for injunctive relief. Obtain evidence of the precise communications, role start date, and any compensation/consulting before Nov. 1.

The separate Jantzen employment agreement supports his access, confidentiality duties, return/deletion duties, and a possible separate claim, but does not make him a party to this case. Its broad 12-month non-compete and 18-month customer/employee nonsolicits are governed by Washington law and King County venue under that agreement; enforceability against an Oregon-based worker and statutory limits require separate, current-law analysis. Do not assume the non-compete is enforceable or use it as the primary basis for a Greenleaf injunction.

## C. Trade secret misappropriation (OUTSA / DTSA) — credible, but corporate-use link is incomplete

The transferred files map to information expressly identified as confidential in the distribution and employment agreements: a pricing model, 1,847-account database with purchasing/contract details, and route analytics. The forensic memo gives file names, size, timestamps, delivery log, and hash-preserved imaging; the employment agreement prohibits transfer to personal accounts. This is strong proof of unauthorized acquisition by Jantzen. Reported access restrictions, labels, password/MFA controls, and employee agreements support reasonable secrecy measures, subject to production of the actual policies and system evidence. Compilations of account-level history, terms, profitability, and route data are more defensible secrets than public contact details or general buyer relationships.

The harder issue is Greenleaf’s acquisition, knowledge, or use. The Aug. 10 “whole playbook” message precedes the transfer and is powerful circumstantial evidence of expectation and intent, but may refer to experience and relationships rather than files. The Oct. 20 message affirmatively denies bringing files while describing a rapid rollout based on memory and public sources; the Oct. 15 email to personal Gmail contradicts any literal claim that no files were taken, but does not establish that Greenleaf received them. The Nov. 15 pricing concern and Nov. 18 response are probative of notice and possible consciousness of risk; they do not, without more, prove when or how the data were used. Greenleaf will argue common market price points, independently developed pricing, public directories, personal knowledge, and lawful customer choice. Greenleaf’s monthly access to sales/account-activity reports under §4.3 is another issue: distinguish those permitted summaries from the detailed database, account terms, and algorithms allegedly exfiltrated.

For both statutory claims, establish (i) the precise secret and owner; (ii) secrecy measures; (iii) improper acquisition by Jantzen; (iv) Greenleaf’s knowledge or reason to know; (v) Greenleaf’s receipt/use or threatened use; and (vi) injury. The files are not attached and their contents, versions, metadata, and similarity analyses have not been independently examined. The complaint names only Greenleaf; consider whether to add or separately pursue Jantzen after venue, choice-of-law, limitations, and strategy review. DTSA/OUTSA do not authorize a restraint on employment based merely on knowledge; any order should prohibit use/disclosure and require targeted return/destruction/inspection, not bar Greenleaf from competing or hiring Jantzen absent proof and legal basis.

## D. Tortious interference and unjust enrichment — supportive, but derivative/overlap concerns

Tortious interference requires proof of specific existing or prospective retail relationships, Greenleaf’s knowledge, intentional interference, improper means or purpose, causation, and nonduplicative loss. The agreement’s exclusivity, alleged secret use, and targeted recruitment can supply improper means beyond ordinary competition or a bare contract breach. Evidence that accounts actually shifted to Greenleaf and the resulting margin loss is not yet supplied. Cascade should identify accounts, dates, prior volume, communications, and lost distribution economics. Greenleaf will argue it sold its own products to willing retailers and that Cascade’s alleged injury is simply the contract expectancy. Expect dismissal, narrowing, or damages overlap arguments if the tort count is based only on the same contract breach and trade-secret use.

Unjust enrichment is expressly pleaded in the alternative, but the written agreement governs much of the relationship, which ordinarily makes quasi-contract restitution difficult for matters within its scope. Preserve it for benefits outside the contract or if the agreement is held not to govern the specific benefit (particularly secret use), but do not seek duplicative restitution for the same contract margin, infrastructure, customer acquisition, or trade-secret injury.

It states that the work is preliminary, assumes liability and the full forecast period, relies on Cascade/counsel data, and defers legal recoverability to counsel. # 5. Defenses, procedural issues, and evidentiary cautions

- Force majeure / Year 2 shortfall: obtain the April 5, 2021 notice, Cascade’s response, pandemic/supply records, notice and mitigation documentation, and post-shortfall purchase records. A purchase shortfall is expressly material under §5.3, but force majeure and the unfulfilled §9.1 termination steps substantially weaken an after-the-fact cause defense.

- Non-renewal: the Sept. 8 letter was before the deadline, so its wording and recipient/delivery matter. The agreement requires express §3.2 non-renewal content and §17.6 delivery. Obtain full certified-mail receipts, email transmission/receipt, address designation history, and any later notice.

- Mediation / negotiations condition: §§15.1–15.3 require senior-executive negotiations for at least 30 days, mediation, and no litigation until the dispute remains unresolved for 60 days from commencement of the mediation process. Complaint ¶14 states a Feb. 1 mediation demand, Mar. 15 session, and Apr. 12 filing; 60 days after Feb. 1 elapsed before filing, but the record does not show when negotiations began or when mediation formally commenced. Confirm notices, negotiation meetings, mediator engagement/commencement, and any waiver. If commencement is treated as Mar. 15, suit was filed before 60 days; Greenleaf could seek a stay/dismissal without prejudice. This may be curable and is not a merits defense.

- Limitation of liability / foreseeability / mitigation: §16.2 expressly names lost profits and loss of business. Establish willful misconduct or misuse of Confidential Information to invoke the exception. Demonstrate foreseeability and the net, avoidable-cost-adjusted nature of profit damages; document substitute product sales, asset redeployment, and customer retention efforts.

- Privilege and internal emails: The Apr. 18 wrapper is a GC-to-outside-counsel request for legal advice; protect it. The Aug. 10 CEO-to-GC email expressly requests contract advice, and Sept. 5 GC legal analysis and Sept. 7 CEO response may be privileged in whole or part. Business communications (Oct. 20 Jantzen rollout; Nov. 15 VP sales concern; Nov. 18 CEO response) are not privileged merely because later forwarded to counsel or labeled “work product.” Pre-existing emails are not cloaked by forwarding; privilege is communication-specific and mixed legal/business content may be segregable. Work product requires preparation because of anticipated litigation, not simply a litigation-hold or counsel forwarding. If the compilation was inadvertently produced/received, stop broader review, segregate it, notify the producing counsel, follow the protective order and Fed. R. Evid. 502 / Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(5)(B), and resolve provenance/clawback before quoting it in filings. Crime-fraud is a narrow exception and should not be presumed from alleged contract evasion alone.

- Forensic report chronology and custody: report says laptop returned Oct. 31, review initiated Nov. 2, and imaging verified before analysis. Complaint ¶64 says review occurred before return on Oct. 30. Correct the pleading/record; preserve original image, hash records, chain-of-custody log, complete Exchange logs, and the forensic examiner’s methods. The inconsistency is avoidable impeachment material.

- Other complaint/report mis-citations: the agreement places governing law in §17.1 (not §15.3); notices in §17.6 (not §16.1); return/destruction in §12.2 (not §12.4; §12.4 is equitable relief). Complaint ¶71 also assumes a §12.2 return clock ran despite the dispute over whether termination was effective. Correct these before briefing and distinguish a demand for return from a contractual post-termination trigger.

- Damages/expert disclosure: report is preliminary and prepared at counsel’s direction; its assertions are not independent proof. Confirm source records and expert qualifications/methodology, Rule 26(a)(2) disclosures, workpapers, and whether the report will be supplemented. Use a single consistent forecast and a clear alternative/overlap schedule.

# 7. Overall posture and recommended framing

Lead with the straightforward written-contract story: Greenleaf selected an immediate exit after its own counsel explained the actual contractual path and fee; it then stopped supplying Cascade and began selling directly while the agreement remained operative. Treat the 2021 minimum shortfall as a serious but fact-dependent defense, not something dismissed as categorically waived. The hiring clause and contemporaneous recruiting record are strong independent contractual leverage.

Frame trade-secret liability as a focused circumstantial case that requires forensic corroboration, not as established fact. The best theory is that Greenleaf recruited a key employee for the account/pricing network, Jantzen secretly exfiltrated the exact restricted files shortly before departure, Greenleaf built a direct operation unusually quickly, and its pricing group identified apparent replication. The case will turn on whether that sequence is supported by Greenleaf-side artifacts and customer evidence, and whether Cascade can differentiate protectable confidential compilations from skills, experience, public information, and data Greenleaf was contractually permitted to receive.

For damages, present the \$22.52m report total as a preliminary claim, not a floor. Separate (i) contract lost profits, (ii) any fee claim in the alternative, (iii) provable stranded investment, and (iv) trade-secret loss/royalty or defendant enrichment; explicitly map overlaps and avoid duplicate recovery under contract, tort, trade-secret, and restitution theories. Preserve statutory enhancements, fees, and tailored equitable relief as contingent remedies. Reassess valuation after source data and forensic discovery; the current record does not support a reliable settlement range.
