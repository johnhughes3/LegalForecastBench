# III. Subpoena: authenticity, scope, and procedural response

## A. Authenticate before substantive contact; do not disregard

Rule 17(a) permits issuance by the clerk or an attorney for the government. The text provided has a blank clerk/deputy-clerk execution line and a blank AUSA signature line, yet says “BY ORDER OF THE COURT”; it displays a placeholder for a court seal. The issuing line identifies “Jonathan Cromdale Consulting,” while the displayed email is jonathan.mercer@usdoj.gov. The proof of service leaves the server’s name and title blank. These are meaningful authenticity/execution questions, not a basis to ignore a document that was actually delivered to the registered agent.

Inspect the exact served original (including any wet or electronic signature, seal, envelope, attachments, and version) and the agent’s receipt log. Verify the prosecutor’s identity, case number, subpoena authority, and return instructions using public SDNY/USAO contact information obtained independently. Do not send sensitive facts, the intake memo, or substantive objections to the email/number printed on the suspect copy until verified. If the instrument is genuine but unsigned, request a corrected instrument and reserve objections; if not genuine, escalate through verified official channels. Continue the preservation hold in either event.

Service was reportedly personal delivery to Grayfield’s registered agent on June 5. Delivery to a duly authorized registered agent may be effective for the LLC; confirm the agent’s authority, the person who accepted it, and exactly what was delivered. A blank proof-of-service identity may weaken proof but does not necessarily undo actual notice. The subpoena purports to cover every “Related Entity, Fund, and Affiliate”; service on Grayfield Capital does not automatically establish possession, custody, or control over every separately organized affiliate, investor, or employee’s personal records.

## B. Rule 17(c) objections and proposed narrowing

A grand jury’s investigative authority is broad. A subpoena is generally enforced unless it lacks a legitimate investigative purpose, seeks material with no reasonable possibility of relevance, or is unreasonably broad/oppressive; Rule 17(c)(2) authorizes quashing or modifying an unreasonable or oppressive subpoena. See Fed. R. Crim. P. 17(c)(2); United States v. R. Enterprises, Inc., 498 U.S. 292, 297–301 (1991). The strict Nixon trial-subpoena test should not be assumed to govern a grand-jury investigation in the same way. Still, the range here supports a burden, relevance, privacy, and control meet-and-confer.

The directly relevant core is VRDN trading, decision-making, Ashford communications, the MNPI pathway, personal trading/pre-clearance, and preservation/SEC contacts. The subpoena’s catch-all definitions (“in whole or in part, directly or indirectly”), all formats including deleted ESI, and all Marcus-owned entities at a 10% threshold combine with “all documents” requests, 18 months of personal communications, every security traded, all fund flows, investor addresses/capital balances, all tax returns, and a continuing production obligation. Propose limits based on named custodians, VRDN/Veridian and related communications, funds that held/traded VRDN, reasonable date ranges, actual possession/custody/control, and proportionate investor/financial information. Request staged production and confidentiality safeguards for LP, personal, and tax data. Do not unilaterally redact or omit material pending agreement or court relief.

A request for all 2024 federal and state returns is facially impossible as of June 2024 for a tax year still in progress (and 2024 returns would not ordinarily yet have been filed). Identify what actually exists, preserve it, and object to the prospective/impossible element. The purported continuing duty to supplement every 30 days until the AUSA releases the recipient is not a typical Rule 17 discovery regime; reserve the issue and offer a finite, agreed rolling schedule, with later requests handled by an additional subpoena if needed.

## C. Testimony command and Fifth Amendment issues

Attachment B directs Grayfield Capital to “designate and produce” corporate representative(s) prepared to testify about 11 broad topics. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 17 does not provide the civil Rule 30(b)(6) mechanism under which an organization must designate and prepare a witness to give entity-attributed testimony after reasonable preparation. A corporation/LLC may be required to produce corporate records and appropriate custodians/individual witnesses may be subpoenaed, but this language appears to impose a collective, potentially indefinite preparation duty without naming any natural-person witness. Preserve an objection and ask the Government to identify the witness(es), topics, permissible scope, and whether it intends to subpoena individuals separately. The same-day document return and 2:00 p.m. testimony further supports sequencing and an extension.

The subpoena to the entity does not itself make any employee the entity’s witness or compel a particular person who was not individually served to appear. The entity has no Fifth Amendment privilege; individual testimony is different. A natural person who is properly subpoenaed must appear and may assert a personal Fifth Amendment privilege question by question with personal counsel, if a good-faith basis exists. Corporate records ordinarily cannot be withheld on an individual custodian’s Fifth Amendment ground. See Braswell v. United States, 487 U.S. 99 (1988). Grayfield and Zheng’s separate subpoenas (reported in Tsao’s intake) require coordinated but independent individual-counsel planning; no one should make a blanket refusal or improvise testimony.

# IV. Merits and key evidentiary issues

## A. Potential insider-trading / tipping theory

The potential theories include trading while aware of MNPI under Exchange Act §10(b) and Rule 10b-5, a classical or misappropriation breach, and tipper-tippee liability. Under Dirks v. SEC, 463 U.S. 646 (1983), tipper liability turns on breach and personal benefit, and tippee liability requires knowledge of the breach; Rule 10b5-2, 17 C.F.R. §240.10b5-2, addresses duties of trust or confidence in specified relationships. The record must establish (at minimum) material nonpublic information, a duty/breach by the source or trader, the relevant person’s knowledge and use/connection to a trade, and the requisite scienter. The confidential 89% response-rate data reportedly shared at a March 3 SAB meeting could be material if not public; an SAB member bound by a Veridian NDA appears to owe a confidentiality duty to Veridian. The exact dataset, meeting content, data maturity, and when/what Veridian publicly disclosed remain unverified.

If Ashford conveyed the data to Grayfield, a jury could infer a personal benefit from a gift of confidential information to a close family member/relative; Salman v. United States, 580 U.S. 39 (2016), treats a gift to a trading relative as sufficient personal benefit in the circumstances there. Grayfield is Ashford’s brother-in-law, rather than a sibling within the express family examples in Rule 10b5-2; do not assume an automatic presumption. The NDA and any history, agreement, confidentiality expectation, benefit, or repeated information-sharing matter. For a tippee theory, determine whether Grayfield knew or recklessly disregarded that the information was improperly disclosed. Zheng has no reported direct contact with Ashford, but a downstream tip through Grayfield remains a factual question.

The present chronology is damaging circumstantial evidence but not proof of a tip: (i) March 3 confidential SAB meeting and reported 89% data; (ii) March 4 first 45,000 fund shares (25,000 and 20,000 lots; the intake characterizes the first day/week in a different way); (iii) March 5 Grayfield’s 15,000-share personal buy; (iv) March 12 Ashford–Grayfield 22-minute call and a fund 25,000-share buy that the OMS note calls an “increased order size” day; (v) further accumulation to 550,000 shares by April 5; and (vi) sales beginning on April 8 approval day. Obtain trade-order-entry, execution, allocation, and call timestamps—date-only records cannot establish sequence. Retrieve all Ashford/Grayfield direct and indirect communications and determine what was said, what data was public, and whether the call was a routine family call.

Evidence that could support a legitimate-public-information defense includes Zheng’s pre-March 3 tracking and thesis, contemporaneous public research, the March 14 IC memo, and the March 11–25 emails describing clinical data, competing products, and position sizing. That record is not yet a safe answer: the email and memo contain material inconsistencies noted below, and they were authored/approved after the first purchases. Establish the dated source materials, access logs, drafts, author inputs, and who knew the Ashford relationship and SAB role when each decision was made. A statement of “independent research” is not self-proving.

The April 2 public 8-K reportedly announced positive topline results and VRDN rose about 4%; the April 8 FDA approval reportedly drove a roughly 47% rise. The fund continued buying through April 5, then sold 400,000 shares over April 8–12 and retained 150,000; Grayfield personally sold 10,000 on April 9 and retained 5,000. The IC memo’s exit strategy contemplated reducing 60–75% within the first week after approval, broadly consistent with the actual 400,000/550,000 sale. This is relevant to motive, profit, and timing, but not dispositive of prior MNPI use.

## B. Personal trading and control failures

Grayfield is expressly an Access Person under the Code. The reported March 5 purchase (15,000 shares at \$41.90; \$628,500 cost) and April 9 sale (10,000 at \$60.50; \$605,000 proceeds; \$186,000 stated gross profit) are Covered Securities transactions in a Reportable Account. Tsao says neither was pre-cleared. The Code requires prior written approval at least one business day before a trade and requires duplicate statements/confirmations. Obtain the portal/email logs and Fielding & Strauss statements rather than relying on the intake summary.

Separately, the Code’s Blackout Period starts when an investment decision is communicated and extends until two business days after the Firm has completed trading in the same security for all client accounts. The fund was trading VRDN from March 4 through April 12; on the stated dates both Grayfield’s March 5 buy and April 9 sale occurred during fund trading (and the buy followed the fund’s initial trades). Subject to confirming the operative policy/version and exact trade dates, both appear to violate the written blackout even apart from any MNPI. Check whether the restricted/watch list, required family-relationship disclosure, and any exposure report were completed; the IC memo’s assertion that VRDN was not on either list is not a substitute for those records.

Clearwater’s November 2023 audit reports 38 of 247 sampled trades (15.4%) without timely documented pre-clearance; 12 of the 23 senior-personnel misses were Grayfield’s, and 7 were never documented by audit close. It records Grayfield describing the requirement as “more of a formality,” resistance to oversight, and missed training. This is relevant to the adequacy of the firm’s compliance program, notice/intent, and credibility; it also gives Grayfield an individual-versus-entity defense conflict. The audit predates the trades at issue, so it cannot by itself prove the later trades were unapproved, but makes historical pre-clearance records central. The audit’s findings and the known off-channel gap also create potential independent adviser-compliance exposure under Advisers Act §§204A and 206(4)-7 and the books-and-records rule, 17 C.F.R. §275.204-2, apart from any insider-trading case; the entity should not assume the investigation will be confined to individuals.
