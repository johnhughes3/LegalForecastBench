# III. Threshold Issues with the Subpoena Itself

## A. Authenticity and Identity of the Issuing AUSA

The subpoena names the issuing AUSA as "Jonathan Cromdale Consulting" throughout, but the email address listed is **jonathan.mercer@usdoj.gov**. The surname "Consulting" looks like a drafting or template error, and the mismatch between name and email needs to be resolved before we communicate substantively. The proof of service is also unsigned, with the server’s name and title left blank. The registered agent ("DC Statutory Agent") is listed at a New York address.

**Recommendation:** Before any substantive contact, verify the AUSA’s identity and the matter through the SDNY USAO main switchboard (a number we look up independently, not the number on the subpoena) and the Securities and Commodities Fraud Task Force. Ask for the correct spelling of the AUSA’s name and the right email address, and confirm service details with the registered agent. I do not recommend challenging service. Grayfield actually received the subpoena, and a service challenge would buy nothing and cost goodwill. We should, however, record the defects in our file.

## B. Attachment B — Compelled Corporate Designee Testimony

Attachment B commands Grayfield to "designate and produce" corporate representatives prepared to testify on 11 topics. This borrows from civil Rule 30(b)(6); Rule 17 has no equivalent mechanism in the grand jury context. A Rule 17(c) subpoena duces tecum compels documents, and testimony is ordinarily compelled from a named individual. Several practical problems also arise:

- The obvious designees are Marcus, Zheng, Tsao and possibly Mehta, and each is a percipient witness with potential personal exposure. Any of them could assert the Fifth Amendment individually. The entity has no Fifth Amendment privilege (\*Braswell v. United States\*, 487 U.S. 99 (1988); \*Hale v. Henkel\*), but it cannot force an individual to give up his.

- A prepared designee would be giving sworn testimony on topics (for example, Topics 4, 7, 9 and 10) that effectively concede the Firm’s positions on the central disputed facts, before our internal investigation is done.

- Topic 9 (preservation steps after the SEC Formal Order) would require sworn testimony about the iPhone trade-in and the Signal issue before we have decided on a disclosure strategy.

- Scheduling testimony on the same day as the document return date is unworkable.

**Recommendation:** Ask the AUSA to withdraw or adjourn Attachment B indefinitely. In its place, offer (i) a custodian-of-records certification or declaration to authenticate business records (FRE 803(6), 902(11)), and (ii) an attorney proffer on organizational structure and document-retention systems (Topics 1, 8). Substantive topics should await completion of our investigation. If the government insists, we should consider a motion to quash or modify Attachment B under Rule 17(c)(2).

## C. Legal Standard for Challenging Requests

Grand jury subpoenas are presumed reasonable. A motion to quash on relevance grounds must fail unless "there is no reasonable possibility that the category of materials the Government seeks will produce information relevant to the general subject of the grand jury’s investigation." \*United States v. R. Enterprises, Inc.\*, 498 U.S. 292, 301 (1991). Overbreadth and burden arguments stand a somewhat better chance when a request reaches unrelated entities or would impose unreasonable cost. Overall, our leverage is in negotiation, not motion practice. We should approach the AUSA constructively and hold litigation in reserve for the corporate-testimony demand and for any privilege dispute.

## D. Parallel SEC Proceeding

SEC HO-14492 covers substantially the same conduct, and the SEC has separately subpoenaed Marcus and Zheng personally. Request 7 seeks everything produced to the SEC. We should treat every SEC production as a grand jury production (and the reverse), keep one Bates-numbered production set and one privilege log, and assume information is shared between the two agencies. Any SEC testimony by Marcus or Zheng raises Fifth Amendment waiver issues that their separate counsel must evaluate before they appear.

# V. Substantive Document Issues

## A. Is the Clearwater Audit Report Privileged? (Tsao Question 1)

**Short answer: almost certainly not. We should not assert privilege over it.**

- **Attorney-client privilege** requires a communication with counsel made to obtain legal advice. The report is from a non-lawyer consultant to a non-lawyer CCO. Ms. Tsao concedes that no attorney directed, oversaw or received it. \*Kovel\* (296 F.2d 918 (2d Cir. 1961)) protects consultants only when they help a lawyer give legal advice, and there was no lawyer here. Under the Second Circuit’s predominant-purpose test (\*In re County of Erie\*, 473 F.3d 413 (2d Cir. 2007)), the report fails on its face, since it describes itself as "the Firm’s annual compliance review for calendar year 2023." Rule 206(4)-7 requires that review as a regulatory matter; it is a business function.

- **Work product** requires a document prepared "because of" anticipated litigation (\*United States v. Adlman\*, 134 F.3d 1194 (2d Cir. 1998)). Clearwater was engaged in September 2023 for a routine annual review, months before any investigation. The report would have been prepared in substantially the same form regardless of litigation.

- **The legend does not help, and it may hurt.** A "privileged" label does not create privilege. Here the legend states something false ("prepared at the direction of counsel"). Putting the report on a privilege log based on that legend would mislead the government, and the report’s own text ("commissioned at the direction of Ms. Tsao in her capacity as Chief Compliance Officer") would contradict us. Our credibility with the AUSA is more valuable than this document.

- **Waiver through distribution** is also possible if the report or its findings were shared with Marcus or others, or given to the SEC during examinations.

**Substantive impact.** The report cuts both ways. For the entity, it shows that the CCO commissioned an independent review, identified the problem and tried to escalate it. It is also the strongest evidence that the pre-clearance lapses were attributable to Marcus personally ("more of a formality"; "historically resisted"), not to a systemic failure. That directly contradicts the intake memo’s description of "administrative lapses rather than intentional circumvention." Against the entity, it shows the Firm was **on written notice in November 2023** of (i) Marcus’s non-compliance, (ii) the off-channel and ephemeral-messaging gap, and (iii) inadequate compliance staffing. Four months later, Marcus made an unprecleared trade in VRDN during a blackout period. The SEC will use this to support a Rule 206(4)-7 claim, a Section 204A claim (failure to enforce MNPI policies), and failure-to-supervise theories. The DOJ will use it in evaluating the Firm’s compliance program (Justice Manual 9-28.800; the Criminal Division’s Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs (updated March 2023), which specifically addresses ephemeral messaging).

**Recommendation:** Produce the report and the confidential appendix, and prepare to present the Firm’s remediation history. We should obtain documentary proof of the remediation steps Ms. Tsao claims (the electronic pre-clearance system, and when it went live), because the March 5, 2024 trade suggests they were ineffective or not applied to Marcus. By contrast, the Ridgeline engagement (retained by W&C on June 10) is properly protected work product under \*Kovel\*. We should confirm the engagement letter runs from W&C and states its litigation purpose.

## D. The March 5 Fund Fill That Matches Marcus’s Personal Trade

The blotter records a fund purchase on March 5, 2024 (GOF-240305-001) of **15,000 shares at \$41.90 for \$628,500, through Fielding & Strauss**. Marcus’s personal purchase that same day, through the same broker, was **15,000 shares at \$41.90 for \$628,500**. ## E. Code of Ethics Violations Beyond Pre-Clearance

The intake memo presents Marcus’s March 5 trade as a pre-clearance "oversight" by his assistant. The Code shows broader violations:

- **Blackout period (Code § IV.C).** Access persons may not trade while the Firm is trading or contemplating trading the same security, until two business days after the Firm finishes. # VI. Preservation, Spoliation and Disclosure (Tsao Question 2)

## A. Deficiencies in the June 6 Preservation Notice

- **Late.** The duty to preserve attached no later than May 15, 2024, when the SEC Formal Order was served (arguably earlier, if an investigation was reasonably anticipated after the April trading). The Code (§ X.C) required the CCO to issue a hold upon a regulatory investigation. None was issued for 22 days, and the iPhone was traded in during that gap.

- **Scope gaps.** The notice covers Firm systems and Firm-issued devices only. It does not expressly reach personal devices, personal email, text or iMessage, Signal, WhatsApp or other messaging apps. It does not tell anyone to disable auto-delete or disappearing-message settings. The subpoena (Instr. 3) expressly demands all of these. The notice’s general line that employees are "individually responsible" for materials outside Firm systems is not enough.

- **Subject-matter scope is narrower than the subpoena.** The notice is limited to Veridian topics. The subpoena reaches the entire Opportunity Fund blotter, entity governance, bank records, tax returns, investor records and all Marcus and Zheng communications.

- **The CCO as gatekeeper.** The notice directs that "the Compliance Office will make all relevance determinations." Ms. Tsao is a fact witness, is individually named in Request 4, and reports to Marcus. Relevance and collection decisions must move to counsel and an independent vendor.

- **Whistleblower-impeding language.** The notice prohibits discussing the investigation with "any person outside the Firm" without Compliance authorization, and Code § IX.C bars responding to any government inquiry without CCO or counsel authorization. Neither has a carve-out for communications with the SEC, DOJ or other regulators. **This language risks violating Exchange Act Rule 21F-17(a)**, which the SEC has enforced in standalone actions. In a criminal investigation it could also be portrayed as an effort to discourage witness cooperation.

- **Privilege legend.** The notice claims privilege, but it was issued by a non-lawyer to all employees. Given the spoliation issues, expect the notice and its acknowledgments to become discoverable.

**Recommendation:** W&C should issue a superseding hold notice within 24–48 hours. It should (i) cover all subpoena categories; (ii) expressly cover personal devices, personal email, SMS/iMessage and all messaging apps; (iii) direct custodians to **disable disappearing-message and auto-delete settings immediately** and not to delete apps, reset or replace devices; (iv) include a clear Rule 21F-17 carve-out confirming that nothing restricts communications with any government agency; (v) direct questions to W&C; and (vi) require written acknowledgment. Code § IX.C and the confidentiality provisions should be amended accordingly. We should also confirm IT actually suspended Exchange auto-deletion and backup rotation, and preserve Smarsh exports.

Joint representation of a constituent is permitted under Rule 1.13(d) only if Rule 1.7 is satisfied. Here the conflict is concurrent, direct and, in my view, **non-consentable** under Rule 1.7(b), because no lawyer could reasonably believe we could provide competent and diligent representation to both:

# VIII. Substantive Exposure — Tipper-Tippee Analysis (Tsao Question 5)

**Theories.** The government has two paths. (1) **Misappropriation** (\*United States v. O’Hagan\*, 521 U.S. 642 (1997); Rule 10b5-2): Ashford owed Veridian a duty of trust and confidence under his SAB confidentiality agreement and misappropriated the SAB data by tipping it. (2) **Classical/tipper-tippee** (\*Dirks\*): as an SAB member, Ashford may be treated as a temporary insider. Under either theory, tippee liability requires that the tipper received a personal benefit and that the tippee knew or should have known of the breach. **Salman** makes personal benefit easy to establish where the tip is a gift to a relative, and a brother-in-law fits comfortably (\*see also United States v. Martoma\*, 894 F.3d 64 (2d Cir. 2017)). The government may also charge under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (Title 18 securities fraud), which in the Second Circuit does not carry the \*Dirks\* personal-benefit requirement for corporate confidential information (\*United States v. Blaszczak\*, 947 F.3d 19 (2d Cir. 2019), vacated on other grounds as to government information after \*Kelly\*; the corporate-property theory of \*Carpenter\* remains intact).

**Entity exposure.** Grayfield can be held criminally liable under respondeat superior for employees acting within the scope of employment and at least partly to benefit the entity. Trading for the fund plainly meets that test. The Firm also faces civil exposure: disgorgement of approximately \$9.2 million in fund gains; civil penalties under Exchange Act § 21A, including controlling-person penalties of up to the greater of \$1 million or three times the profit gained if the Firm knowingly or recklessly failed to take appropriate steps to prevent the violation (§ 21A(b)(1)), which the Clearwater report makes a live risk; Advisers Act §§ 204A, 206(4) / Rule 206(4)-7, and Rule 204-2 (recordkeeping) violations; possible § 206 violations if the March 5 allocation was improper; and investor claims and redemptions.

**The "one-day gap" (March 3 SAB meeting to March 4 first trade).** This is the government’s strongest timing fact, and the intake memo’s answer ("Zheng’s independent research") is currently undocumented. Key points:

- The March 12 call **cannot explain** the March 4 trade. The critical question is whether Ashford and Marcus (or Zheng) communicated on March 3–4, a Sunday and Monday. Carrier records will answer this, and the government likely already has them. The call record we know about is only the one Ms. Tsao identified.

- Who made the March 4 decision? We need to establish whether Marcus directed Zheng to begin buying on March 3 or 4, and through what channel (the lost iPhone, Signal, in person).

- Exculpatory evidence would include pre-March 3 research files, model timestamps, sell-side research downloads, expert-network logs, Bloomberg alerts, earlier watch lists or pipeline documents mentioning VRDN, and any public catalyst on or around March 1–4 (for example, a conference presentation or public PDUFA date confirmation). Ridgeline should also examine market-wide VRDN volume and price on March 4 and review Zheng’s other biotech event-driven trades for a consistent approach (Request 8 may actually help here).

- The fund kept buying 140,000 shares **after** the public April 2 8-K, and the IC memo’s exit strategy was set out in advance. Both are helpful. Nevertheless, the concentration (the largest single-name biotech bet we are aware of), the timing and the family relationship make this a case the government will likely pursue at least against individuals.

**Preliminary assessment:** Criminal exposure for Marcus is serious. Zheng’s exposure depends on what he knew about the source of the information. The entity’s exposure is significant, and its best mitigation lies in prompt, credible cooperation and remediation: independent governance, disclosure of the data loss, a genuine investigation, and personnel action if warranted. SDNY’s corporate voluntary self-disclosure and cooperation policies, and the DOJ Criminal Division Corporate Enforcement Policy, should be evaluated as the facts develop, since the entity’s interests may favor early cooperation.
