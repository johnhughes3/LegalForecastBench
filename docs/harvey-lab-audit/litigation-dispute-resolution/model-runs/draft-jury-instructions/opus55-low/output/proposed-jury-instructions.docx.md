**Count I — Title VII Retaliation: Elements**

Plaintiff claims that Creston terminated her employment in retaliation for her internal complaint of sex-based pay discrimination filed with Creston's Human Resources Department on January 17, 2023. To prevail on this claim, Plaintiff must prove each of the following elements by a preponderance of the evidence:

1.  Plaintiff engaged in activity protected by Title VII;

2.  Creston took a materially adverse action against Plaintiff; and

3.  Plaintiff's protected activity was the but-for cause of Creston's decision to terminate her employment, as I will define that term for you in Instruction No. 3.

I have already determined that Plaintiff's January 17, 2023 internal complaint of sex-based pay discrimination was protected activity under Title VII, and the parties agree that the termination of Plaintiff's employment on August 3, 2023 was a materially adverse action. You should therefore treat the first two elements as established. The question for you on this claim is causation.

For purposes of this Title VII claim only, the protected activity you may consider is Plaintiff's complaint of sex-based pay discrimination. Plaintiff's March 9, 2023 complaint to the Ohio Environmental Protection Agency about waste disposal is not activity protected by Title VII, because Title VII protects only opposition to discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin and participation in proceedings under Title VII. You may consider evidence regarding the Ohio EPA complaint only in connection with Plaintiff's Ohio Whistleblower claim, and, to the extent it helps you understand the reasons for Creston's decision, in deciding whether Plaintiff's pay-discrimination complaint was the but-for cause of her termination. You may not find for Plaintiff on her Title VII claim on the ground that Creston acted because of her Ohio EPA complaint.

*Authority:* 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a); *Laster v. City of Kalamazoo*, 746 F.3d 714, 730 (6th Cir. 2014); Order §§ II.B, IV.A & VI ¶ 2(a); Eleventh Circuit Pattern Civil Jury Instruction 4.22 \[verify\]; Seventh Circuit Pattern Civil Jury Instruction 3.02 \[verify\].

# DEFENDANT'S PROPOSED INSTRUCTION NO. 3

**Count I — But-For Causation**

To establish causation on her Title VII claim, Plaintiff must prove that her January 17, 2023 complaint of sex-based pay discrimination was the "but-for" cause of Creston's decision to terminate her employment. This means Plaintiff must prove that Creston would not have terminated her employment in the absence of that complaint — in other words, that if she had not made the complaint, and everything else had remained the same, she would not have been terminated.

It is not enough for Plaintiff to prove that her complaint was a motivating factor, a contributing factor, or one of several things Creston considered. The but-for standard is a stricter standard than the one I will describe for you on Plaintiff's Ohio Whistleblower claim, and you must not confuse the two. Plaintiff's complaint need not have been the only cause of her termination, but it must have been a cause without which the termination would not have occurred.

If you find that Creston would have terminated Plaintiff's employment on August 3, 2023, even if she had not made her complaint of sex-based pay discrimination — for example, because her position would have been eliminated in the reduction in force regardless — then you must find for Creston on this claim.

*Authority:* *Univ. of Tex. Sw. Med. Ctr. v. Nassar*, 570 U.S. 338, 360, 362 (2013) (retaliation claims "must be proved according to traditional principles of but-for causation," which "requires proof that the unlawful retaliation would not have occurred in the absence of the alleged wrongful action or actions of the employer"); *Bostock v. Clayton County*, 590 U.S. 644, 656 (2020) (but-for cause need not be sole cause); Order §§ IV.A, V.B.

*Drafting Note (delete before filing):* The "need not be the only cause" sentence is included deliberately. It is an accurate statement of *Bostock* and *Nassar*; the Court will almost certainly give it, and offering it ourselves makes the rest of the instruction (including the "would have terminated regardless" paragraph) more likely to be adopted verbatim.

# DEFENDANT'S PROPOSED INSTRUCTION NO. 4

**Count I — Timing of Events**

In deciding whether Plaintiff has proved causation, you may consider the timing of events, including the length of time between Plaintiff's protected activity and Creston's decision to terminate her employment. Timing is one circumstance you may weigh together with all of the other evidence. A short interval between protected activity and an adverse action may, together with other evidence, support an inference of causation; a longer interval may weaken that inference. The fact that an adverse action followed protected activity does not, by itself, prove that the protected activity caused the adverse action. You must decide causation on the basis of all of the evidence.

*Authority:* *Clark Cnty. Sch. Dist. v. Breeden*, 532 U.S. 268, 273–74 (2001) (per curiam); *Mickey v. Zeidler Tool & Die Co.*, 516 F.3d 516, 525 (6th Cir. 2008); *Nassar*, 570 U.S. at 362; Order § II.D.

*Drafting Note (delete before filing):* This replaces Pretrial Brief No. 6A. The brief cited *Mickey* for the proposition that proximity beyond "a few weeks" is insufficient; *Mickey* actually held that very close temporal proximity can by itself suffice to establish the causal element of a prima facie case, and noted that where time has elapsed the plaintiff must couple timing with other evidence. The Order cites *Mickey* for that latter point. Do not repeat the brief's characterization. Note also that the Court has characterized Exhibit 14 as "bridg\[ing\] the temporal gap"; a one-sided timing instruction is unlikely to be given.

# DEFENDANT'S PROPOSED INSTRUCTION NO. 5

**Legitimate Business Reason; Reduction in Force; Business Judgment**

Creston contends that it terminated Plaintiff's employment because her position was eliminated in a company-wide reduction in force undertaken for financial reasons, and not because of any protected activity. Creston does not have the burden of proving that its stated reason was the actual reason for its decision. Plaintiff at all times retains the burden of proving that retaliation was the but-for cause of her termination (on her Title VII claim) or a contributing factor in her termination (on her Ohio Whistleblower claim).

You may consider whether Creston's stated reason is believable. If you find that Creston's stated reason is not the true reason for its decision, you may, but are not required to, infer that the true reason was retaliation. You must consider all of the evidence in deciding what the true reason was.

An employer is entitled to make business decisions, including decisions to reduce costs, reorganize, consolidate job duties, and eliminate positions, for any reason that is not unlawful, even if you disagree with the decision or believe it was unwise, harsh, or mistaken. You should not find for Plaintiff merely because you would have made a different decision. The question is not whether Creston's decision was wise or fair, but whether it was made in retaliation for protected activity.

*Authority:* *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 143, 147–48 (2000); *St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 507–11 (1993); *Tex. Dep't of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 253–56 (1981); Seventh Circuit Pattern Civil Jury Instruction 3.07 \[verify\]; Eleventh Circuit Pattern Civil Jury Instruction 4.22 (business-judgment language) \[verify\].

*Drafting Note (delete before filing):* Some Sixth Circuit panels have discouraged giving the McDonnell Douglas burden-shifting framework to juries; this instruction avoids burden-shifting terminology and states only the ultimate-burden and pretext principles. Do not argue that the RIF was "already in development" on March 22, 2023 unless we have documents pre-dating Exhibit 14; the record in the Order shows the plan was approved April 15, 2023.

# DEFENDANT'S PROPOSED INSTRUCTION NO. 9

**Count II — Contributing-Factor Causation**

To establish causation on her Ohio Whistleblower claim, Plaintiff must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that her March 9, 2023 report to the Ohio EPA was a contributing factor in Creston's decision to terminate her employment. A "contributing factor" is a factor that actually played a part in the decision. The report need not have been the only reason or the principal reason for the decision, but Plaintiff must prove that it was in fact one of the reasons Creston made the decision. It is not enough that the report preceded the decision in time, or that the decision-maker was aware of the report, unless you also find that the report actually played a part in the decision.

This standard is different from, and less demanding than, the but-for standard that applies to Plaintiff's Title VII claim. You must apply the contributing-factor standard only to the Ohio Whistleblower claim, and the but-for standard only to the Title VII claim.

If you find that Plaintiff's Ohio EPA report played no part in Creston's decision to terminate her employment, you must find for Creston on this claim.

*Authority:* Order §§ IV.B, V.B (adopting contributing-factor standard for Count II and directing differentiated instructions); *Contreras*, 73 Ohio St. 3d at 248 \[verify that Contreras articulates this standard\]; cf. *Nassar*, 570 U.S. at 362.

*Drafting Note (delete before filing):* This replaces Pretrial Brief No. 7. The brief's definition — "any factor which, alone or in connection with other factors, tends to affect in any way the outcome" — is borrowed from federal whistleblower statutes (e.g., SOX/AIR-21 case law) and is the most plaintiff-favorable formulation available; Defendant should not volunteer it. The formulation above is accurate but requires that the report "actually played a part." Consider preserving (by separate filing or at the charge conference) an objection that Ohio law does not adopt a contributing-factor standard for § 4113.52; the Order adopted it and we should not contest it in the instruction itself.

# DEFENDANT'S PROPOSED INSTRUCTION NO. 10

**Count IV — Breach of Implied Employment Contract**

Plaintiff's third claim is that Creston breached an implied contract of employment by terminating her without following the progressive-discipline procedure described on pages 27 and 28 of Creston's Employee Handbook.

Under Ohio law, employment is presumed to be "at will," which means that either the employer or the employee may end the employment relationship at any time and for any reason that is not otherwise unlawful. Plaintiff has the burden of overcoming that presumption. To prevail on this claim, Plaintiff must prove each of the following by a preponderance of the evidence:

10. That Creston and Plaintiff mutually intended that the progressive-discipline provision of the Handbook would be a binding term of Plaintiff's employment that limited Creston's right to terminate her;

11. That the progressive-discipline provision, by its terms, applied to the termination of Plaintiff's employment through the elimination of her position in a reduction in force; and

12. That Creston failed to comply with that provision and that Plaintiff suffered damages as a result.

In deciding the first question, you should consider the Handbook as a whole and all of the circumstances, including: whether the language of the progressive-discipline provision was specific and definite; the at-will provision on page 3 of the Handbook, which states that "\[n\]othing in this handbook creates a contract of employment," that employment "may be terminated by either party at any time, for any lawful reason, with or without cause," and that no one other than the Chief Executive Officer, in a signed writing, may make a contrary agreement; whether that provision was clear and conspicuous; the Handbook's statement that Creston reserved the right to revise or rescind any policy at any time; the statement on page 28 that Creston "reserves the right to determine the appropriate level of discipline in each individual situation" and that steps "may be repeated, combined, or skipped"; the parties' stipulation that Plaintiff signed an acknowledgment on September 14, 2021 confirming that she had received and read the Handbook, including the at-will provision; and whether Plaintiff actually relied on the progressive-discipline provision. An employer's general statements of policy, procedures, or expectations in a handbook do not by themselves create contractual obligations.

In deciding the second question, you should consider the language of the provision and its context. The provision appears in a section titled "Performance Management and Discipline," which states that the procedures are established "to address performance deficiencies and violations of company policy." It is undisputed that Plaintiff's termination was not based on her performance or conduct. If you find that the progressive-discipline provision applies only to discipline for performance or conduct, and not to the elimination of a position for business reasons, you must find for Creston on this claim, whether or not you find that the provision was otherwise a binding term of employment.

*Authority:* *Mers v. Dispatch Printing Co.*, 19 Ohio St. 3d 100, 103–04 (1985); *Karnes v. Doctors Hosp.*, 51 Ohio St. 3d 139, 142 (1990); *Wing v. Anchor Media, Ltd. of Tex.*, 59 Ohio St. 3d 108, 110–11 (1991); Joint Ex. 1 at 3, 27–28; Joint Pretrial Stipulation ¶¶ 12–13; Order §§ II.G, IV.D.

*Drafting Note (delete before filing):* This replaces Pretrial Brief No. 9, which directed a defense verdict if the disclaimer was "clear and conspicuous." The Order (§ IV.D) frames a multi-factor jury question and describes *Karnes* as holding that a general disclaimer may be insufficient; the brief's version is very unlikely to be given. Our reading of *Karnes* is that the Ohio Supreme Court held that, absent fraud in the inducement, a handbook disclaimer stating that employment is at will precludes a non-at-will contract based on the handbook. If confirmed, that argument should be preserved by Rule 50(a) motion and objection, not by an instruction that contradicts the Order. The brief's acknowledgment date (Sept. 15, 2021) conflicts with Stipulation ¶ 13 (Sept. 14, 2021); the stipulation controls.

**Damages — General Principles**

If you find for Creston on a claim, you will not consider damages on that claim. The fact that I am instructing you on damages does not mean that I have any view as to which party should prevail.

If you find for Plaintiff on one or more claims, you must determine the damages, if any, that Plaintiff has proved by a preponderance of the evidence were caused by the conduct on which you based your finding of liability. Damages must be reasonable and must be based on evidence, not on speculation, guesswork, or sympathy. Plaintiff may not recover for losses that are remote, speculative, or not caused by Creston's unlawful conduct.

The verdict form asks you to state damages separately for each claim and for each category of damages. Plaintiff may not recover more than once for the same loss. If you award damages for the same loss under more than one claim, record the amount on each claim as instructed on the verdict form; the Court will ensure that Plaintiff does not recover twice for the same loss. You should not add amounts together or adjust any award for that reason.

Damages for emotional distress and punitive damages are not recoverable on Plaintiff's breach-of-contract claim.

*Authority:* *Memphis Cmty. Sch. Dist. v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 306–08 (1986); *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 264 (1978); 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(c)(2) (jury not to be informed of statutory cap); OJI-CV 315.01 \[verify\]; Order § V.B; Standing Order § IV.

The claim-by-claim breakdown on the verdict form is what allows the cap to be applied post-verdict and prevents uncapped state-law awards from absorbing Title VII damages.

# DEFENDANT'S PROPOSED INSTRUCTION NO. 13

**Mitigation of Damages**

A person who claims lost wages must make reasonable efforts to reduce those losses by seeking and accepting other suitable employment. This is called "mitigation of damages." Creston has the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that Plaintiff failed to mitigate her damages.

To establish this defense, Creston must prove either of the following:

\(a\) that substantially equivalent employment was available to Plaintiff and that Plaintiff failed to use reasonable diligence in seeking it; or

\(b\) that Plaintiff was offered a position that was substantially equivalent to her position at Creston, and that she unreasonably refused it.

"Substantially equivalent employment" means a position that offers virtually identical promotional opportunities, compensation, job responsibilities, working conditions, and status. Plaintiff was not required to accept a position that was not substantially equivalent, or to go into another line of work, accept a demotion, or take a demeaning position. Reasonable diligence does not require that Plaintiff's efforts be successful, but it does require a good-faith, reasonable effort.

If you find that Creston has proved that Plaintiff failed to use reasonable diligence during some period, you must reduce any award of lost wages by the amount Plaintiff reasonably could have earned during that period. If you find that Creston has proved that Plaintiff unreasonably refused a substantially equivalent position, then for the period after the date she could have begun that position, you may award lost wages only in the amount, if any, by which her Creston compensation exceeded the compensation of the position she refused.

*Authority:* *Ford Motor Co. v. EEOC*, 458 U.S. 219, 231–32 & n.15 (1982); *Rasimas v. Mich. Dep't of Mental Health*, 714 F.2d 614, 623–24 (6th Cir. 1983) (employer bears burden of proving failure to mitigate; defining substantially equivalent employment); *Killian v. Yorozu Auto. Tenn., Inc.*, 454 F.3d 549, 556–57 (6th Cir. 2006) \[verify\]; Seventh Circuit Pattern Civil Jury Instruction 3.11 \[verify\].

# DEFENDANT'S PROPOSED INSTRUCTION NO. 16

**Punitive Damages — Title VII Claim Only; Good-Faith Compliance**

If you find for Plaintiff on her Title VII claim and award her compensatory damages or lost wages, you must then decide whether Plaintiff has proved that she is entitled to punitive damages. Punitive damages may be considered only on the Title VII claim. You may not award punitive damages on the Ohio Whistleblower claim or the breach-of-contract claim.

Punitive damages are not favored and are never required. To be eligible for punitive damages, Plaintiff must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that a managerial employee of Creston, acting within the scope of his or her employment, retaliated against her with malice or reckless indifference to her federally protected rights. This means that the managerial employee knew that retaliating against Plaintiff for her pay-discrimination complaint would violate federal law, or acted in the face of a perceived risk that it would violate federal law. It is not enough that the retaliation was intentional; Plaintiff must prove that the managerial employee acted with knowledge or perceived risk of violating federal law.

Even if Plaintiff proves that a managerial employee acted with malice or reckless indifference, you may not award punitive damages against Creston if Creston proves by a preponderance of the evidence that it made good-faith efforts to comply with Title VII and that the managerial employee's conduct was contrary to those efforts. In deciding whether Creston made good-faith efforts to comply, you may consider whether Creston adopted a written anti-retaliation policy, whether it communicated the policy to employees, whether it trained managers on the policy, whether it provided channels for reporting concerns, and whether it enforced the policy in practice, including how it responded to complaints. A written policy alone is not sufficient; Creston must have made a good-faith effort to implement it. The question is whether Creston made sincere and meaningful efforts to comply with the law, not whether those efforts succeeded in preventing the conduct at issue.

If you decide to award punitive damages, the amount should be what is reasonably necessary to punish Creston for the conduct you found and to deter similar conduct, and must bear a reasonable relationship to the harm Plaintiff suffered. You may not award punitive damages to punish Creston for conduct directed at anyone other than Plaintiff.

Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408, 419–23 (2003); *Philip Morris USA v. Williams*, 549 U.S. 346, 353–55 (2007); Order §§ IV.A, V.A; Ohio Rev. Code § 4113.52(E) \[verify absence of punitive remedy\].
