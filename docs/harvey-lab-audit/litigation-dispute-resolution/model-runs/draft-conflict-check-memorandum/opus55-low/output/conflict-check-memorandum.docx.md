## C. Governing Standards

The Illinois Rules of Professional Conduct of 2010 apply, principally:

- **Rule 1.7:** concurrent conflicts, covering both direct adversity and the material-limitation risk from duties to others or the lawyer's personal interests. Consent requires a reasonable belief that competent and diligent representation is possible, and informed consent from each affected client.

- **Rule 1.9:** duties to former clients. A lawyer may not act against a former client in a substantially related matter. A lawyer may not use or reveal the former client's information.

- **Rule 1.10:** imputation. Under Rule 1.10(a), personal-interest conflicts that do not create a significant risk of limiting the representation are not imputed. Under Rule 1.10(e), a lateral's Rule 1.9 conflict is not imputed if the lawyer is timely screened, receives no part of the fee, and the former client gets prompt written notice.

- **Rule 1.18:** duties to prospective clients.

- **Rule 1.6:** confidentiality.

- **Rule 1.5(c):** contingent-fee agreements must be in a writing signed by the client.

- **Rules 5.1 and 5.3:** supervisory duties, including over non-lawyer staff and screening practices.

## A. Hit 3 — Marcus Reilly's Prior Representation of TriPoint (Trident Sensor Solutions LLC) — HIGH

**Facts.** At Castellan Merritt LLP (2012–2016), Reilly was lead counsel for Trident in two matters:

- EEOC Charge No. 440-2013-01782, an age-discrimination defense resolved in 2014

- Cook County Case No. 2015 L 004218, a wrongful-termination defense resolved in 2016

He also personally advised Trident's then-CEO on restructuring the engineering division. His 2016 lateral disclosure lists what he had access to:

- personnel files and performance reviews for engineering-division employees

- organizational charts and reporting structures

- compensation data, salary bands and **retention incentives**

- **compensation and retention strategies for key engineering personnel**

- **strategic plans for the division's technical direction**

- **internal assessments of engineering personnel's capabilities and roles**

Reilly himself recommended in 2016 that any engagement adverse to this entity be reviewed first by the General Counsel, noting possible issues under Rules 1.9 and 1.10.

**Former-client status and identity.** The Trident attorney-client relationship ended when Reilly left Castellan Merritt, so TriPoint is a former client. The March 2017 rebrand does not change that: TriPoint is the same entity, at the same Schaumburg address. ConflictTracker correctly matched the alias.

**Substantial relationship (Rule 1.9(a)).** Matters are substantially related if they involve the same transaction or legal dispute, or if there is a substantial risk that confidential information normally obtained in the earlier matter would materially advance the new client's position (Rule 1.9, cmt. \[3\]). The legal theories differ: employment defense then, trade secrets now. But the factual overlap is significant:

- The proposed case focuses on TriPoint's engineering division. The tortious-interference claim turns on how TriPoint recruits, pays and deploys engineering talent, including whether it targeted Kline and Torres for their Project Helix knowledge. Reilly's knowledge of TriPoint's engineering recruitment, retention and compensation strategy, and of its technical-direction plans, maps directly onto those issues. It would also inform deposition strategy, discovery requests and the TRO record.

- Reilly's knowledge came from strategic advisory work for the CEO, not just litigation files. Comment \[3\] recognizes that a lawyer who has learned a client's internal "playbook" may be conflicted in later matters even where the claims differ.

- **Mitigating factors:** the information is 8–10 years old; Kline and Torres did not join until 2024; Ridgeline has owned TriPoint since 2021; and Reilly kept no files. Staleness can reduce materiality (cmt. \[3\]). But strategic and cultural knowledge of the same division, gained from the CEO, does not become obsolete in the way ordinary personnel data does. Given the injunction stakes and a certain disqualification motion from TriPoint, we should not rely on staleness.

**Conclusion.** Reilly is personally disqualified from representing Verano against TriPoint unless TriPoint gives informed written consent. Consent is unrealistic and should not be sought, because asking would reveal the engagement before filing. Reilly may not serve as Lead or in any role.

**Imputation (Rule 1.10).** Reilly's disqualification arises from his association with a prior firm, so it can be kept from spreading to the whole Firm under Illinois Rule 1.10(e) if the Firm:

1.  screens Reilly in time from any participation in the matter;

2.  gives him no part of the fee from it; and

3.  promptly gives TriPoint written notice so it can confirm compliance.

**Timeliness concerns.** Ms. Ruiz-Morrison says she has "spoken informally" with Reilly, and he is listed as Lead on the intake form. Before the screen is certified, Reilly should confirm in writing:

- what Verano information he received;

- whether he shared any TriPoint or Trident information with anyone at the Firm, including Voss, who prepared the intake form; and

- that he has done no work on the matter.

If Reilly has already passed TriPoint confidences to the team, a screen may not cure the problem. The Ethics Committee would then need to consider whether the Firm as a whole must decline.

**Notice timing.** Rule 1.10(e) notice to TriPoint must be "prompt". But notice before the TRO filing would tip off the adverse party and would disclose Verano's confidential plans without Verano's consent. We recommend the following:

- Tell Verano about this requirement now and get its informed consent to the notice.

- Put the screen in place immediately and document it.

- Send written notice to TriPoint's GC, Nathan Siddoway, at the same time as filing, or immediately afterwards.

- Document the reason for that timing in the conflicts file.

**Client communication.** Verano asked for Reilly by name. Under Rule 1.4, the Firm must explain that Reilly cannot participate. It must do so without revealing TriPoint's confidential information: saying only that he has a prior-representation conflict is enough.

## B. Hit 2 — Ridgeline Capital Partners LP (Current Client; 72% Owner of TriPoint) — HIGH

**Facts.** The Firm currently represents Ridgeline in Matter WH-2023-0088, which is active and in good standing:

- Scope: SEC regulatory advisory services for Ridgeline Fund III LP only. The engagement letter expressly excludes litigation and services for any other fund, portfolio company or affiliate (§1).

- Staffing: Partner Samuel Ottinger.

- Billing to date: 618 hours; \$387,500 in fees; invoices current.

Ridgeline acquired its 72% stake in TriPoint in March 2021 and manages about \$3.2B across three funds. Verano's claims do not name Ridgeline. However, the \$42M damages claim and an injunction that could stop a TriPoint product launch would directly affect the value of Ridgeline's controlling investment.

**Direct adversity (Rule 1.7(a)(1)).** Suing a client's affiliate is not automatically direct adversity to the client (Rule 1.7, cmt. \[34\]; ABA Formal Op. 95-390). The affiliate should be treated as the client if any of the following applies:

- the circumstances suggest the lawyer's client considers the affiliate part of the client;

- the two share management, legal functions or control so closely that they are effectively one;

- the lawyer's work for the client gave access to confidential information about the affiliate; or

- the client reasonably expects the lawyer to avoid adversity to the affiliate.

Relevant factors here:

- **Against direct adversity:** Ridgeline owns 72%, not 100%. TriPoint has its own management, CEO and GC. The engagement is narrow, limited to regulatory compliance and expressly excluding portfolio companies. Ridgeline is a sophisticated client with counsel.

- **For direct adversity, or at least unresolved:** Ridgeline controls TriPoint. **We do not yet know which Ridgeline fund holds the TriPoint stake.** If it is Fund III, the fund the Firm advises, then the Firm's SEC reporting work for Fund III (such as Form PF or ADV disclosures, portfolio valuations and investor reporting) may have exposed it to non-public TriPoint financial, valuation or business information. The engagement letter's confidentiality clause (§4) expressly covers information about Ridgeline's "portfolio companies". Litigation may also produce discovery aimed at Ridgeline, such as board-level communications about recruiting Kline and Torres or about Project Helix. Adding Ridgeline as a defendant, for example on an alter-ego or conspiracy theory, would be directly adverse to a current client and is prohibited without consent.

**Material limitation (Rule 1.7(a)(2)).** Even without direct adversity, there is a significant risk that the Firm's relationship with Ridgeline could limit its representation of Verano. Examples:

- reluctance to seek discovery from Ridgeline or Helen Strauss;

- reluctance to name Ridgeline as a defendant or pursue it as a source of recovery;

- pressure to soften settlement posture; and

- Ridgeline's value as a client, at \$625/hour.

The "hot potato" doctrine also means the Firm cannot drop Ridgeline to take Verano.

**Advance waiver (Engagement Letter §7).** Section 7.2 lets the Firm act adverse to Ridgeline "or its affiliates" in matters that are not substantially related and where no Ridgeline confidential information is used. But:

- Under **§7.3(b)**, the waiver does not apply if the Firm has received Confidential Information (§4, which includes portfolio-company information) that is material to the adverse matter. Whether that applies turns on the unresolved Fund III question.

- The waiver is general and open-ended and **does not specifically mention litigation against portfolio companies** (as ConflictTracker also notes). Under Rule 1.7, cmt. \[22\], a general waiver's effectiveness depends on how well the client reasonably understood the risks. Ridgeline's sophistication helps, but a \$42M suit seeking to enjoin a 72%-owned portfolio company's product line is the kind of material adversity a court may find the client did not specifically anticipate.

- **§7.4 requires the Firm to notify Ridgeline promptly** of any representation within the waiver's scope, so notice is required even if the waiver applies.

- §7.3(a), advocating directly contrary to Ridgeline in a matter where the Firm actively represents Ridgeline, does not appear to apply because the Firm does not represent Ridgeline in any litigation.

**Recommendation.** Take these steps before clearance:

4.  With Ottinger, and without revealing Verano's identity or plans until Verano consents, find out which Ridgeline vehicle holds TriPoint and what TriPoint information, if any, the Firm has received.

5.  If the Firm holds material TriPoint or Ridgeline confidential information, the waiver does not apply. The Firm should decline unless Ridgeline gives specific informed consent, which is unlikely given the stakes.

6.  If the Firm holds no such information, the prudent course is still to get Ridgeline's specific informed written consent. At a minimum, give §7.4 notice. Either way, disclosure to Ridgeline, TriPoint's controlling owner, requires Verano's prior informed consent (Rule 1.6), and timing should be handled as with the Rule 1.10(e) notice so the TRO is not compromised. If Ridgeline objects, the matter goes back to the Ethics Committee.

7.  Get Verano's informed written consent under Rule 1.7(b) to the Ridgeline relationship. This must include the specific limitation that the Firm will not assert claims against Ridgeline or its funds. Verano's email says its claims are "directed at TriPoint ... not at Ridgeline directly", which supports but does not replace written consent.

8.  Put an ethical wall between the Ridgeline team (Ottinger and anyone working on WH-2023-0088) and the Verano team, with ConflictTracker and document-management access restrictions.

9.  Brief Ottinger on the relationship-management implications.
