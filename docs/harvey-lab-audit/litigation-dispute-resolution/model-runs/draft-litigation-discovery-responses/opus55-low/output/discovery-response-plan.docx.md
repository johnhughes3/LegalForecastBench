## 3.4 Mandatory Content of Our Written Responses

The precedent (*Westbrook v. Harborline*, S.D. Tex. 2023) is a useful template, but **it falls short of the current Rules and our stipulation in several ways.** The draft responses fix each of these:

1.  **Rule 34(b)(2)(C) withholding statement.** Every objection must say whether responsive material is being withheld because of it. The precedent never does. D. Colo. judges regularly treat objections without that statement as waived.
2.  **Rule 34(b)(2)(B) timing.** When we say we will produce, we must give a completion date. We propose rolling production beginning June 9, with substantial completion by July 25, 2025.
3.  **Stipulation § 3(a) format designation.** For each RFP, we must state whether ESI will be produced native or as TIFF, and if TIFF, why native is not practicable. Spreadsheets, SAP/ERP exports, and structured data must be native. The precedent has no such designations. Each draft response includes a “Form of production” line.
4.  **Boilerplate general objections.** D. Colo. disfavors them. The drafts keep a short preliminary statement but make every operative objection specifically in the individual response.
5.  **Rule 36(a)(4) reasonable inquiry.** A party may claim lack of knowledge only if it states that it made a reasonable inquiry and the information it knows or can readily obtain is not enough. Precedent RFA 7 (“Denied … lacks sufficient knowledge”) would be defective here. Our drafts use the correct formulation.
6.  **Oath.** Greenleaf’s RFA Instruction 1 demands answers “under oath.” Rule 36(a)(3) requires only a writing signed by the party or its attorney, so we object to that instruction. Nothing else in these sets requires a verification. (Interrogatories would.)
7.  **Caption and signature details.** Do not carry over anything from the precedent: the S.D. Tex. caption, “Dkt. No. 22,” Harborline’s 200% cap, Tidewater, and so on. **Bar numbers and phone numbers conflict across our own filings.** The Answer lists Kessler as TX 24078391, Aldridge as 24103856, and (713) 555-0140; the precedent lists 24078653, 24102987, and (713) 555-4200. Confirm the correct figures before signing. Also confirm that Kessler, Aldridge, and Tanaka are admitted to the D. Colo. bar or have pro hac vice status.

# 4. Substantive Strategy by Issue

## 4.1 Causation: “Conforming When Shipped” (Fourth Affirmative Defense)

- Our strongest defense is that the COAs accurately reported release testing, and any non-conformity arose after delivery. The weakest link is chain of custody for the **“retained samples”** Thornfield tested. We need to know whose samples they were, how they were stored, how long they sat, and whether they came from sealed drums or from Greenleaf’s in-process tanks.
- **Immediate steps:**
  - Confirm whether Prismavale kept its own **retention samples** for both lots. If it did, propose **joint split-sample testing** under an agreed protocol with GC-MS for 1,4-dioxane and USP \<61\>/\<62\> for microbial counts. If our retains test in specification, that is the best evidence in the case. If they test out of specification, we need to know that now, before we answer RFAs 9–11 and 21 and before any mediation.
  - Serve our first RFPs and a Rule 45 subpoena to **Thornfield Analytical Labs** (Lakewood, CO) for the full data package: chain of custody, sample IDs, instrument raw data, and method validation.
  - **Plausibility check:** 1,4-dioxane is a by-product of ethoxylation. It is hard to explain how the level would rise after delivery during storage. The microbial theory (PS-302 during transit or storage) is more plausible than the 1,4-dioxane theory. We should evaluate that honestly with a consulting expert before we commit to a denial we cannot back up at trial.
- **Oakvale Freight Services.** Greenleaf’s RFP 11 names Prismavale’s carrier. If our theory is contamination or temperature abuse in transit, Oakvale is a potential Rule 14 third-party defendant. **Joinder deadline: July 14, 2025.** Pull the temperature-logger data and the carrier contract now. If the contract gives Prismavale a right to Oakvale’s records, those records are within our “control” under Rule 34 and must be produced. Decide on a tender or third-party claim by June 30.

## 4.2 The Hot Documents and Willfulness (Damages-Cap Exposure)

Greenleaf’s route around the § 12.4 cap is **willfulness** (Compl. ¶ 56). Its evidence is: (i) the Line 3 validation failure eight days before the SB-102 batch; (ii) root-cause closure on Apr. 30, after shipment; (iii) the Ferris email of Apr. 25; and (iv) Ortiz releasing PS-302 despite the EM alert. RFAs 14–16 and RFPs 10, 14, 16, 19, 20, and 21 target all four.

- **Our framing, if the facts support it:**
  - For the cleaning validation, a re-validation passed on Mar. 2, **before** the SB-102 batch was made. Under standard GMP practice, a passing re-validation clears the line for production even if the root-cause investigation is still open.
  - For EML-2024-06-10, Complaint ¶ 39 is internally inconsistent. It calls the result an “alert-level” result, then says it “exceeded … action thresholds.” Alert and action levels are different tiers. An alert-level excursion normally calls for trending, not a batch hold. **Pull the EM SOP and the actual reading.** If the reading was alert-level only, we should say so plainly in RFA 15(b).
  - The MSA contains no provision requiring disclosure of internal deviations. RFA 14(c) should be denied only as to any implied duty, not as to the fact of non-disclosure.
- **The Ferris email (Apr. 25, 2024).** Pull it and its full thread now. It is a business communication between two non-lawyers. **It is not privileged**, unless in-house or outside counsel was a participant and the email sought legal advice. Plan for it to be produced. Prepare Ferris (Sales VP, not QA) to explain it as speculation outside her area rather than a finding. Also identify **Timothy Wardell**: his role, whether he is a current or former employee, and whether he could be a whistleblower or the source of Greenleaf’s information. That affects both preservation and witness strategy.
- **Cap math risk.** Both sides use \$5,000,000 (the Mar. 2023–Feb. 2024 purchase price). Greenleaf may later argue that (a) the cap applies **per claim**, so the PS-302 “claim” gets a separate cap measured over Aug. 2023–Jul. 2024, when Year 5 minimum purchases were \$5.5M; and (b) recall costs are direct damages and fall outside the cap. Complaint ¶ 17 quotes § 12.4 as also covering **“incidental”** damages, which supports treating recall costs as capped. Our RFA 25 response admits the cap and quotes the complete category list without taking a position on how the cap is measured. Also note that Answer ¶ 72 overstates the cap (“any damages … are limited”). Correct this in the amended Answer so that we do not create a false inconsistency.

## 4.3 PS-302 and Pre-Suit Mediation (Second Affirmative Defense)

- Greenleaf’s RFA 18 asks for a blanket admission that it complied with § 14.3. We respond with a partial admission: compliance as to SB-102, denial as to PS-302. That matches Answer ¶¶ 56–58.
- **Evidence to secure:** the January 6, 2025 dispute notice and Prismavale’s January 27 response. Both are pre-mediation contract notices, not mediation communications. **We want them produced** (RFP 18), because they are the core proof of this defense.
- **Internal risk to check before production:** did Prismavale’s own mediation statement, term sheets, or Clearwater correspondence discuss PS-302? If they did, the defense is weakened in substance, and Greenleaf will argue waiver or estoppel. Withhold mediation communications under C.R.S. § 13-22-307, FRE 408, and the Clearwater confidentiality agreement. Log them categorically. But **know what they say.**
- **Practical point:** the usual remedy for skipping a pre-suit mediation step is a stay pending mediation, not dismissal. Consider offering a short PS-302 mediation to strengthen our position, or using the defense as leverage for a global mediation.

## 4.4 Section 8.1 Rejection Window (First Affirmative Defense)

This is our weakest defense. Complaint ¶¶ 16 and 25 plead latent defects that could be detected only by GC-MS or incubation. Under UCC § 2-607(3)(a), the buyer’s duty is to give notice within a reasonable time after it discovers or should have discovered the breach, and courts rarely apply a short inspection clause to bar claims for latent defects. We deny RFAs 12 and 13 because reasonableness is fact-bound and we have a good-faith basis under § 8.1 and in the timeline (consumer complaints in early April; notice around Apr. 22). That gives us reasonable ground under Rule 37(c)(2)(C). **But do not build the case around § 8.1.** Instead, use discovery to press Greenleaf’s own incoming QC practices, since the Answer’s Sixth Affirmative Defense says a 1,4-dioxane-risk component should have been GC-MS screened.

## 4.5 Negligence Claim: Economic Loss Rule (Not Yet Pleaded)

Under Colorado law, the economic loss rule bars tort claims for purely economic losses caused by breach of duties that arise from a contract (*Town of Alma v. AZCO Constr., Inc.*, 10 P.3d 1256 (Colo. 2000); *BRW, Inc. v. Dufficy & Sons, Inc.*, 99 P.3d 66 (Colo. 2004)). Count IV seeks only commercial losses and alleges duties that mirror the MSA. That is also Greenleaf’s main argument for avoiding the cap (Compl. ¶ 106). **Recommendation:**

- Add the economic loss rule as an affirmative defense in the amended Answer, and consider a Rule 12(c) motion.
- Winning on this issue narrows the discovery that matters, removes the argument that the tort claim escapes the cap, and eliminates comparative-fault issues.
- Add the “no duty independent of contract” point already made in Answer Count IV.
