# II. Clawback Deadline

**Governing rule.** Written Clawback Notice is due within 10 business days of the "Discovery Date" (Order § IV.B.1). That is the date counsel **or the e-discovery vendor** first becomes aware of facts indicating inadvertent production. Constructive knowledge is imputed where the producing party or its agents "possessed information sufficient to identify the inadvertent production through the exercise of reasonable diligence." If constructive knowledge came first, it controls (§ IV.B.3). The notice must go by email to AUSA Cooperman, with a hard copy by overnight delivery. It must also identify each document by Bates number, the privilege and its holder, and the factual basis (§ IV.B.2). A conforming log must accompany the notice or follow within 5 business days.

## A. RDGL-00020114–16 — Pre-engagement Ellsworth/Nagarajan emails (Sept. 2020)

The chain opens with your Sept. 14, 2020 business-development follow-up after the DIA meeting. That email is not privileged. Ms. Nagarajan's Sept. 18 reply describes gaps in Ridgeline's PRC process and asks for your views. Your Sept. 22 response gives substantive regulatory observations: legal sign-off for off-label clinical content, FDCA § 502(a), and speaker-program risk. It closes with "happy to discuss further if you'd like to engage us."

**Analysis.** No attorney-client relationship existed. The October 15, 2023 engagement letter expressly states that it formalizes the relationship "effective as of the date hereof" and is not a "retroactive adoption or ratification" of prior informal discussions. A prospective-client theory (RPC 1.18) is available for the Sept. 18 and 22 messages, but it is weak: the exchange was framed as networking and general best practices, and the Government will point to our own engagement letter. Asserting privilege would also focus attention on the lead partner's personal 2020 knowledge of Ridgeline's compliance gaps. That could raise advocate-witness questions (RPC 3.7) and invite a subject-matter fight over what Ridgeline was told and when.

**Recommendation:** Do not claw back. The content is also somewhat helpful: it shows Ridgeline sought compliance advice in 2020. If you prefer to preserve the claim, the fallback entry is: "RDGL-00020114–15; 09/18–22/2020; email; P. Nagarajan, Esq. / C. Ellsworth, Esq.; confidential preliminary consultation by prospective client seeking legal advice regarding promotional review compliance; AC (prospective client); Ridgeline." Note that Ellsworth is personally involved, so this decision should be documented and ideally made by another partner.

## B. RDGL-00020231–34 — Viklund speaker-program memo forwarded to Dr. Deshmukh

The March 15, 2022 memo is classic in-house legal advice. It was requested by Medical Affairs, marked privileged, and instructed recipients not to forward it. On March 17, Dr. Lassiter forwarded it to Dr. Anita Deshmukh, an external rheumatologist and paid Ridgeline speaker (see 20348), asking for her "perspective."

**Analysis.** The Order does not cure this problem. It expressly preserves the Government's right to argue waiver "by voluntary disclosure to third parties" (§ IV.D). Dr. Deshmukh is not within the privilege. She is not an agent assisting counsel and not a functional equivalent of an employee. Our best argument is that only corporate management can waive the corporation's privilege. A non-lawyer VP acting against counsel's express instruction lacked authority to waive, and the forward was not made to further any Ridgeline legal objective. Courts are divided, and a VP-level officer is a harder case than a rank-and-file employee.

**Substantive note.** The memo cuts both ways. It is strong evidence of knowledge: off-label topics, the Hargreaves IIS fibromyalgia data, targeting of pain specialists, and unimplemented Aldersgate recommendations. But it also shows Legal gave correct, protective advice. Lassiter's cover note ("our legal folks seem pretty spun up") is not privileged under any theory, and the Government already has it.

**Recommendation:** Claw back the embedded memo and produce the cover note, which preserves the claim at modest cost. Brief the Government's likely challenge in advance. Also check whether Dr. Deshmukh (or other speakers) forwarded the memo further, and whether any third-party subpoena to her would reach it.

## D. RDGL-00020401–02 — Mullins/Nagarajan Q4 2022 speaker deck (HIGHEST SENSITIVITY)

Mullins asked the General Counsel for legal review of a draft deck themed "emerging data on pain management applications," aimed at rheumatologists and pain specialists. The deck included slides on Nakamura fibromyalgia data and a Ridgeline-sponsored chronic low back pain study. Facially, the exchange is a request for and rendering of legal advice, and it is privileged.

**Crime-fraud risk.** Ms. Nagarajan's reply goes beyond compliance advice. She recommends keeping the off-label fibromyalgia data in the deck behind a disclaimer, removing Ridgeline branding from the off-label slides, and framing KOLs as presenting "independent" research. She then writes: "We should keep this between us — the compliance team would flag this if they saw the original version. Let's make sure the cleaned-up version is what goes to the PRC." The Government will argue that this was advice sought and given to further, or disguise, off-label promotion and to evade internal controls. That is a textbook crime-fraud argument, and the Order preserves it (§ IV.D). The threshold for in camera review is low (United States v. Zolin, 491 U.S. 554 (1989)). A contested ruling could produce a judicial finding that extends to related GC communications.

**Recommendation:** Include the document in the clawback notice. Failing to do so is a waiver decision that belongs to the client, and silence would be treated as waiver under § IV.B.5. However, (i) the decision to assert or withdraw must be made by an unconflicted client representative, not Ms. Nagarajan (§ IV.A); (ii) we should assess, before any meet-and-confer, whether defending this document is worth the risk of a crime-fraud finding; and (iii) the Audit Committee's counsel will almost certainly treat this document as central to its investigation.

## F. RDGL-00020512–15 — Nagarajan/Metcalf "coordination" emails (Nov. 2, 2023)

Correa's counsel (Metcalf) and Ridgeline's GC exchanged four emails the day after the CID. Metcalf proposed that the two sides "present a consistent narrative to the government." Nagarajan shared the CID, Ridgeline's preliminary defense theory, and Viklund's preliminary review findings, and proposed advance notice of DOJ interview requests "so we can ensure consistency in how both sides are presenting the facts."

**Analysis.** In the Third Circuit, common interest can apply without a written agreement where counsel share communications in furtherance of a substantially similar legal interest (In re Teleglobe, 493 F.3d 345). The claim is colorable because both sides face the same investigation and the emails are between counsel. The weaknesses are serious, though: (i) no common-interest agreement existed, and H&C was not yet involved; (ii) the interests were already diverging, since Correa's position is that she relied on Legal/MLR, and Viklund's own Dec. 2023 deck calls her a cooperation risk whose interests "may diverge"; (iii) the Government will characterize "consistent narrative" and interview coordination as witness-influence evidence and invoke crime-fraud; and (iv) Metcalf's opening email (20515) came before any shared understanding. Ms. Correa is a co-holder, and her counsel may take a different position, possibly one adverse to Ridgeline.

**Recommendation:** Claw back the full chain on a common-interest basis to preserve it, after a call with Metcalf to confirm Kendrick Sable's position (Correa may not have consented to the Order under § VI.B). Separately, advise the client that any future coordination with individual counsel should go through H&C under a written common-interest agreement, and that "consistent narrative" communications must stop.

## D. Defensibility of the clawback (FRE 502(b) / reasonable diligence)

The facts are favorable. Our reviewers coded all 47 documents correctly. The error was a silent vendor script defect (an OR/AND logic error in v4.2.1). The Order-contemplated QC process caught it within five business days, and the vendor notified us within 27 minutes. The Order treats vendor retention as a reasonable step (§ V.D) but says technology does not excuse our own diligence. Two weaknesses: v4.2.1 was deployed without mixed-privilege testing, and there was no pre-production check comparing final coding against reviewer coding. We should get a sworn declaration from Lisa Choi (offered in § 7.2 of the report) and keep the NorthBridge report itself privileged. It is labeled work product and should not be attached to the notice. We should also remind the Government in the notice of § IV.C.2, which bars use of any substance already reviewed, and ask it to identify anyone who has already reviewed the documents.

## E. Factual inconsistencies to resolve (Aldersgate)

The documents give inconsistent accounts of Aldersgate Consulting Group: retained 2020 with an Oct. 2021 final report whose recommendations were "not yet fully implemented" as of March 2022 (B); a speaker-program review in 2021 (G, slide 5); advisory support in 2022, "substantially implemented" (G, slide 4); an assessment in Q1 2022 (J § 3.7); and "coordinating" on PRC streamlining in Aug. 2022 (C). We should establish the Aldersgate timeline and implementation status now. The Government will use these inconsistencies, and Ridgeline's narrative on compliance remediation depends on getting it right.
