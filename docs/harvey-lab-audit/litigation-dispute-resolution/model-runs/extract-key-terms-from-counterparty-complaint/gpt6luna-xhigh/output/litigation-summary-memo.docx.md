# 3. Contract, forum, and choice-of-law analysis

## A. Renewal and the Year 2 shortfall

The agreement excerpt is unusually explicit: Section 4.2 requires notice at least 180 days before expiration, requires the notice to be delivered by the terminating party’s CEO or General Counsel, and declares a notice that fails the signatory or timing requirements “void and of no force or effect.” Hargrove’s July letter does not meet the specified signatory condition. The September letter has the correct signer but was delivered 169 days before expiration. Its “supersedes and replaces” language further weakens any argument that it relates back to July. Apex’s August 3 warning, followed by Greenfield’s silence until after the deadline, is unfavorable evidence on notice and equitable defenses.

Date calculation correction: the calendar-date difference between September 1, 2023 and February 28, 2024 is 180 days; August 31 is 181 days before February 28. The complaint is therefore one day early in identifying the cutoff. September 12 remains 11 days late, so the calculation error does not cure the notice. Confirm the agreement’s notice-delivery mechanics, actual delivery receipts, and any controlling interpretation of “prior to” before finalizing the position.

The Year 2 shortfall is not a persuasive present defense to the renewal. Section 5.3 required written notice and a 60-day cure opportunity before termination rights could be exercised; Yuen confirms none was sent. The record also reflects acceptance of later performance and cumulative purchases above the five-year total. The omission is likely a substantial waiver/notice problem. The shortfall may be relevant to breach, but cannot safely be treated as automatically terminating the agreement or excusing Greenfield’s direct sales. COVID-related defenses need not be resolved to assess that procedural failure.

## B. Direct sales and contract damages

Even if the September notice had been timely, all four representative invoices fall before February 28, 2024, while the Initial Term was still operative. Thus the direct sales can support a contract breach independent of renewal. The full agreement is essential: confirm whether exclusivity is absolute, whether Greenfield reserved direct-account or house-account rights, what sales are covered, how the alleged 18% commission is defined, and whether Sections 12 or 5 impose other notice/cure conditions. The excerpts do not include these provisions or any complete termination/notice article.

The two-year renewal is also plausible under the text. But Apex’s claimed \$7.084m in future lost profits assumes \$16.1m annual sales at a 22% gross margin for both renewal years. Section 5.2 provides a renewal minimum: absent a mutual agreement 60 days before the renewal, the Year 5 \$14m figure adjusts by CPI-U. It does not establish \$16.1m annual sales or a 22% margin. Obtain the parties’ renewal discussions, applicable CPI calculations, actual 2024–2025 orders, comparable sales history, costs/margins, and mitigation evidence. Distinguish contractual minimum purchases from sales forecast and profit evidence.

## C. Choice of law, venue, and removal

- **Venue and personal jurisdiction:** the agreement’s broad forum clause selects state or federal courts in Mecklenburg County and consents to jurisdiction; Greenfield’s principal place of business is in Charlotte. The current state forum is consistent with the clause.

- **Contract:** Section 16.1 selects Georgia substantive law for interpretation, validity, performance, and enforcement. North Carolina procedure governs in this state court, including Rule 65 and the service/pleading rules.

- **Tort and trade-secret choice of law:** the distribution agreement’s Georgia clause is strong for contract issues, but does not conclusively resolve every independent tort choice-of-law question. The tortious-interference claim concerns customer relationships in several states; the North Carolina court may need to apply North Carolina conflicts rules. DTSA is federal law. The GTSA claim’s governing-law basis should be contested/briefed if material rather than assumed from the contract clause.

- **Employee covenants:** both Exhibit F agreements separately select Georgia law; each employee is identified as a Georgia resident when signing. Georgia’s Restrictive Covenants Act generally permits covenants for qualifying employees and can presume a restriction of two years or less reasonable in duration, but employee status, scope, geographic fit, consideration, and the full agreements remain material. The noncompete’s prohibition on work for a competitor “in any capacity” across eight states is broad. A North Carolina forum may be asked to apply North Carolina public policy / its restrictive-covenant standards despite the selection clause; outcome is not certain.

Greenfield is not a party to the employment agreements. Their choice-of-law provisions govern Apex–employee covenant issues, not automatically Greenfield’s liability. Apex may pursue a properly supported interference theory against Greenfield, but it must prove an enforceable covenant, knowledge, intentional inducement, breach, causation, and improper conduct. The former employees are not named defendants in this case. Do not assume the injunction can simply enforce their personal covenants against Greenfield.

# 5. Damages and exposure framework

The same four transactions and customer relationships underlie (i) commission/lost-profit contract damages, (ii) the \$6.2m customer-relationship valuation, (iii) the \$1.491m Greenfield profit estimate, and potentially (iv) the trade-secret valuation. Courts generally prevent duplicative compensation for one injury; statutory unjust-enrichment and exemplary remedies are subject to separate statutory proof and limits.
