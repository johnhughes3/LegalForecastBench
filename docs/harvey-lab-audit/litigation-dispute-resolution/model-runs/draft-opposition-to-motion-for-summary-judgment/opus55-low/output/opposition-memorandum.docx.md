# I. INTRODUCTION

Defendant Ridgeline National Bank (the “Bank”) asks the Court to hold, as a matter of law, that nothing about the termination of Elena Vasquez could be retaliatory. The record it asks the Court to ignore shows the following sequence:

> **February 10, 2023:** Regional President Marcus Caldwell rates Ms. Vasquez “Outstanding,” calls her portfolio management “exemplary,” and recommends her for the Bank’s executive-level emerging leaders program. This was her eleventh straight positive review.
>
> **April 3, 2023:** Ms. Vasquez gives the Chief Compliance Officer a seven-page memorandum documenting \$18.7 million in wire activity through Orion Gateway Holdings LLC that bears the marks of money laundering. She recommends that the Bank file Suspicious Activity Reports (“SARs”).
>
> **May 2, 2023:** General Counsel Thomas Hargrove declares the matter “reviewed and resolved.” He produces no written analysis, and no SARs are filed. When the Bank’s own AML analyst later concludes that immediate SAR filing is required, he is told to “stand down.”
>
> **June 15, 2023:** Ms. Vasquez reports the Orion Gateway activity to FinCEN.
>
> **July 28, 2023:** The OCC demands Orion Gateway documents based on “information received from a confidential source.”
>
> **August 1, 2023:** Hargrove writes to Caldwell that the OCC inquiry “almost certainly stems from an internal leak,” that “Elena Vasquez is the obvious candidate,” and that “\[w\]e need to identify the source and contain this before it escalates further.” Caldwell replies: “Understood. Let me handle this.”
>
> **August 14, 2023:** Thirteen days later, Caldwell issues Ms. Vasquez the first Performance Improvement Plan (“PIP”) of her 11½-year career. It lasts 32 days, although Bank policy sets a 60-day minimum.
>
> **September 22, 2023:** Ms. Vasquez is fired “for cause,” and Hargrove is copied on the letter. Twenty-two days later, the Bank fires the AML analyst who had recommended SAR filing on Orion Gateway. It calls that a “restructuring” and fills his job three weeks later.

The PIP’s stated grounds do not hold up against the Bank’s own documents. The PIP says Ms. Vasquez missed a “\$15 million” Q2 origination target. The 2023 Business Plan that Caldwell signed sets that target at \$10 million, and Ms. Vasquez originated \$11.3 million. The PIP calls three loan files deficient. The Bank’s Internal Audit Department rated all three “Satisfactory” three months earlier, and Caldwell cannot name a single missing document. The PIP cites two client complaints. One client swears under penalty of perjury that he never complained. The other “complainant” is the principal of Orion Gateway, the entity Ms. Vasquez reported for suspected money laundering, which was not even her client.

This is not a close case for summary judgment. The Bank’s three arguments each fail. First, the Bank’s General Counsel identified Ms. Vasquez in writing as the “obvious candidate” behind the regulatory inquiry, and he gave that message to the decision-maker, who promised to “handle this.” A reasonable jury could find that the Bank knew, or at a minimum believed, that she was the whistleblower. Second, the evidence of pretext is overwhelming. Third, the Bank’s SOX argument rests on a quotation of 18 U.S.C. § 1514A(a) that uses an ellipsis to remove the very words that cover it: the statute reaches “any subsidiary or affiliate whose financial information is included in the consolidated financial statements” of a public company. Fourth, the preemption argument ignores SOX’s express savings clause and Supreme Court precedent holding that federal whistleblower remedies do not displace state-law claims. The Motion should be denied in its entirety.

# IV. LEGAL STANDARD

Summary judgment is proper only if “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). “The evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in \[her\] favor.” *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). “Credibility determinations, the weighing of the evidence, and the drawing of legitimate inferences from the facts are jury functions, not those of a judge.” *Id.* A court that credits the movant’s evidence over the non-movant’s, or fails to draw reasonable inferences in the non-movant’s favor, commits reversible error. *Tolan v. Cotton*, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57, 660 (2014) (per curiam).

These principles carry special weight in retaliation cases, where the ultimate question is the employer’s motive. A plaintiff’s prima facie case, “combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer’s asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated.” *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000). In deciding the motion, the court “must disregard all evidence favorable to the moving party that the jury is not required to believe.” *Id.* at 151. The testimony of Caldwell, an interested witness who has already revised his sworn account once, is exactly that kind of evidence.

# V. ARGUMENT

## A. A Reasonable Jury Could Find That the Bank Knew, or Believed, That Ms. Vasquez Was the Whistleblower (Count I).

The Bank’s lead argument is that it could not have retaliated against Ms. Vasquez for her FinCEN report because FinCEN keeps tipsters confidential and she told no one. Br. at 8–13. That argument answers a question no one is asking. The issue is not whether FinCEN disclosed her identity. The issue is whether the people who decided her fate knew or believed she was the source of the regulatory report and acted on that knowledge or belief. The Bank’s own documents answer that question.

### 1. The Bank’s General Counsel identified Ms. Vasquez in writing as the source of the regulatory report and gave that conclusion to the decision-maker.

On August 1, 2023, four days after the OCC letter arrived, the Bank’s General Counsel told the Regional President that the OCC inquiry “almost certainly stems from an internal leak.” He wrote that “Elena Vasquez is the obvious candidate,” that “the timing and substance are too close to be coincidental,” and that “\[w\]e need to identify the source and contain this before it escalates further.” PSAF ¶ 22. He told the Chief Compliance Officer the same thing: Ms. Vasquez “was the likely source of whatever report had been made to the regulators.” No other name was ever mentioned. PSAF ¶ 24. The decision-maker read the email and replied, “Understood. Let me handle this.” PSAF ¶ 23. He admits that, from that day on, he knew the Bank’s leadership believed Ms. Vasquez had supplied the information to the regulator. PSAF ¶ 25.

This is direct evidence of knowledge. At a minimum, it is strong circumstantial evidence from which a jury could find knowledge. The statements of the Bank’s General Counsel and Regional President about matters within the scope of their employment are admissions of a party-opponent. Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(2)(D). The Bank calls Hargrove’s email “conjecture” (Br. at 10–11), but that is an argument about the weight and meaning of evidence, and those questions are for the jury. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255. Hargrove did not guess. He wrote that he had “no doubt,” explained why the “timing and substance” pointed to Ms. Vasquez, and called for action to “contain” her. And he was right: Ms. Vasquez had in fact reported to FinCEN six weeks before the OCC letter arrived. PSAF ¶ 20. An employer that correctly identifies the whistleblower and then acts against her cannot escape liability because no one handed it a signed confession.

The anti-retaliation inquiry concerns the employer’s state of mind. The Supreme Court has held, in the analogous First Amendment setting, that when an employer takes adverse action because it believes an employee engaged in protected activity, “\[t\]he government’s reason for demoting \[the employee\] is what counts,” even if the employer is mistaken about the facts. *Heffernan v. City of Paterson*, 578 U.S. 266, 272–73 (2016). There was no mistake here. The Bank believed Ms. Vasquez was the source, and she was. If an employer’s mistaken belief is enough to establish retaliatory motive, a belief that happens to be correct is certainly enough.

### 2. The Bank’s attempt to separate the internal memorandum from the external report fails on its own terms.

The Bank argues that Hargrove’s email reflects knowledge only of the April internal memorandum, not the FinCEN report. Br. at 10–11; SUMF ¶ 41. The email says otherwise. Its subject is the OCC inquiry, a federal regulator’s response to “information received from a confidential source.” Hargrove wrote that the OCC inquiry “stems from an internal leak,” meaning that someone inside the Bank had given information to someone outside it, and he named Ms. Vasquez as that person. PSAF ¶ 22. The April memorandum was the evidence for his conclusion. The external report was the conclusion. The Bank’s argument that its General Counsel only “knew” of an internal memo he was using to identify an external reporter mischaracterizes the document. At a minimum, it offers a competing reading of the email that a jury is entitled to reject.

The Bank also relies on Caldwell’s testimony that he had no “knowledge or belief that she had filed a report with FinCEN.” Br. at 11 (quoting Caldwell Dep. 72:14–73:2). The question was carefully limited to FinCEN by name. Caldwell has admitted that he knew the Bank believed Ms. Vasquez had reported to a federal regulator. PSAF ¶ 25. Whether the regulator was FinCEN or the OCC, and whether the report went directly or through a referral, makes no difference to retaliatory motive. What matters is that the decision-maker believed she had taken the Bank’s Orion Gateway problem outside the Bank. The jury also need not credit Caldwell’s denials. He first swore that he “wasn’t aware of Ms. Vasquez’s compliance report at the time I drafted the PIP,” then conceded that he learned of it from Hargrove’s email thirteen days before the PIP. PSAF ¶ 26. A jury that disbelieves an interested witness on one point may disbelieve him on others. *See Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 147, 151.

### 3. Even apart from Caldwell’s own knowledge, Hargrove’s animus is attributable to the Bank.

Caldwell’s “Let me handle this” reply, his conversation with Hargrove about the termination, Hargrove’s “generally supportive” position, and the copy of the termination letter sent to Hargrove would each allow a jury to find that Hargrove’s intent to “identify the source and contain this” helped produce the PIP and the termination. PSAF ¶¶ 22–23, 47. Under settled agency principles, an employer is liable when a biased official “performs an act motivated by \[retaliatory\] animus that is intended by the supervisor to cause an adverse employment action,” and that act is a proximate cause of the adverse action, even if another official formally makes the decision. *Staub v. Proctor Hosp.*, 562 U.S. 411, 419–22 (2011). The Bank invoked privilege to keep Caldwell from saying whether Ms. Vasquez’s compliance report came up in his termination discussions with Hargrove. PSAF ¶ 47. Having done so, the Bank cannot now ask the Court to find as a matter of law that those discussions had nothing to do with it.

### 4. The remaining elements are not seriously contested and are satisfied.

The Bank does not dispute that the FinCEN report is protected activity or that termination is an adverse action. Causation is readily inferred. The PIP came 13 days after Hargrove named Ms. Vasquez to Caldwell, and the termination came 52 days after that email and 99 days after the FinCEN report. PSAF ¶¶ 20–23. The Tenth Circuit has recognized that an adverse action “very closely” following protected activity may by itself support an inference of causation. It has held that a gap of about six weeks suffices, while longer gaps require additional evidence. *Anderson v. Coors Brewing Co.*, 181 F.3d 1171, 1179 (10th Cir. 1999). Here the timing is measured in days from the moment the Bank identified Ms. Vasquez as the source, and it is backed by the additional evidence described in Part V.B below. *See Metzler v. Fed. Home Loan Bank of Topeka*, 464 F.3d 1164, 1171–72 (10th Cir. 2006) (temporal proximity combined with other evidence of retaliatory motive supports causation).[^1]

## B. The Record Is Replete with Evidence of Pretext (Count I).

Even under *McDonnell Douglas*, a plaintiff defeats summary judgment by showing “such weaknesses, implausibilities, inconsistencies, incoherencies, or contradictions in the employer’s proffered legitimate reasons for its action that a reasonable factfinder could rationally find them unworthy of credence.” *Argo v. Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Kan., Inc.*, 452 F.3d 1193, 1203 (10th Cir. 2006) (cited at Br. 17). Pretext may be shown with evidence that the stated reason was false, that the employer acted contrary to its own written policy, or that similarly situated employees were treated differently. *Kendrick v. Penske Transp. Servs., Inc.*, 220 F.3d 1220, 1230 (10th Cir. 2000). Shifting or inconsistent explanations are also evidence of pretext. *Jaramillo v. Colo. Judicial Dep’t*, 427 F.3d 1303, 1308–10 (10th Cir. 2005). The record contains every one of these forms of evidence.

### 1. Each of the three stated grounds for the PIP is contradicted by the Bank’s own records.

**1. The origination “shortfall” depends on a \$15 million target that appears nowhere but the PIP.** The Bank’s brief repeats three times that Ms. Vasquez missed a \$15 million Q2 target. Br. at 3, 15–16. The only written target is in the Business Plan that Caldwell signed on January 20, 2023: \$10,000,000. PSAF ¶ 28. Ms. Vasquez originated \$11.3 million and exceeded that target by 13%. PSAF ¶ 31. Asked when the target rose to \$15 million, Caldwell said he “believe\[d\]” there was a conversation “sometime in the spring” with someone he thought was Ms. Vasquez but was “not entirely certain.” He conceded that no email, memorandum, or amendment reflects it. PSAF ¶ 30. Bank policy forbids exactly this: “\[u\]nder no circumstances should a PIP introduce performance standards or targets that were not previously communicated to the employee in writing.” PSAF ¶ 43. A jury could readily find that the target was invented after the fact so that a quarter in which she beat her target could be recast as a failure. That finding alone would be enough to defeat summary judgment. *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 147–48.

**2. The “documentation deficiencies” were rated “Satisfactory” by Internal Audit.** Three months before the PIP, the Bank’s Internal Audit Department examined the same three files the PIP cites (Thornburg, Westlake, and Meridian) and rated each “Satisfactory,” with “\[n\]o corrective action recommended.” It described Meridian as “thorough and well-maintained.” It also noted that no audit had ever found a corrective-action item in any file Ms. Vasquez managed. PSAF ¶ 32. Caldwell received that report. Against it, the Bank offers only Caldwell’s undocumented “own review.” He cannot date it, made no notes of it, never mentioned it to Ms. Vasquez or to Internal Audit, and cannot identify one specific missing document from any of the three files. PSAF ¶ 33. The Bank has described the supposed deficiencies in four different ways in the PIP, the SUMF, its brief, and Caldwell’s testimony. PSAF ¶ 33. The PIP even lists account numbers and borrower names that do not match the Bank’s audit records for those relationships. PSAF ¶ 34. An explanation that changes each time it is given, and that the employer’s own auditors contradict, is classic evidence of pretext. *Jaramillo*, 427 F.3d at 1308–10.

**3. One “client complaint” was never made, and the other came from the target of Ms. Vasquez’s money-laundering report.** Jeffrey Thornburg swears that he never complained, that no one from the Bank ever asked him about any complaint, and that Ms. Vasquez was “consistently highly responsive.” PSAF ¶ 37. Caldwell cannot say who told him otherwise and cannot produce a record of it. PSAF ¶ 38. The other complaint came from Raymond Sellars, the principal of Orion Gateway, the very entity Ms. Vasquez had reported for suspected money laundering. Orion Gateway was not her client, so she had no reason to be returning its calls. PSAF ¶¶ 35–36. A jury could reasonably conclude that the Bank built its case against a whistleblower in part on a complaint from the subject of her report. That fact is itself evidence of retaliatory motive, and the Bank’s reliance on it without investigation is evidence of pretext.

### 2. The PIP was an abrupt reversal of an eleven-year record.

Six months before the PIP, Caldwell called Ms. Vasquez “one of our highest-performing SVPs,” praised the exact competencies the PIP later faulted (documentation, responsiveness, and origination), and recommended her for executive-level advancement. PSAF ¶¶ 2–4. As of February 10, 2023, he had no concerns. PSAF ¶ 5. Asked what changed, he could only say “\[t\]he ones documented in the PIP,” arising “sometime in the summer of 2023.” PSAF ¶ 27; Ex. 1 at 24–25. The Chief Compliance Officer, asked the same question, answered: “The Orion Gateway matter.” Ex. 2 at 169–178. A sudden decline in a long-tenured employee’s evaluations immediately after protected activity is well recognized as circumstantial evidence of pretext, and a jury is entitled to weigh it here.

The Bank’s claim that Caldwell began identifying deficiencies “in June 2023—well before the OCC letter arrived” (Br. at 18) is not supported by the testimony Plaintiff elicited. Caldwell testified only that he “believe\[d\]” he began “considering” a PIP in “July.” He admitted that he had no emails, calendar entries, or notes of any kind before August 1, and that the first documented step toward the PIP came after Hargrove’s email. PSAF ¶ 27. On summary judgment, the Court must credit the absence of any documentary support over the self-serving recollection of an interested witness. *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 151.

### 3. The Bank disregarded its own PIP policy at nearly every step.

An employer’s departure from its written procedures is evidence of pretext. *Kendrick*, 220 F.3d at 1230. The Bank quotes the Handbook’s statement that PIPs “shall *generally* span” 60 to 90 days and argues that “generally” gave Caldwell discretion. Br. at 18–19. The Bank omits the next clause of the same section: “*in no event* should a PIP period be less than 60 calendar days unless extraordinary circumstances exist and are documented in writing with the approval of the HR Director.” PSAF ¶ 41 (emphasis added). No such written justification has been produced. Ms. Vasquez’s PIP lasted 32 days, the shortest Caldwell ever issued. PSAF ¶¶ 41–42. The Bank also skipped the required prerequisites: no prior coaching or counseling, no documented evidentiary basis, and a target that had never been communicated in writing. PSAF ¶¶ 43–44. It then set requirements designed to guarantee failure. She was required to close \$5 million in executed commitments in 32 days, and any client complaint, however baseless, would “be considered a failure.” PSAF ¶ 39. The Handbook describes PIPs as “remedial” tools that are “not . . . a precursor to termination.” PSAF ¶ 43. A jury could find that this PIP was designed to lead to termination.

### 4. Similarly situated SVPs with worse records were not disciplined.

The Bank argues that Ms. Vasquez “has not identified” comparators. Br. at 19–20. She has. Bradley Whitfield and Kenneth Lomax held the same title, reported to the same supervisor, worked in the same region, and were evaluated in the same time period under the same rating system. Both were rated “Needs Improvement” for 2022, two tiers below Ms. Vasquez’s “Outstanding.” Neither received a PIP or even a written warning. PSAF ¶ 45. The Q1 2023 audit rated all of their files “Satisfactory,” just like hers. Ex. 6 § 3. Whitfield onboarded Orion Gateway, the client at the center of the suspected layering, and received a verbal conversation. PSAF ¶ 46. The Bank’s only response is that “\[e\]ach employee’s situation is evaluated individually.” Ex. 1 at 104. That is not a legitimate distinction. It is a restatement of the disparity. Whether employees are similarly situated is ordinarily a question of fact. The Bank’s generic list of ways employees “inevitably” differ (Br. at 20) does not identify any actual difference.

### 5. Additional evidence of retaliatory motive.

The Bank argues that Ms. Vasquez’s case “rests entirely on temporal proximity and speculation.” Br. at 21. The record shows much more:

> The General Counsel’s written instruction to “identify the source and contain this,” naming Ms. Vasquez, and the decision-maker’s reply, “Let me handle this.” PSAF ¶¶ 22–23.
>
> The Bank’s handling of the Orion Gateway matter: a thirteen-day, undocumented “review,” closed by a General Counsel with no AML training, in violation of the Bank’s written procedures, followed by an order silencing its own analyst. This gives the Bank a clear motive to suppress anyone who took the matter to regulators. PSAF ¶¶ 10–19.
>
> The termination of Carlos Medina, the only other employee who documented a SAR recommendation on Orion Gateway, 22 days after Ms. Vasquez. The stated “restructuring” affected no one else, was imposed over the Chief Compliance Officer’s head, and was followed three weeks later by hiring a replacement into a substantially similar role. PSAF ¶¶ 48–49. Evidence of the Bank’s treatment of another Orion Gateway reporter is admissible to show motive and intent. Fed. R. Evid. 404(b)(2).
>
> Caldwell’s recanted testimony about when he learned of Ms. Vasquez’s report. PSAF ¶ 26.
>
> The characterization of the termination as “for cause,” which stripped Ms. Vasquez of her bonus. Ex. 10.

Taken together, and viewed in the light most favorable to Ms. Vasquez, this evidence would allow a reasonable jury to conclude that the Bank’s performance rationale is “unworthy of credence” and that the real reason for her termination was retaliation. *Argo*, 452 F.3d at 1203. Summary judgment on Count I should be denied.

## C. The Bank Is a Covered Employer Under Sarbanes-Oxley (Count II).

### 1. Section 1514A expressly covers subsidiaries whose financial information is consolidated with a public parent.

The Bank’s SOX argument depends on an ellipsis. The Bank quotes § 1514A(a) as covering a “company with a class of securities registered under section 12 . . . or that is required to file reports under section 15(d) . . . .” Br. at 22. It then argues that “\[i\]f Congress had intended” to reach subsidiaries, “the statutory text would reference subsidiaries and affiliates.” Br. at 23. The statutory text does exactly that. Since the 2010 Dodd-Frank amendments, the words omitted by the Bank’s first ellipsis provide:

> No company with a class of securities registered under section 12 of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (15 U.S.C. 78l), or that is required to file reports under section 15(d) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (15 U.S.C. 78o(d)) **including any subsidiary or affiliate whose financial information is included in the consolidated financial statements of such company** . . . may discharge . . . an employee . . . .

18 U.S.C. § 1514A(a) (emphasis added); *see* Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, Pub. L. No. 111-203, § 929A, 124 Stat. 1376, 1852 (2010). Congress added that language specifically to close the gap the Bank now asks the Court to reopen.

The Bank admits that it is a wholly-owned subsidiary of RDFG, whose common stock trades on NASDAQ. SUMF ¶ 2; Br. at 6, 23. A wholly-owned bank subsidiary’s financial information is included in its holding company’s consolidated financial statements. PSAF ¶ 50. The Bank does not contend otherwise. Its Motion never addresses the subsidiary clause at all. Because the Bank falls within the statute’s plain terms, its arguments about Ms. Vasquez’s W-2s and offer letter (Br. at 22–23) are beside the point. Ms. Vasquez was an “employee” of a covered “subsidiary.” So too is the Bank’s suggestion that she should have sued RDFG instead. Br. at 24. Congress made the subsidiary itself liable.

Even before Dodd-Frank, the Supreme Court rejected a similarly narrow reading of § 1514A, holding that the statute protects employees of a public company’s private contractors. *Lawson v. FMR LLC*, 571 U.S. 429, 433 (2014). The Bank’s argument that coverage of subsidiaries would “transform SOX . . . into a general-purpose employment protection statute” (Br. at 23) is a policy objection to legislation Congress enacted, and it cannot override the statute’s text.

### 2. The Bank raises no other ground for summary judgment on Count II.

The Bank challenges Count II solely on coverage. Br. at 22–24. It does not argue that Ms. Vasquez’s reports were not protected activity under § 1514A, and it cannot raise new grounds for the first time in reply. Plaintiff notes that SOX claims are governed by the burden-shifting framework of 49 U.S.C. § 42121(b), not *McDonnell Douglas*. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 1514A(b)(2)(C). An employee need show only that protected activity was a “contributing factor” in the adverse action. The burden then shifts to the employer to prove “by clear and convincing evidence” that it would have taken the same action anyway. *Lockheed Martin Corp. v. Admin. Review Bd.*, 717 F.3d 1121, 1129 (10th Cir. 2013). The employee need not prove retaliatory intent. *Murray v. UBS Sec., LLC*, 601 U.S. 23 (2024). On this record, the Bank could not come close to meeting a clear-and-convincing burden as a matter of law. Summary judgment on Count II should be denied.

## D. Ms. Vasquez’s Colorado Public-Policy Claim Is Not Preempted (Count III).

### 1. The Bank cannot overcome the presumption against preemption.

In any preemption analysis, courts “start with the assumption that the historic police powers of the States were not to be superseded by the Federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress.” *Wyeth v. Levine*, 555 U.S. 555, 565 (2009) (quoting *Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr*, 518 U.S. 470, 485 (1996)). Protecting employees from wrongful discharge is a traditional area of state regulation. Colorado has long recognized a tort claim for discharge in violation of public policy. *Martin Marietta Corp. v. Lorenz*, 823 P.2d 100, 109 (Colo. 1992). The Bank identifies no statutory text showing a “clear and manifest” intent to displace that tort.

### 2. Congress expressly preserved state-law remedies for whistleblowers.

The Bank argues that the federal whistleblower schemes “occupy the field.” Br. at 25–26. Congress said the opposite. The very SOX provision the Bank invokes states: “Nothing in this section shall be deemed to diminish the rights, privileges, or remedies of any employee under any Federal or State law, or under any collective bargaining agreement.” 18 U.S.C. § 1514A(d). An express savings clause for state-law remedies is incompatible with any claimed intent to occupy the field. The Bank’s brief does not mention this provision.

The Supreme Court rejected a nearly identical field-preemption argument in *English v. General Electric Co.*, 496 U.S. 72 (1990). There, the Court held that the federal whistleblower provision of the Energy Reorganization Act, part of a detailed federal regulatory scheme in an area of intense federal interest, did not preempt a whistleblower’s state-law tort claim arising from retaliation for reporting safety violations. *Id.* at 83–90. If the federal government’s pervasive regulation of nuclear safety did not preempt a state tort remedy for retaliation against a nuclear whistleblower, the BSA’s anti-retaliation provisions do not preempt Colorado’s public-policy tort. The Bank’s appeals to national-bank uniformity (Br. at 26) do not change the analysis. Colorado’s generally applicable tort does not regulate how a national bank conducts its banking business. It simply forbids firing employees for reporting suspected money laundering. That is a result federal law itself encourages. *Cf. Barnett Bank of Marion Cnty., N.A. v. Nelson*, 517 U.S. 25, 33 (1996) (state law is preempted where it “prevent\[s\] or significantly interfere\[s\] with the national bank’s exercise of its powers”).

### 3. There is no conflict between Colorado law and federal law.

Conflict preemption requires either that compliance with both state and federal law be impossible or that state law stand as an obstacle to the accomplishment of Congress’s objectives. *English*, 496 U.S. at 79. Neither applies. The Bank can easily comply with both regimes by not retaliating against whistleblowers. A state remedy that deters retaliation against employees who report money laundering advances the BSA’s goals. The Bank’s objection to the potential availability of punitive damages (Br. at 26–27) is foreclosed by *Silkwood v. Kerr-McGee Corp.*, 464 U.S. 238, 256–58 (1984), which held that state-law punitive damages were not preempted despite comprehensive federal regulation of the field. It is also foreclosed by *English*, which rejected the argument that the availability of greater state-law remedies creates an obstacle conflict. 496 U.S. at 86–90.

### 4. The “adequate statutory remedy” argument is a state-law point that fails on this record.

Finally, the Bank argues that Colorado courts may decline to recognize the public-policy tort where a statute already provides an adequate remedy, citing *Crawford Rehabilitation Services, Inc. v. Weissman*, 938 P.2d 540 (Colo. 1997). Br. at 27. That is not a preemption argument, and by the Bank’s own description *Crawford* holds only that a statutory remedy “may” bear on the tort. *Id.* The argument fails here for three reasons.

First, the Bank cannot argue both that the federal remedies are adequate and that they are unavailable. In the same brief, it contends that SOX does not apply to it at all and that the BSA claim fails for lack of knowledge. Br. at 8–13, 22–24. If the Bank were correct on those points, Ms. Vasquez would have no federal remedy, and the premise of the “adequate remedy” argument would disappear. Second, the Federal Rules expressly permit alternative and inconsistent claims. Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(d)(2)–(3). Any question of election or duplicative recovery can be addressed at judgment and provides no basis for summary judgment now. Third, the public policy supporting Count III is not limited to the federal whistleblower statutes. It also includes Colorado’s own criminal prohibition on money laundering, C.R.S. § 18-5-309, and the broader policy against requiring employees to be complicit in, or silent about, suspected financial crime. \[*Drafting note: conform to the public-policy sources pleaded in the Complaint, ¶¶ 78–112.*\] The Bank makes no argument that Ms. Vasquez cannot satisfy the elements of the *Martin Marietta* tort. Summary judgment on Count III should be denied.

# VI. CONCLUSION

The Bank’s General Counsel named Ms. Vasquez as the source of a federal regulatory inquiry and called for her to be “contain\[ed\].” The decision-maker replied, “Let me handle this,” and thirteen days later issued a PIP built on an invented target, loan files the Bank’s auditors had approved, and a complaint the client denies ever making. The Bank then fired the only other employee who had recommended SAR filing on Orion Gateway. A jury should decide why Ms. Vasquez was fired. Section 1514A covers the Bank by its express terms, and Congress preserved the state-law remedies the Bank says are preempted. Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court deny Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment in its entirety.
