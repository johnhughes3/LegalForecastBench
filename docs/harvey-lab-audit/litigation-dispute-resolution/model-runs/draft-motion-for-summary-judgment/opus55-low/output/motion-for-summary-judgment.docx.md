**BRIEF IN SUPPORT OF PLAINTIFF RIDGELINE MANUFACTURING CORP.’S MOTION FOR PARTIAL SUMMARY JUDGMENT**

# I. INTRODUCTION

Ridgeline hired Apex to implement an enterprise resource planning (“ERP”) system across its three aerospace and automotive component plants. Two capabilities mattered most: integrating the new system with Ridgeline’s Siemens Teamcenter engineering platform, and configuring an AS9100D aerospace quality module that Ridgeline’s certification and customers depend on. Apex won the \$2.85 million engagement by telling Ridgeline that it had “deep experience” with Teamcenter integration and had “successfully completed this integration for multiple manufacturing clients.” It named two such clients and described the results of each. It also claimed “6 Certified AS9100D Implementation Specialists on Staff.” CSMF ¶¶ 6–7, 11, 15–16.

None of that was true, and Apex’s witnesses have said so under oath. Apex’s Vice President of Sales testified that Apex “never actually performed a Teamcenter integration for anyone before Ridgeline.” Its CEO testified that Apex had one AS9100D-certified employee, not six, and called the claim “aspirational.” Its project manager testified that he warned the sales lead before the Proposal went out, and was told “we’ll figure it out.” Ten weeks into the project he wrote internally: “We have zero experience with Teamcenter. I’ve been Googling the API docs for two weeks. . . . Don’t raise this with the client yet.” CSMF ¶¶ 18–27, 54.

The project unfolded accordingly. Phase 1 was eleven weeks late, and Apex’s project manager admits “\[t\]he delays were on our side.” Phase 2 was never completed. Apex’s Teamcenter design used what Ridgeline’s unrebutted expert describes as a deprecated, unsupported architecture, and seven of twelve critical AS9100D traceability requirements did not work. Apex then demanded \$680,000, and later \$1.2 million, in additional fees to finish work it had already contracted to deliver for a fixed price. When Ridgeline served a notice of breach, Apex offered to cure only if Ridgeline paid more money and granted more time. The MSA states in terms that such a response “shall not constitute cure.” CSMF ¶¶ 47, 59–61, 64–65, 71–78, 83.

No trial is needed to decide whether Apex breached the MSA, whether Apex may collect fees for milestones it never reached, or whether Apex knowingly misrepresented its capabilities. Ridgeline asks the Court to resolve those questions now and to reserve for trial only the amount of Ridgeline’s damages.

# II. STATEMENT OF UNDISPUTED FACTS

Ridgeline incorporates its CSMF and summarizes the material facts below.

## A. Apex’s Pre-Contract Representations

Ridgeline issued its RFP on January 10, 2022. CSMF ¶ 4. Apex’s January 28, 2022 Proposal, which was led by Vice President of Sales Tara Bellingham and approved by CEO Jordan Kresch, represented that Apex “has deep experience integrating Stratos ERP with Siemens Teamcenter and has successfully completed this integration for multiple manufacturing clients.” It also represented that Apex’s team “includes certified AS9100D compliance specialists,” who would be “engaged throughout all three phases.” CSMF ¶¶ 5–8. None of Apex’s formal references involved Teamcenter or AS9100D work. When Ridgeline’s project lead, Paul Szymanski, raised that gap on a February 14, 2022 call, Ms. Bellingham identified Corridor Metals and PrimeTech Industries as the Teamcenter projects. Mr. Szymanski recorded this in contemporaneous notes. CSMF ¶¶ 10–12. On February 22, 2022, Ms. Bellingham sent a Capability Summary. Its Slide 7 described both engagements as “\[c\]ompleted” Teamcenter integrations with quantified results, and its Slide 9 claimed “6 Certified AS9100D Implementation Specialists on Staff.” CSMF ¶¶ 13–17. The parties signed the MSA six days later. CSMF ¶ 33.

## B. Apex’s Admissions of Falsity and Knowledge

Apex had never performed a Teamcenter integration for any client. Corridor Metals ran SAP, not Stratos, and Apex did no Teamcenter work for it. PrimeTech was a consulting assessment. CSMF ¶¶ 18–21. Apex had one AS9100D-certified employee, Gerald Frisk, who left in April 2022. No certified replacement was hired. CSMF ¶¶ 22–24, 53. Project manager Ryan Ostroff reviewed the Teamcenter language before the Proposal was submitted, “flagged concerns” to Ms. Bellingham, and was told “we’ll figure it out.” He “knew we hadn’t done those integrations.” CSMF ¶¶ 25–27. At the time, Apex faced a cash-flow shortfall and needed \$2.5 million in new contracts by Q1 2022 to satisfy its lender’s covenants. Ridgeline was its largest pending opportunity. CSMF ¶¶ 29–30.

## C. The MSA

The MSA recites that Apex “represents that it . . . possesses the requisite skill, experience, personnel, and technical expertise to perform the implementation services described herein.” It requires Teamcenter integration (§ 2.2(c)) and AS9100D module configuration (§ 2.2(d)). CSMF ¶¶ 35–37. It fixes milestone dates of July 15, 2022 (Phase 1), November 30, 2022 (Phase 2), and March 31, 2023 (Go-Live), and makes them “firm commitments” as to which time “\[i\]s of the \[e\]ssence,” modifiable only by an executed Change Order. CSMF ¶¶ 39–40. Apex warranted workmanlike performance consistent with industry standards, conformity to specifications, and properly qualified and certified personnel (§ 5.1(a), (b), (e)). CSMF ¶ 46. Apex could not replace its project manager without fifteen business days’ written notice and a replacement of equivalent qualifications (§ 2.3). CSMF ¶ 38. Milestone payments were conditioned on Ridgeline’s written sign-off (§ 4.4). CSMF ¶ 43. A response to a breach notice that conditions cure on additional fees or time “shall not constitute cure” (§ 8.2). CSMF ¶ 47.

## D. Apex’s Performance and Ridgeline’s Termination

On May 3, 2022, Mr. Ostroff wrote in Apex’s internal project channel that Apex had “zero experience with Teamcenter,” was “flying blind,” and should not “raise this with the client yet.” CSMF ¶¶ 54–55. Five weeks later he told Ridgeline that Phase 1 was “on track” and that the Teamcenter design was “\[p\]rogressing well.” He now admits it was “\[n\]ot really” on track. CSMF ¶¶ 57–58. Phase 1 was signed off eleven weeks late, on October 3, 2022, with an express written reservation of rights. CSMF ¶¶ 59, 62. Phase 2 was never completed. CSMF ¶ 64. In December 2022, Apex sought \$680,000 and five more months to “re-architect” its failed Teamcenter approach, and Ridgeline refused. CSMF ¶¶ 65–66. In January 2023, Apex moved Mr. Ostroff to a revenue-generating project and replaced him with a five-month employee who had no Teamcenter experience and was still “learning” AS9100D. CSMF ¶¶ 67–70. In February 2023, Apex demanded \$1.2 million more. CSMF ¶ 71. By April 2023, seven of twelve critical AS9100D traceability requirements were non-functional, and three requests for remediation had gone unanswered. CSMF ¶¶ 72–74.

Ridgeline served notice of material breach on May 1, 2023. CSMF ¶ 82. Apex offered to cure within sixty days, “contingent upon Ridgeline’s approval of the revised scope and fee structure” of \$1.2 million. CSMF ¶ 83. Apex did not cure, and Ridgeline terminated effective June 1, 2023. CSMF ¶ 84. Ridgeline then paid replacement vendors \$3,575,000 and reached Go-Live on April 15, 2024, more than a year late. CSMF ¶¶ 85–86.

# III. LEGAL STANDARD

Summary judgment is appropriate where “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). A party may move for summary judgment on a claim or on “part of each claim.” *Id.* A dispute is genuine only if “the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party.” *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). Once the movant carries its initial burden, the nonmovant “must do more than simply show that there is some metaphysical doubt as to the material facts.” *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 586 (1986); *see Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322–24 (1986). Conclusory, self-serving assertions do not create a genuine dispute. *Kirleis v. Dickie, McCamey & Chilcote, P.C.*, 560 F.3d 156, 161 (3d Cir. 2009). Where, as here on its affirmative claims, the movant bears the burden of proof at trial, it must show that the record evidence on each element is such that no reasonable jury could find otherwise. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a); *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 252–55 (the summary judgment inquiry incorporates the substantive evidentiary standard, including clear and convincing evidence). If the Court does not grant all the relief requested, it “may enter an order stating any material fact . . . that is not genuinely in dispute and treating the fact as established in the case.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(g).

The statements of Apex’s CEO, Vice President of Sales, and project manager about matters within the scope of their employment are admissions of a party opponent. Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(2)(D). The MSA is governed by Pennsylvania law. CSMF ¶ 51.

# IV. ARGUMENT

## A. Ridgeline Is Entitled to Summary Judgment on Liability for Breach of Contract.

A breach of contract claim under Pennsylvania law requires “(1) the existence of a contract, including its essential terms, (2) a breach of a duty imposed by the contract\[,\] and (3) resultant damages.” *CoreStates Bank, N.A. v. Cutillo*, 723 A.2d 1053, 1058 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1999); *accord Ware v. Rodale Press, Inc.*, 322 F.3d 218, 225 (3d Cir. 2003). Where contract language is clear and unambiguous, its meaning is a question of law for the court, determined from the writing itself. *Murphy v. Duquesne Univ. of the Holy Ghost*, 777 A.2d 418, 429–30 (Pa. 2001); *Steuart v. McChesney*, 444 A.2d 659, 661 (Pa. 1982). No party contends that the relevant MSA provisions are ambiguous.

### 1. The MSA is a valid and binding contract.

Apex’s CEO signed the MSA, and Apex sues on it in its counterclaim. CSMF ¶¶ 34, 87. The first element is not disputed.

### 2. Apex breached the firm milestone dates, and Phase 2 was never completed.

Sections 3.2 through 3.5 required Phase 1 by July 15, 2022, Phase 2 by November 30, 2022, and Go-Live by March 31, 2023. They declare those dates “firm commitments” as to which time is “of the \[e\]ssence,” alterable “only by mutual written agreement in the form of a Change Order.” CSMF ¶¶ 39–40. Apex missed Phase 1 by eleven weeks. CSMF ¶ 59. Apex never completed Phase 2 at all, and its project manager conceded that “\[t\]he Phase 2 milestones hadn’t been achieved.” CSMF ¶ 64. Go-Live never occurred under the MSA. CSMF ¶¶ 84, 86. No Change Order extended any date. Ridgeline rejected CO-004 and the February 2023 “framework,” as § 6.3 entitled it to do. CSMF ¶¶ 45, 65–66, 71. Because § 3.5 forbids “unilateral extension,” Apex’s hope for more time is legally irrelevant. On these admitted facts, Apex breached §§ 3.2 through 3.5.

### 3. Apex breached its express warranties in MSA § 5.1.

*Section 5.1(a) (workmanlike performance consistent with industry standards).* Ridgeline’s technical expert, Marcus Tran, opines that Apex’s Teamcenter design relied on a deprecated “direct database-level integration” that no competent integrator would have proposed after 2018. He also opines that its AS9100D configuration made “architectural choices that reflect a lack of understanding of what AS9100D requires,” and that Apex’s performance fell below generally accepted industry standards. CSMF ¶¶ 76–78. Apex’s expert does not address § 5.1, does not opine that Apex met industry standards, and does not respond to Mr. Tran’s technical findings. CSMF ¶ 80. Where a qualified expert’s opinion on a technical standard is unrebutted, and the nonmovant offers no contrary evidence, there is no genuine dispute for a factfinder to resolve. *See Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 249–50 (evidence that is “merely colorable” or “not significantly probative” does not defeat summary judgment). Apex’s own admissions are even more direct. Its project manager wrote that Apex was “Googling the API docs” and “flying blind,” and testified that the work was hard “\[b\]ecause we had never done it before.” CSMF ¶¶ 54, 60.

*Section 5.1(b) (conformity to specifications).* Sections 2.2(c) and (d) and Exhibit A required a functioning, bidirectional Teamcenter integration and a fully configured AS9100D module with lot and serial tracking, NCR and CAPA workflows, FAI management, and full aerospace traceability. CSMF ¶¶ 36–37. Apex never delivered a working integration. It abandoned its original approach, and its CEO conceded the approach “needed to be revised.” CSMF ¶ 65. Seven of twelve critical AS9100D traceability requirements were non-functional, and Apex admitted in writing that AS9100D items “remain in progress.” CSMF ¶¶ 72–74.

*Section 5.1(e) (qualified and certified personnel).* Apex’s only AS9100D-certified employee left in April 2022, and no certified specialist worked on the project afterward. Apex then installed as project lead a five-month employee with no Teamcenter experience who was “learning” AS9100D. CSMF ¶¶ 53, 68. That is a breach of the warranty that “\[a\]ll personnel assigned to the Project shall possess the qualifications, training, professional certifications, and experience necessary.”

### 4. Apex breached the key-personnel provision in MSA § 2.3.

Section 2.3 barred Apex from replacing its project manager without fifteen business days’ prior written notice. It also required that any replacement have “qualifications and experience at least equivalent.” CSMF ¶ 38. Apex replaced Mr. Ostroff to redeploy him to a billing project, over his own objection that “this project needed someone with more experience.” CSMF ¶¶ 67, 69. Ms. Cho had never led an implementation of this complexity and lacked Teamcenter and AS9100D experience. CSMF ¶ 68. Apex’s CEO does not claim she was equivalent. When confronted, Apex did not assert that it had given notice. It asserted instead a “discretion” that § 2.3 does not confer. CSMF ¶ 70.

### 5. Apex’s “Ridgeline-caused delay” defense fails as a matter of law.

Apex has asserted that Ridgeline’s IT staff were slow to provide data. CSMF ¶ 83; App. Ex. 16. The defense fails for three independent reasons.

*First*, the MSA extends milestones for Ridgeline-caused delay only if Apex gave “timely written notice specifying the nature of Ridgeline’s failure and its impact on the schedule” and mitigated. CSMF ¶ 41. \[Apex has identified no such § 3.6 notice. *Confirm against full production before filing.*\] Instead, Apex’s contemporaneous written communication told Ridgeline the opposite, reporting on June 10, 2022 that Phase 1 was “on track” and that Apex did not “anticipate any issues.” CSMF ¶ 57.

*Second*, Apex’s own project manager, the person on the ground, testified that “Ridgeline was pretty responsive. The delays were on our side,” and that any minor Ridgeline delays “weren’t the reason we missed the Phase 1 deadline.” CSMF ¶ 61. A party cannot create a genuine dispute through an expert whose premise is contradicted by its own witness’s admissions. *See Kirleis*, 560 F.3d at 161.

*Third*, the defense is immaterial even if fully credited. Apex’s expert attributes at most three to four weeks of Phase 1 delay to Ridgeline. That leaves at least seven weeks of Apex-caused Phase 1 delay. It says nothing about Phase 2, which was more than five months overdue when Ridgeline served notice, or about the non-conforming Teamcenter and AS9100D work. CSMF ¶¶ 64, 72, 81, 82. The expert’s complexity opinion also rests on the premise that Ridgeline ran Teamcenter 12.0, when the MSA specifies version 13.2. CSMF ¶ 81. A dispute that cannot change the outcome is not “material.” *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 248.

### 6. Ridgeline did not waive any breach by paying the Phase 1 milestone.

Apex may argue that Ridgeline’s October 3, 2022 Phase 1 payment accepted Apex’s performance. The MSA forecloses that argument three times over. Payment “shall not constitute acceptance” or “a waiver of any . . . breach” absent a specific signed writing referencing § 4.4 (§ 4.4). Payment accompanied by a “contemporaneous written reservation of rights” works no “acceptance, waiver, release, or estoppel” (§ 4.5). And waivers must be in a signed writing (§ 14.3). CSMF ¶¶ 43–44. Ridgeline’s payment email, copied to Apex’s CEO, expressly reserved “all rights regarding the delay and the incomplete Teamcenter integration design.” CSMF ¶ 62.

### 7. Apex’s breaches were material, and Ridgeline properly terminated under MSA § 8.2.

Whether a breach is material is ordinarily a question of fact. It becomes a question of law where the facts permit only one reasonable conclusion. *See LJL Transp., Inc. v. Pilot Air Freight Corp.*, 962 A.2d 639, 648–52 (Pa. 2009) (holding breach so fundamental that termination without opportunity to cure was permitted as a matter of law); Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 241 (factors include the extent to which the injured party is deprived of the benefit reasonably expected). The parties agreed in § 3.5 that timely completion “is material to this Agreement.” CSMF ¶ 40. Teamcenter integration and AS9100D compliance were the two capabilities Apex itself identified as the reasons to hire it. CSMF ¶ 14. Apex delivered neither. Ridgeline was deprived of essentially the entire benefit of its bargain.

Ridgeline followed § 8.2 to the letter. It gave written notice identifying four specific breaches and allowed thirty days to cure. CSMF ¶ 82. Apex did not cure. Its response made any remediation “contingent upon Ridgeline’s approval of the revised scope and fee structure” and sought to double the cure period. CSMF ¶ 83. Section 8.2 anticipates exactly that response and removes it from dispute: a response “that conditions cure upon the non-breaching Party’s agreement to modify the terms of this Agreement, approve additional fees, or approve an extension of the project timeline shall not constitute cure.” CSMF ¶ 47. Independently, a statement that a party will not perform unless the other party agrees to terms it is not obligated to accept is a repudiation. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 250 cmt. b; *2401 Pa. Ave. Corp. v. Fed’n of Jewish Agencies of Greater Phila.*, 489 A.2d 733, 737 (Pa. 1985). Termination was proper as a matter of law.

### 8. Damages.

The existence of damages is undisputed. Ridgeline paid Apex \$1,282,500, received no functioning system, and paid replacement vendors \$3,575,000. CSMF ¶¶ 63, 85–86. Apex’s own expert concedes that Ridgeline is owed at least \$400,000. App. Ex. 21, Opinion 3. The parties dispute the amount, not whether damages exist, and that dispute does not preclude judgment on liability. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a) (summary judgment available on “part of” a claim). Ridgeline asks the Court to enter judgment on liability and reserve the amount of damages for trial.

## B. Apex’s Counterclaim for Unpaid Milestone Fees Fails as a Matter of Law.

Apex seeks \$1,567,500, the Phase 2, UAT, and Go-Live installments. CSMF ¶ 87. Each installment was “due and payable upon Ridgeline’s written sign-off” (or, for Go-Live, upon achievement of Go-Live). Each was “conditioned upon Apex’s completion of the corresponding Deliverables to Ridgeline’s reasonable satisfaction, as evidenced by Ridgeline’s written sign-off.” CSMF ¶¶ 42–43. A condition precedent is an event that must occur before performance becomes due. If the condition does not occur, the duty never arises. Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 224, 225(1). Ridgeline never signed off on Phase 2 or UAT, and Go-Live never occurred under the MSA. CSMF ¶¶ 64, 86–87. Section 8.4(b) confirms that, upon termination, Ridgeline owes only for services “properly performed and accepted,” subject to setoff. CSMF ¶ 48. Moreover, a party that has committed an uncured material breach cannot insist on the other party’s counter-performance. *LJL Transp.*, 962 A.2d at 648. Summary judgment should be entered for Ridgeline on the counterclaim.

## C. Ridgeline Is Entitled to Summary Judgment on Liability for Fraudulent Inducement.

To establish fraud, a plaintiff must prove “(1) a representation; (2) which is material to the transaction at hand; (3) made falsely, with knowledge of its falsity or recklessness as to whether it is true or false; (4) with the intent of misleading another into relying on it; (5) justifiable reliance on the misrepresentation; and (6) the resulting injury was proximately caused by the reliance.” *Gibbs v. Ernst*, 647 A.2d 882, 889 (Pa. 1994). Fraud must be proven by clear and convincing evidence. *Id.* Here, each element rests on Apex’s own documents and sworn admissions.

### 1. Apex made false representations of existing fact.

Apex’s representations concerned historical facts, not predictions. They addressed whether Apex had “successfully completed” Teamcenter integrations, whether it had done so for Corridor Metals in 2020 and PrimeTech in 2021 with specific quantified results, and how many AS9100D-certified specialists were “on \[s\]taff.” CSMF ¶¶ 6–7, 11, 15–16. Apex’s witnesses concede each was false. Apex had never performed a Teamcenter integration for anyone. Neither named engagement involved one. Apex had one certified specialist, not six. CSMF ¶¶ 18–24. Mr. Kresch’s description of the specialist count as “aspirational” is an admission that the statement described something that did not exist. CSMF ¶ 23. Ms. Bellingham’s testimony that she “may have overstated” and that Slide 7 “wasn’t entirely accurate” leaves nothing for a jury to resolve on falsity. CSMF ¶ 21.

### 2. The representations were material.

A misrepresentation is material if it is of such character that, had it not been made, the transaction would not have been entered into. *See Gibbs*, 647 A.2d at 889. Apex itself told Ridgeline that “Teamcenter integration experience” and “AS9100D compliance expertise” were two of the three reasons Ridgeline should choose Apex. CSMF ¶ 14. Both capabilities were core contractual deliverables. CSMF ¶¶ 36–37. Mr. Szymanski specifically raised the Teamcenter gap before contracting, which is how the Corridor Metals and PrimeTech claims came to be made. CSMF ¶¶ 10–12.

### 3. Apex knew the representations were false.

Scienter requires knowledge of falsity or reckless indifference to the truth. *Gibbs*, 647 A.2d at 889. This case does not require the inferences from circumstantial evidence that usually make intent a jury question. Mr. Ostroff “knew we hadn’t done those integrations,” and he told the author of the Proposal so before it was submitted. CSMF ¶¶ 25–27. Ms. Bellingham, who led the Proposal and personally sent the Capability Summary, admits Apex had “never actually performed a Teamcenter integration for anyone.” CSMF ¶¶ 5, 13, 18. Mr. Kresch approved the Proposal, knew Apex had exactly one certified AS9100D employee, and nonetheless allowed Ridgeline to receive a deck claiming six. CSMF ¶¶ 5, 13, 22, 28. Apex’s documented need to close \$2.5 million in contracts to meet loan covenants supplies the motive. CSMF ¶¶ 29–30. Apex’s post-contract conduct confirms its state of mind. It told its team “\[d\]on’t raise this with the client,” and then told the client that the Teamcenter work was “\[p\]rogressing well.” CSMF ¶¶ 54, 57. No reasonable jury could find that Apex believed its statements were true.

### 4. Apex intended Ridgeline to rely on the representations.

The Proposal and Capability Summary were sales documents prepared “for Ridgeline” to win the engagement. The Corridor Metals and PrimeTech names were given in direct response to Ridgeline’s due-diligence question. CSMF ¶¶ 5, 11, 13–14. Ms. Bellingham conceded Apex “wanted to put our best foot forward” to close the deal. Bellingham Dep. 43:5–9 (App. Ex. 6). A representation made to induce a specific counterparty to contract is, by definition, intended to be relied upon.

### 5. Ridgeline justifiably relied on the representations.

Mr. Szymanski testified that he relied on Apex’s representations, did not call the third reference because of Ms. Bellingham’s assurances, and recommended Apex on that basis. The MSA was signed six days after the Capability Summary arrived. CSMF ¶¶ 4, 31–33. Apex’s anticipated argument that Ridgeline should have demanded a proof of concept or independently verified the named projects fails. A recipient of a fraudulent misrepresentation is justified in relying on it “although he might have ascertained the falsity of the representation had he made an investigation.” Restatement (Second) of Torts § 540. Nor was the falsity obvious. The claimed engagements came with specific dates and quantified results, and verifying them would have required Ridgeline to contact Apex’s other clients about confidential projects that Apex had chosen to keep off its formal reference list. CSMF ¶¶ 10, 15.

### 6. The MSA’s integration clause does not bar the claim.

Apex’s principal defense will be MSA § 14.1, which provides that the MSA supersedes prior representations and that each party “has not relied upon any statement . . . not expressly set forth in this Agreement.” CSMF ¶ 50. Under Pennsylvania’s parol evidence rule, a party to a fully integrated contract generally may not base a fraudulent inducement claim on prior representations concerning matters the contract covers but that the writing omits. *Yocca v. Pittsburgh Steelers Sports, Inc.*, 854 A.2d 425, 436–39 (Pa. 2004); *Toy v. Metro. Life Ins. Co.*, 928 A.2d 186, 204–05 (Pa. 2007). The rule does not apply here, for three reasons.

*First*, the representation at the heart of this claim is “expressly set forth in this Agreement.” In the MSA’s Recitals, “Apex represents that it . . . possesses the requisite skill, experience, personnel, and technical expertise to perform the implementation services described herein.” Those services include the Teamcenter integration and the AS9100D module. Section 5.1(a) and (e) repeat that Apex possesses the requisite “experience” and that its personnel hold the necessary “professional certifications.” CSMF ¶¶ 35–37, 46. By § 14.1’s own terms, Ridgeline’s reliance on those statements is preserved. The rationale of *Yocca* is that a party who signs an integrated writing cannot claim reliance on promises the writing leaves out. *See* 854 A.2d at 436–37. That rationale does not reach a representation the writing includes.

*Second*, Ridgeline does not offer the Proposal, the February 14 call, or the Capability Summary to add to, vary, or contradict the MSA. It offers them as evidence of what the MSA’s own experience-and-personnel representation meant in context and to prove that the representation was false when made and that Apex knew it. The specific claims (Corridor Metals, PrimeTech, “6 Certified” specialists) are the concrete content of the general representation the MSA contains, and Apex’s admissions about them prove the contractual representation false. The parol evidence rule excludes evidence offered to alter the terms of an integrated writing. It does not exclude evidence offered to prove that a term of the writing is a lie.

*Third*, the gist-of-the-action doctrine does not convert this claim into a contract claim. That doctrine asks whether the duty breached arises from the contract or from a broader social duty that exists regardless of the contract. *Bruno v. Erie Ins. Co.*, 106 A.3d 48, 68–69 (Pa. 2014). The duty not to lie about one’s existing capabilities to induce a counterparty to sign is a duty imposed by society, not by the contract, and it was breached before the contract existed. Pennsylvania courts have long distinguished fraud in the inducement, which is collateral to the contract, from fraud in the performance of contractual duties. *See eToll, Inc. v. Elias/Savion Advert., Inc.*, 811 A.2d 10, 17 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2002). Ridgeline’s claim is one of inducement.

Ridgeline acknowledges that courts applying *Yocca* have construed integration and non-reliance clauses broadly, and that Apex will argue that § 14.1 reaches the Proposal and Capability Summary. Even if the Court were to agree, the fraud claim would still rest on the representation contained in the MSA itself. At a minimum, the Court should resolve the scope of § 14.1 now as a question of law, so the parties can try the remaining issues on a defined record.

### 7. Injury and causation.

Ridgeline paid \$1,282,500 for work it could not use and incurred substantially greater cost and delay to obtain the performance Apex promised. CSMF ¶¶ 63, 85–86. The amount of fraud damages, including consequential losses, is properly reserved for trial. Ridgeline seeks judgment on liability.

### 8. Alternatively, the Court should enter a Rule 56(g) order.

If the Court concludes that any element, such as justifiable reliance in light of § 14.1, presents a question for trial, Ridgeline requests an order under Rule 56(g) treating the following as established. First, the Proposal, February 14, 2022 statements, and Capability Summary representations identified in CSMF ¶¶ 6–7, 11, and 15–16 were false when made. Second, they were material. Third, Apex, through Ms. Bellingham and Mr. Kresch, knew they were false or made them with reckless indifference to their truth. Apex’s witnesses have conceded each of these points, and trying them would waste the Court’s and a jury’s time.

## D. MSA § 11.2 Does Not Bar Ridgeline’s Recovery.

Section 11.2 caps each party’s “aggregate liability” at \$2,850,000. CSMF ¶ 49. Two points warrant resolution now.

*First*, the cap does not bar or reduce Ridgeline’s direct contract damages. Those damages consist of the \$1,282,500 in fees paid plus the \$725,000 cost-of-cover differential (\$3,575,000 in replacement cost less the \$2,850,000 contract price), for a total of \$2,007,500. That figure falls below the cap. CSMF ¶¶ 63, 85; App. Ex. 20 § I.C. Apex disputes the amount of these damages, but no amount it could establish would exceed the cap. The cap therefore has no bearing on Ridgeline’s contract recovery.

*Second*, § 11.2 cannot limit Ridgeline’s recovery for Apex’s intentional fraud. Pennsylvania public policy does not allow a party to contract away liability for its own intentional or reckless misconduct. *Cf. Tayar v. Camelback Ski Corp.*, 47 A.3d 1190, 1199–1203 (Pa. 2012) (pre-injury release unenforceable as to reckless conduct as against public policy). A limitation clause procured through the very fraud that induced the contract cannot shield the defrauding party from the consequences of that fraud. At a minimum, the Court should hold that § 11.2 does not apply to damages awarded on the fraud claim, leaving the amount of those damages for trial.
