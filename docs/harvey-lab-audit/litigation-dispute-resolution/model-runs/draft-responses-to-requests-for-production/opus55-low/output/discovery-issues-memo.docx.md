## B. Loss after the hold was issued

Pinnacle describes the Slack loss as caused by the retention policy. But the arithmetic shows that part of the loss happened **after Redfield’s own hold was in place**:

- **Slack.** On September 16, 2024, Slack held messages back to about June 18, 2024. By November 1, only messages from about August 3 forward remained. So messages dated about **June 18 to August 3, 2024** were auto-deleted during the 46 days after the hold issued. That window is when Lakeshore’s demand was presumably being prepared and received.

- **Teams.** Pinnacle does not address this. With a one-year retention policy, Teams messages dated about **September 16 to November 1, 2023** existed on September 16, 2024 and were purged before the November 1 supplemental hold. That period falls in Contract Year 3, during the Northpoint relationship and shortly after the Product Advisory.

- **Voicemail.** Pinnacle says voicemail retention had expired “by the time collection activities commenced.” That appears to be wrong. With 60-day retention, voicemails from about July 18, 2024 forward existed on September 16–17, 2024 and were never preserved. We should correct Pinnacle’s characterization before anyone repeats it.

There is also **pre-hold** exposure. Everything Kessler, Morell, and Reeves discussed on Slack between 2021 and mid-2024 is gone: the Agreement term, Northpoint onboarding, the Elkhorn reports, and the Advisory. Lakeshore will argue that Redfield reasonably anticipated litigation well before September 2024. It will point to the Kessler email, Ng’s April 2023 reference to assessing Redfield’s “legal position,” the April 2024 Lakeshore meeting, and the demand letter. Under Michigan law, the duty to preserve attaches when litigation is reasonably foreseeable, not when suit is filed (see, e.g., Bloemendaal v. Town & Country Sports Ctr., 255 Mich. App. 207 (2002); Brenner v. Kolk, 226 Mich. App. 149 (1997)). The court has inherent authority to remedy spoliation, including through an adverse-inference instruction (M Civ JI 6.01). The failure to suspend auto-delete when the hold issued is the weakest point in our position, because it concerns data Redfield knew it had to keep.

The recipient must then return, sequester, or destroy it and may not use it until the claim is resolved. \[Please verify the current subsection before we cite it. Log entry 178 cites “MCR 2.302(B)(6),” which I believe is the wrong subsection.\] Without an agreement, waiver turns on the reasonableness of our precautions, how quickly we acted to fix the error, the scale of the production, the extent of the disclosure, and fairness. Our precautions are defensible (a two-stage screen across 14,832 documents, and one document out of 1,247). Our speed is not. Every additional day of delay strengthens a waiver argument. It will argue that (a) the “do not circulate the Elkhorn report” instruction is business direction, not legal advice, and (b) the crime-fraud exception applies because the email is alleged to be part of the concealment at the heart of Count II. It may also have already read and relied on the email. We should decide now whether we are prepared to litigate this claim. If we clawback and lose, that ruling may make it harder to protect entries 1 and 3–7.

# VIII. Merits and Pleading Issues Surfaced in Drafting

**A. First Affirmative Defense vs. Agreement § 3.3.** Section 3.3 provides that a shortfall is **not** a material breach “unless and until” Redfield gives written notice electing to reduce the Territory or terminate. It also provides that failing to exercise a remedy within 60 days after the Contract Year ends (roughly March 14, 2024 for Year 3) is a **deemed waiver** of that year’s shortfall. Unless Redfield sent a § 3.3 notice, the First and Second Affirmative Defenses (the Year 3 shortfall as a material breach) are weak, and RFP 16 and 24 will expose that. Please confirm with Ng whether any notice was sent. The draft response to RFP 16 specifically commits to producing any § 3.3 notice or election.

**B. Fifth Affirmative Defense (limitations).** The defense pairs MCL 600.5813 with MCL 600.5855 as a “discovery rule.” Section 5855 is the fraudulent-concealment tolling statute, which extends the time to sue and works in the plaintiff’s favor; it is not a discovery-rule limit. Michigan has also rejected a common-law discovery rule (Trentadue v. Buckler Lawn Sprinkler, 479 Mich. 378 (2007)). Measured against a six-year period, claims based on 2022–2023 test reports look timely. Consider withdrawing the defense or restating it. Note too that the defense makes pre-October 2022 V-Series quality information relevant. That is one reason the draft proposes a QA period starting January 1, 2020 (RFP 7) rather than a narrower one.

**C. Answer ¶ 24 (14-day fulfillment).** We said Redfield met the 14-day commitment. Section 5.1 also requires priority for Lakeshore over non-exclusive distributors and pro-rata allocation during supply constraints. The ERP data produced under RFP 17 will test this, and we should look at it before production.

**D. Answer ¶ 25 (lack of knowledge of resale territory).** See Section III.

**E. Timeliness of the Answer.** The Complaint was filed October 1 and the Answer on November 5. Please confirm the date and method of service of the summons and complaint so we can make sure no default or timeliness issue is lurking.
