# II. Case and deadline context

Pacific Corridor alleges breach, fraud and negligent misrepresentation based on (i) pre-contract fleet, insurance and FY2020 financial representations; (ii) performance against a 120-truck daily availability commitment and alleged missed pickups; and (iii) termination. It claims at least \$14.3 million plus \$1.407 million liquidated damages. Meridian denies liability and counterclaims for \$3.2 million based on Pacific Corridor’s monthly minimum-volume commitment, alleged shortfalls, wrongful termination, and lost profits/reliance costs. Meridian’s Answer/Counterclaim affirmatively says internal finance analyses, senior-management reports and board presentations document the alleged damages (Answer/Counterclaim ¶¶ 45–48, 52–53, 59, 63–65).

Key dates. RFPs served May 15, 2024; responses served June 28; production and log served August 12; deficiency letter August 19; Meridian response September 2; follow-up September 9; latest Meridian response September 15. The Scheduling Order sets fact discovery close September 30, 2025, but fixes October 1, 2024 as the motion-to-compel deadline for productions completed on or before August 31, 2024. It also requires a direct telephone or in-person meet-and-confer, not email alone, at least 14 days before a discovery motion. No completed direct conference appears in the provided email chain; Meridian’s proposed week of September 23 would be too late to support an October 1 filing under that requirement. Arrange a direct conference by September 17 if possible, and address any extension with the Court before the October 1 cap if needed.

# III. Systemic production and response defects

## A. Index accuracy and RFP mapping

- The production index lists numbered entries 1–332, while its summary and the meet-and-confer email state 312 documents and 4,217 pages (Bates MLC-000001–MLC-004217). Reconcile unique document count, grouped families/attachments, page count, Bates continuity, and entry-level production mapping. Several entries aggregate many communications (e.g., 42 letters in MLC-003821–MLC-003979) or use “Various,” limiting document-level author/custodian and request mapping required by the ESI Protocol.

- RFP coding is not reliable as the sole measure of completeness. Examples: the Fleet Capacity Report is indexed under RFPs 3 and 6, not the directly applicable RFP 2/10; monthly operations/dispatch material is coded under RFP 10/20/21 despite the actual RFP 10 seeking Fleet Capacity Report source materials and RFP 21 seeking complaints; KPI reports are indexed under RFP 21 although the served KPI request is RFP 22; and counterclaim damages emails/reports at MLC-003681–MLC-003820 are coded RFP 25, while RFP 25 actually seeks insurer communications. Produce a corrected, document-level responsiveness crosswalk and identify any documents withheld or omitted because of a coding error.

- The Production Index has eight numbered entries spanning MLC-003980–MLC-004055 described as Greystone Distribution Partners records, unrelated to the Pacific Corridor volume/damages request; its detailed content sheet characterizes nine documents in this range as Greystone-related. Explain the misproduction and count; identify whether these are a different client’s confidential records and confirm appropriate access/designation handling. Do not treat them as support for Meridian’s counterclaim.

- The ESI Protocol calls for Bates range, date, author/custodian, document type, subject description and responsive request(s) for each document. The index supplies most fields, but grouped collections and “Various” entries require an item-level supplement. For spreadsheets, fleet databases, dispatch exports and damages models, specifically request native production with formulas/metadata and Bates placeholders under the Protocol.

## B. The written responses do not reliably describe the served requests

Several specific response summaries materially change the subject of the request: RFP 2 (due-diligence materials) is answered as a term-sheet request; RFP 3 (Meridian internal decision analyses) is answered as due diligence; RFP 9 (maintenance/repair records) is answered as vehicle leases; RFP 10 (Fleet Capacity Report sources) is answered as maintenance records; RFP 12 (certificates of insurance) is answered as claim files; RFP 13 (claim files) is answered as endorsements; and RFP 18 (lease obligations/accounting) is answered as board financial presentations. The index also cross-codes material inconsistently. Require amended, request-specific responses tied to the actual May 15 requests, identifying the scope actually searched, what will be produced, what is withheld, and whether responsive material does not exist. Blanket incorporation of all 12 general objections and “subject to and without waiving” language does not resolve ambiguity or identify what is being withheld under Rule 34(b)(2)(C).

# V. Privilege-log analysis

The log was served with the production on August 12 and includes the Scheduling Order’s basic columns: unique placeholder, date, author, recipients/CC, type, subject description, privilege asserted and RFP. The concerns are the facial basis of certain claims and whether descriptions are sufficiently document-specific under the Court’s express order.

## A. Strongest log challenges

- \*\*Entries 12–18 (PRIV-000012–PRIV-000018):\*\* described as internal pre-contract business emails between Nolan Brisk and Rebecca Cho concerning fleet assumptions, owned-versus-total capacity, fleet roster, subcontractor estimates, draft EBITDA and capitalization, and an insurance coverage summary. The listed senders/recipients are business personnel, not counsel; the descriptions sound operational/financial rather than requests for or delivery of legal advice. On this record, attorney-client privilege is not adequately supported. These documents are highly material to intent, knowledge and the fraud/negligent-misrepresentation defenses. Seek production, or an itemized explanation of counsel’s role and the specific confidential legal advice at issue; request in-camera review if unresolved.

- \*\*Entries 33–40 (PRIV-000033–PRIV-000040):\*\* described as ordinary insurance placement/renewal communications between Rebecca Cho and Pinnacle representative James Whitfield, with no lawyer listed. They include the \$2M option and December 1, 2020 reduction endorsement, followed by a January 11 request for a certificate showing \$5M and January 12 transmittal. Work product requires preparation because of anticipated litigation, not routine coverage procurement; no such basis is apparent from the descriptions or dates. Ask for production and the contemporaneous facts supporting anticipation of litigation or any recognized common-interest theory. These entries are central to the insurance representation issue.

- \*\*Entry 31 (PRIV-000031):\*\* described as General Counsel’s January 10, 2021 advice to the CEO and Brisk about fleet-capacity representations and exposure if the actual count were disclosed. This may be a genuine confidential legal-advice communication, but the description raises a focused crime-fraud issue. Do not assume the exception applies solely from the log. Request a non-privileged factual basis and, if necessary, in-camera review under the governing prima-facie standard as to whether advice was sought or used to further prospective fraud, rather than merely to assess exposure.

## B. Log-detail and scope requests

- Several descriptions for entries 1–11, 19–30 and 41–43 use generic formulations (“legal advice regarding…,” “litigation strategy,” “document review”) that may not meet the Scheduling Order’s demand for detailed, document-specific descriptions. Request supplemental descriptions identifying the business/legal issue, context and purpose with enough detail to assess each claim without disclosing substance.

- For entries with attachments or compilations (e.g., 5, 6, 9, 13, 16, 17, 21, 25, 28, 38, 40, 42), require attachment-level identification and privilege analysis, including whether nonprivileged underlying business records are being withheld as a compilation. Do not demand disclosure of protected attorney mental impressions; request production of separable source documents and nonprivileged portions.

- The log’s work-product labels do not distinguish ordinary from opinion work product, although the RFP instruction requests that distinction; the Scheduling Order requires the specific privilege(s), and conclusory labels are expressly insufficient. Ask Meridian to identify the protection and basis document by document, and state whether any responsive portions were redacted rather than withheld in full.

## C. Likely legitimate claims

Entries describing counsel’s litigation advice, draft pleadings, legal analyses of breach/cure/termination and outside-counsel strategy (including many entries 1–11 and 19–30, 32, 41–43) may support privilege or work product if confidentiality, legal purpose and litigation nexus are established. The appropriate request is a more informative log and segregability—not blanket disclosure. Ordinary business material does not become protected merely because counsel later collected, reviewed or transmitted it.

# VII. Bottom line

The production contains meaningful materials—including contract drafts, fleet records, operations reports, selected policies, claims files and counterclaim summaries—but the record presented leaves important claims and defenses materially underdeveloped. The strongest, most supportable near-term issues are (1) RFP 8’s acknowledged but unproduced carrier materials; (2) the RFP 19 seven-month dispatch gap and preservation/accounting explanation; (3) the log’s apparent overclaim over business and insurer communications directly bearing on the alleged fraud; (4) absent board, accounting and lease support despite Meridian’s pleaded reliance on financial analyses; and (5) the incomplete/contradictory production index, termination chronology and counterclaim-damages source records. A fast, focused direct conference and verified supplemental responses are necessary to preserve the October 1 motion deadline.
