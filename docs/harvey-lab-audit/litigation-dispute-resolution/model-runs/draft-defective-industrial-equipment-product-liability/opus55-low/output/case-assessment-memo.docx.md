1.  **Liability against the manufacturers is strong.** Meridian’s preliminary report (Dr. Cordero, April 21, 2025) finds two causes that both had to be present: (a) a **manufacturing defect** in the HydraCore HC-ABL-440 bladder, which tested at 1,450 PSI tensile strength against a 2,800 PSI minimum and 18.7% plasticizer against a 12.0% maximum; and (b) an **Ironclad design defect**, namely no redundant pressure relief on the accumulator circuit, contrary to NFPA T2.6.1-2020 §7.3.4 and ISO 4413:2010 cl. 5.4.7.2. The data logs rule out operator error. The Brennan/Cutler emails (Feb. 3–5, 2025) show that Ironclad knew of “a handful” of pressure complaints and deliberately held back a field bulletin to avoid “a paper trail.” Brennan also named Dalton’s serial number in writing 37 days before the incident. 4.  **Dalton’s net exposure is concentrated in three areas:**

# 4. Liability Analysis

## 4.1 HydraCore Components, LLC (bladder manufacturer)

- **Manufacturing defect.** The bladder departs from HydraCore’s own specification on every property tested: tensile strength at 51.8% of the minimum, plasticizer 55.8% over the maximum, hardness of 48 Shore A against a 60–70 range, and poor retained strength after aging. The fracture was brittle, and there was no external damage or fluid contamination. This fits the classic “deviation from design specifications” test under the Ohio Product Liability Act (R.C. 2307.74). Strength: **high**.
- **Batch-wide problem.** Cutler’s email says Ironclad asked HydraCore for batch records in early February. The records already exist, and HydraCore may also have prior notice.
- **Limits:** HydraCore will say the rupture alone would have caused a controlled shutdown if Ironclad’s design had been adequate. It will try to shift the severity of the harm to Ironclad (and to Dalton’s guarding). It may also claim the benefit of Ironclad’s liability cap, because §12.4 extends the cap to “suppliers.” That argument is weak: HydraCore gave no consideration, §15.8 disclaims third-party beneficiaries, and HydraCore was not a party to the Dalton sale.

## 4.2 Ironclad Systems Corp. (press manufacturer / seller / installer)

- **Design defect** (R.C. 2307.75, or the Michigan equivalent). A single relief valve sits downstream of check and flow-control valves, which trapped the surge. Cordero says a redundant accumulator relief valve would have produced “a controlled fluid leak and orderly press shutdown.” A feasible alternative design and industry-standard practice (presses over 1,000 metric tons) are both documented.
- **Express warranty and misrepresentation.** In Terms §18.1, Ironclad represents that the press is designed “in accordance with” NFPA and ISO standards. Cordero identifies specific non-compliance. This is an affirmation in Ironclad’s own drafted terms, so the §10.4 disclaimer does not reach it.
- **Failure to warn after sale** (R.C. 2307.76(A)(2)). Ironclad had actual knowledge by Feb. 3, 2025, and made a conscious decision not to warn, with Dalton’s unit specifically identified. A bladder inspection or pressure-decay test in early February would probably have caught the defect.
- **Supplier oversight and negligence.** Ironclad chose HydraCore and apparently did no incoming inspection. This needs discovery.
- **Gross negligence, willful misconduct and punitive damages.** The Cutler email is the centerpiece. Punitive damages are available in the employees’ Ohio tort suits (R.C. 2307.80 / 2315.21, “flagrant disregard”; generally capped at 2× compensatory damages). §14.2 waives them in any Dalton–Ironclad arbitration. The email also supports arguments that the cap and the exculpatory terms cannot apply to gross negligence or willful misconduct (under Michigan public policy).
- **Installer negligence (§11.2).** Ironclad agreed to indemnify Dalton only for its *gross negligence or willful misconduct in installation*. Investigate whether Ironclad’s technicians set up or signed off on the accumulator isolation and guarding, and whether they drafted or reviewed LOTO-HX9000-01, which was revised Dec. 13, the last day of installation.

## 4.3 Allocation between the defendants

Cordero calls the bladder defect the **initiating** cause and the design defect the **amplifying** cause, and says both were necessary to the outcome. Neither defendant can escape by blaming the other. Under Ohio law (R.C. 2307.22–.23, verify) the fact-finder apportions fault. A defendant found more than 50% at fault is jointly and severally liable for **economic** damages only; non-economic damages are several. *The damages workbook overstates this as full joint and several liability. It should be corrected.* Ohio may also allow fault to be assigned to non-parties, including an immune employer (R.C. 2307.23(C)). Expect both defendants to point to Dalton.

## 4.4 Dalton’s own exposure

- **Employee tort claims against Dalton.** These are barred by workers’ compensation exclusivity (R.C. 4123.74). The exception is an employer intentional tort under R.C. 2745.01, which requires deliberate intent to injure. The presumption for “deliberate removal of an equipment safety guard” does not fit, because nothing was removed. Risk: **low**, but expect it to be pleaded given the OSHA citations and the September 2024 audit.
- **Comparative fault.** Ironclad and HydraCore will use the OSHA citations, the faded-tag audit, the acceptance “with no exceptions,” and the absence of an independent hazard assessment. Mitigating points:
  - Cordero finds the guarding and LOTO conditions **did not cause** the rupture and bear only on how severe the harm was;
  - the press was in production, not being serviced; and
  - Dalton’s TRIR of 2.86 is below the industry average of 3.2.
- **Contractual indemnity to Ironclad (§11.1(d)).** This is the most dangerous provision. See Section 5.3.

# 5. Anticipated Defenses and Our Responses

## 5.1 Arbitration, forum and governing law (§§14, 15.1–15.2)

The PO incorporates the Standard Terms, says they control, and Kemp signed it as Dalton’s authorized representative. A UCC 2-207 “battle of the forms” argument is not realistic. The clause expressly covers tort claims. **Assume Dalton’s own claims against Ironclad will be compelled to AAA arbitration in Grand Rapids under Michigan law.** Arguments to avoid or narrow it:

- Fraud in the inducement aimed at the arbitration clause itself (weak on these facts);
- the subrogation theory: Dalton, as a self-insured employer, is a statutory subrogee of the employees’ claims (R.C. 4123.931), which are not bound by the clause;
- the §14.6 carve-out for provisional relief, for evidence-preservation orders.

**Non-signatories are not bound.** This covers the Reyes estate, Trask, Anand and HydraCore. The §14.3 language purporting to bind “Buyer’s employees” conflicts with §15.8 and basic contract principles.

## 5.3 Ironclad’s claim for indemnity against Dalton (§11.1(d))

Dalton must indemnify and defend Ironclad against claims by Dalton employees “arising from… proximity to the Products.” The only exception is where a claim is “directly and solely caused by a defect… covered by the warranty” to which Dalton did not contribute.

**Why this is dangerous:**

- The bladder is excluded from Ironclad’s warranty (§10.2(f)).
- A design defect is arguably not a defect “in materials and workmanship.”
- The OSHA citations give Ironclad a “contributed” argument.

So Ironclad will say the exception never applies, and will tender the Reyes, Trask and Anand suits, worth up to about \$10–17M in total, to Dalton.

**Responses:**

1.  Michigan requires clear language before indemnifying an indemnitee for its *own* negligence. There is authority both ways on “any and all claims” wording, so research this.
2.  Indemnity for gross negligence or willful misconduct is against public policy.
3.  The combined effect of §10.2(f) and §11.1(d) is illusory and arguably unconscionable.
4.  The indemnity cannot extend to punitive damages.

**This exposure is probably uninsured** (see Section 7). Treat it as the central risk-management issue.

## 5.4 Contractual limitations period (§15.3)

Twelve months from the occurrence, regardless of discovery. The UCC allows parties to shorten the limitations period to no less than one year (UCC 2-725), and Michigan enforces clear contractual limitation periods. **The arbitration demand must be filed by March 14, 2026**, after the §14.1 thirty-day negotiation notice. The Ohio tort limitations period against HydraCore is two years (R.C. 2305.10), i.e., **March 14, 2027**. Do not rely on it; file well before then.

## 5.5 Other expected defenses

- **Spoliation.** Meridian’s tensile, plasticizer and aging tests were **destructive** and were done without notice to Ironclad or HydraCore. Dalton personnel also removed the data drive (Mar. 15) and moved the wreckage (after Mar. 17). Mitigate now:
  - document the chain of custody;
  - offer joint inspection of the retained specimens;
  - agree a protocol for SEM and fatigue testing, as Cordero recommends.
- **Acceptance / waiver.** The Certificate of Acceptance expressly preserves warranty rights. A latent defect in a bladder could not have been found on inspection.
- **Authenticity and admissibility of the Ironclad emails.** The file does not show how the emails were “recovered.” **Before we rely on or disclose them, establish their provenance.** If they came from an unauthorized or inadvertent disclosure, Ohio Prof. Cond. R. 4.4(b) and possible suppression or disqualification issues arise. The emails are party-opponent admissions if properly obtained. Regardless, Brennan’s March 16 oral statement to Kemp (Kemp email, Appendix J) is an independent source, and the emails will be discoverable from Ironclad.

# 8. Venue and Forum Strategy

**Recommended approach: parallel proceedings, keeping HydraCore in Ohio court.**

1.  **Dalton v. HydraCore: Montgomery County Court of Common Pleas.** Ohio law (OPLA manufacturing defect, negligence). Claims for other-property damage, and workers’ compensation subrogation as statutory subrogee. HydraCore is not bound by the arbitration clause or the cap, and a local jury is available. Assess removal: the S.D. Ohio (Dayton) is still a local forum, and HydraCore’s LLC members’ citizenship determines diversity. Expect HydraCore to implead Ironclad for contribution; Ironclad will then move to compel arbitration only as to Dalton-derived claims.
2.  **Dalton v. Ironclad: AAA arbitration, Grand Rapids.** This is unavoidable for contract and warranty claims absent Ironclad’s waiver. Plead breach of express warranty (§§10.1 and 18.1), failure of essential purpose, gross negligence/willful misconduct (to break the cap), and declaratory relief that §11.1(d) does not apply. Advantages: a single arbitrator can be chosen for technical skill, the process is fast, and confidentiality may push Ironclad toward a quieter settlement. Disadvantages: no punitive damages, limited discovery (seek early document subpoenas for the HydraCore batch records and Apex complaint file), and Michigan law.
3.  **Alternatively, propose a global agreement.** Offer Ironclad a stipulation that Dalton’s claims be tried in Ohio court with the employee cases, in exchange for waiving arbitration. Ironclad may prefer one forum to avoid inconsistent results. Get a **tolling agreement** covering the §15.3 period in any event.
4.  **Employee suits (Reyes estate, Trask, Anand)** will almost certainly be filed in Montgomery County against both manufacturers. Dalton has aligned interests (liability, punitive damages, subrogation) and conflicting ones (§11.1(d), non-party fault allocation, possible intentional-tort claims). Coordinate through the subrogation statute and a common-interest agreement **on liability issues only**. Dalton must receive notice of, and protect its lien in, any settlement (R.C. 4123.931).
5.  **Personal jurisdiction** over Ironclad in Ohio is sound: it sold the press into Ohio and installed and commissioned it on site.

# 9. OSHA Citations: Recommendation to Contest

**File a Notice of Contest on both items by May 16, 2025**, one business day before the computed May 19 deadline. An informal conference does not extend the deadline. Final orders would be used by Ironclad and HydraCore as evidence of Dalton’s fault and to trigger §§11.1(d) and 18.4.

- **Item 1, LOTO (1910.147(c)(4)(i), \$18,900):**
  - The standard covers servicing and maintenance. The injury happened during normal production, and the citation itself says no LOTO was in progress (see 1910.147(a)(2)(i)).
  - The facts conflict. The citation says the ball valve had **no lockout hasp**, but Meridian photographed a valve **equipped with a lockout hasp** (Photos 125–130).
  - The real deficiency (no bleed-down valve or gauge) is an OEM design issue.
- **Item 2, Guarding (1910.212(a)(1), \$12,500):**
  - The standard targets point-of-operation hazards and flying chips, not containment of internal hydraulic components in a catastrophic failure caused by a hidden defect.
  - The press had the OEM guarding package.
  - Operator distance is inconsistent: the citation says 8 ft, the incident report about 4 ft.
  - Argue that the hazard was not foreseeable to the employer.
- **Fallback:** Seek reclassification to “Other-than-Serious,” or re-characterization under Ohio’s settlement provisions, with language that the settlement is not an admission for any other proceeding. Also ask for the good-faith reduction on Item 1 to be restored.
- **Complete the abatement work anyway.** It is the right safety step: add accumulator bleed-down and gauges, add containment/blast shielding, and revise LOTO-HX9000-01. Subsequent remedial measures are generally inadmissible to prove fault (Evid.R. 407). Contesting stays the July 28, 2025 abatement date.
- Request the complete OSHA file, including photographs, samples and interview notes, and ask about the status of the separate **investigation of Ironclad**. That investigation could support our case.
