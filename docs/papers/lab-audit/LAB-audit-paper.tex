\begin{abstract}
As frontier AI models have continued to gain capabilities at a rapid pace, they have saturated traditional benchmarks that posed simple tests of legal knowledge, requiring the industry to develop new benchmarks in 2026 that test models' ability to complete long-horizon legal agentic tasks. Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB) and micro1's LegalReasonBench evaluate AI models' ability to draft legal work against lawyer-written rubrics. Some rubrics are used as reinforcement-learning rewards to train models, while held-out sets from both benchmarks are used to evaluate model performance. To assess these new benchmarks that aim to push the frontier of legal model capabilities, as a practicing lawyer who has 18 years of experience in litigation, I audited the publicly released litigation tasks from both benchmarks. AI auditors screened all of the public litigation criteria (\labCriteria{} in LAB's \labTasks{} litigation tasks; \lrbCriteria{} in LegalReasonBench's \lrbAssignments{} litigation assignments), and I reviewed a random sample of \labSample{} criteria from each benchmark that the AI models had flagged as defective or potentially defective, under sampling protocols committed before the draws. I reviewed the task instructions, the environment or record the agents had been given, the governing law, and how each benchmark's own AI judges graded two sample runs involving that criterion. I found that, in LAB, \labDefective{} of \labSample{} sampled criteria were defective, \labObjective{} of them objective errors in which the record, the law, or the arithmetic contradicts the rubric. In LegalReasonBench, \lrbDefective{} of \lrbSample{} were defective, \lrbObjective{} involving objective errors. At 95\% confidence, at least \labDefectiveLB{} LAB criteria (\labDefectiveShare\% of all litigation criteria) and at least \lrbDefectiveLB{} LegalReasonBench criteria (\lrbDefectiveShareLB\%) are defective, and at least \labObjectiveLB{} and \lrbObjectiveLB{} of them, about 6\% of each benchmark's litigation criteria, are objective errors. A separate check of LAB's litigation environments found at least 303 citation errors among 3,618 citations, including 76 authorities that could not be found.

The defects illustrate the difficulty of reducing professional practice to a series of rubrics and straightforward criteria---but the audit also revealed certain patterns in these benchmarks that suggest ways that these new approaches to evaluating frontier legal AI models can be improved. The defects fall into four groups: errors of law or fact, requirements the task does not justify, rewards for hallucinating (or making assertions unsupported by the supplied record), and rubrics that would fail competent work product by making one choice on a debatable question of judgment the only acceptable answer. Some of these defects have the effect of rewarding worse lawyering. For example, LegalReasonBench included penalties that seemingly were intended to discourage models from endorsing or accepting arguments made by a counterparty that are incorrect or misleading. But the rubric as written (and as applied by the benchmark's AI judges) actually penalizes any ``mention'' of those arguments, so models that correctly pointed out the flaws in an opposing party's argument in order to dismantle it were penalized for doing so.

The audit suggests that there are significant opportunities to improve legal benchmarks and environments, but raises questions about whether existing benchmarks are sufficiently reliable. The defect rates identified here exceed the scoring gaps that separate recently released frontier models on the published leaderboards, and the audit pointed to at least some evidence (although not statistically significant) that the defects could affect some models more than others, potentially impacting the scoring and ranking. The defects can also overstate how much room there is for models to improve on legal tasks: criteria that AI auditors flagged as defective or potentially defective resulted in failures \labFlaggedGradeFailPct\% of the time, compared with a failure rate of \labUnflaggedGradeFailPct\% on criteria the auditors did not flag, suggesting that defects could be lowering every model's score and understating the existing model performance. Many existing efforts to post-train models specifically for legal rely on evidence from held-out tasks from the same benchmark family that the models were trained on. While that shows that the training generalizes beyond the specific tasks used for training, it does not address the concerns of construct validity or establish that better performance on a benchmark corresponds to genuine improvement in legal capabilities. I conclude this paper with specific suggestions on how rubric-based benchmarks and training environments for legal could be improved with better quality control, rubric design, and environment design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Extraordinary gains in the quality of frontier models' legal capabilities over the past 2--3 years have saturated many older legal benchmarks, which tested models on the knowledge of well-settled propositions of law. This extraordinary growth in legal capabilities has led recently to a wave of new benchmarks (which often also serve as training environments) that aim to push frontier models' legal capabilities further. The leading example today is Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB), which evaluates AI agents on a range of legal work product tasks, using LLM judges to score performance against human-annotated rubrics. Harvey has trained its own legal AI models using environments that share LAB's structure, and Applied Compute has used LAB as a reinforcement-learning reward \citep{harveyLAB,harveyTenet,appliedCompute}. More recently, micro1, a data vendor that sells training environments to AI labs, built LegalReasonBench the same way \citep{micro1LRB}.

The objective of these benchmarks, and their related training environments, is to create reward signals for evaluating or training models on high-level legal tasks. The expert-written rubrics aim to reduce at least a major portion of a lawyer's professional judgment to a list of criteria that can help judge models distinguish good from bad legal work product. Recognizing that this process is imperfect, both micro1 and Harvey open-sourced a portion of their benchmark tasks; Harvey expressly invited practicing lawyers to audit the criteria its benchmarks use and to offer feedback on how these benchmarks can be improved \citep{harveyLAB}. Taking up that invitation, I audited the open-source litigation tasks from both benchmarks in order to understand how effectively the AI training techniques used at the frontier teach legal models legal judgment. I am a litigator with sixteen years of practice at top 10 law firms, with a broad generalist litigation practice that has given me exposure to everything from criminal trials and mass torts to securities class actions and bankruptcy; as such I audited the litigation tasks. After reviewing an initial sample, I gave AI auditors instructions on how to assess every criterion in LAB's \labTasks{} publicly available litigation tasks and in the \lrbAssignments{} litigation assignments of LegalReasonBench's public sample. The AI auditors flagged criteria they believed were defective or arguably defective under the standards I had provided. I then reviewed a simple random sample of \labSample{} AI-flagged criteria from each benchmark, reading the task instructions, the supplied record, the governing law, and how each benchmark's own judges had graded two frontier models on the same criterion.

The results showed that measuring further gains in legal capabilities, from the already high base that frontier models have achieved, has become extraordinarily difficult. Both benchmarks, even though they were developed by industry leaders for use with cutting-edge reinforcement-learning techniques, had a significant defect rate, including a mix of legal or factual errors, and rubrics that trained models to take steps that I considered to be unreasonable professional judgments. The audit established, with 95\% confidence, that at least \labDefectiveShare\% of LAB's litigation criteria and at least \lrbDefectiveShareLB\% of LegalReasonBench's are defective, and that at least \labObjectiveLB{} LAB criteria and \lrbObjectiveLB{} LegalReasonBench criteria, about 6\% of each, are objective errors. To be clear, this audit does not suggest that these benchmarks are unusually flawed: they are the leading public examples of their kind, I have no comparable audit of any other benchmark to measure them against, and \pending{Harvey's research team welcomed the feedback and told me they would use it to improve future versions of the benchmark}. Rather, the audit illustrates the challenges of translating lawyer judgment into machine-readable rubrics at scale and raises the question of how and whether AI researchers can be confident that measured gains in legal benchmarks correspond to meaningful improvements in legal capabilities.

One of LegalReasonBench's litigation assignments illustrates the issue. In the task environments, AI models are told they represent a plaintiff who has filed a medical malpractice case in Cook County (Chicago) and need to draft a brief opposing the defendants' motion to transfer venue to a different county where the defendants reside. The defendants' brief argues, as one reason for the transfer, that the Cook County courts have only a 60.5\% clearance rate, but the report from which this statistic is taken is included in the environment and makes clear that the defendants' argument is misleading. It conflates criminal and other cases with the clearance rate for civil matters, which is the relevant rate for a medical malpractice case and is much higher. The expert-written rubric indicates that the model drafting the plaintiff's brief should be penalized if its brief ``states'' that the report shows a 60.5\% clearance rate. The expert presumably meant to make the point that the model should not \emph{endorse} or \emph{adopt} defendants' position that the 60.5\% clearance rate is the relevant comparison. The task presumably wants to check that the model realizes the defendants' statistic is misleading and thus refrains from endorsing it in the opposition brief. But the AI judges interpret ``states'' literally: one of the AI models I tested on the benchmark, Claude Opus 5.5, dealt with the defendants' argument exactly the way a good lawyer would have---only to be punished for it. Under the heading ``Defendants rely on the wrong statistic,'' it stated the defendants' figure, explained that the statistic lumps together criminal, traffic, family, and juvenile matters with civil cases, and it cited the civil case statistic that actually matters: 97.9\% for Cook County and 96.3\% for the proposed forum---meaning, if anything, that on defendants' preferred measure, plaintiff's chosen forum of Cook County was better. But all three AI judges imposed a 30-point penalty on Opus for that response (tied for the heaviest penalty weight in the assignments I reviewed), because the rubric as written literally requires the agents not to mention the statistic at all, not even to refute it---even though that's exactly what a good lawyer should do. Holding every other grade fixed, that one penalty took the assignment score from 86\% to 45\% (Appendix~\ref{app:native}).

I observed several other kinds of defects in the audit. In several instances, criteria simply got the law wrong---or demanded that models assert a fact that was contradicted by the task environment provided to the model. Some rubrics required models to take a particular position on a question of professional judgment where, in my view, the best lawyers either would reach a different position from the one the rubric required or at least would consider a position that the rubric would fail to reward to be no less reasonable than the rubric's preferred position.

Section~\ref{sec:design} reports the results of the audits. Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms} sorts the defects I observed into four recurring groups and gives examples of each. Section~\ref{sec:implications} explores what implications this might have for legal-domain evaluations and training: what the scores can establish at the frontier, where the rewards favor worse lawyering, why penalizing errors is easier than rewarding judgment, and how legal environments could be improved. The appendices have a detailed write-up on each criterion I reviewed, with notes on how the AI models I tested performed on those criteria and a narrative description of my reasoning for each determination I made.

\section{Audit design and results}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{The benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

Harvey AI Corporation, the leading vertical legal AI company today, released LAB in May 2026. In recent model releases, LAB has been the most widely cited legal benchmark used by frontier labs to illustrate the legal capability of their current frontier models: Anthropic, Google, SpaceX, and Meta, among other labs, included LAB scores in their release posts or system cards in 2026 (OpenAI's GPT-6 model releases did not include a legal benchmark score)\footnote{OpenAI's September 2026 announcement of Astra for Law, a legal product rather than a model release, reported results on Vals AI's Legal Research Bench instead.} \citep{xaiGrok47,googleGemini4Argon,anthropicOpus55Card,metaMuseWonder,mistralLarge4}. The initial release had roughly 1,250 tasks across 24 practice areas (of which litigation, the area I audited, is just one) and more than 75,000 expert-written rubrics. Harvey has said LAB uses real client matters handled by practicing lawyers as inspiration for AI-generated task documents and environments \citep{harveyLAB}. Harvey uses AI generation to get around the difficulty of getting clients to share real legal documents, which risks waiver of privilege over internal materials. Each LAB criterion is a binary pass or fail. Somewhat unusually, Harvey took the position that in legal work missing even one issue is a critical mistake, so a task is graded successful only if every criterion passes. A portion of the benchmark is open source on GitHub and available for lawyers to audit, while a separate private hold-out set is used to report LAB results on the Artificial Analysis leaderboard \citep{labAAboard}. Harvey says it started from real client matters, split them into tasks, and had the rubrics written and reviewed by experts; its launch post names Mercor and Snorkel AI as contributing research groups, and a later talk by Harvey's co-founder describes those providers as running expert review and verification of every generated LAB task \citep{harveyLAB,harveyResearchLab}. \pending{In comments on an earlier draft of this paper, Harvey told me that the outside providers had a more limited role on the public LAB tasks than on the held-out set used for the Artificial Analysis evaluation.} A public post by one of LAB's authors describes review by more than 100 lawyers, at Harvey and through its partner vendors, totaling thousands of hours \citep{pereyraClarification}. I audited the \labTasks{} tasks and \labCriteria{} criteria in the public litigation and dispute resolution folder at Harvey commit \texttt{1dd81403}.

micro1 is a data and research lab that produces reinforcement learning environments used by frontier labs to improve their models, in legal and several other domains, using experts such as practicing attorneys to help with the work. LegalReasonBench has 50 matters, 122 assignments, and 2,236 criteria, of which 353 are penalty criteria. micro1 states that a practicing lawyer created each assignment, the associated case file, and its rubrics \citep{micro1LRB}. It also reports a three-judge classification of its criteria into IRAC categories with high agreement, which is a classification, not a substantive validation of each criterion. The open-source public sample on Hugging Face has 15 assignments and 221 criteria. I audited a litigation subset that covers \lrbAssignments{} assignments, with \lrbCriteria{} criteria across four matters. At least three of the four appear to be adapted from real cases with names and some facts changed. Each rubric has an associated points value; three AI judges decide, by majority vote, whether a given rubric was satisfied or not. The task score is calculated as the points earned on positive rubrics, less penalties imposed under negative rubrics, divided by the total points available on positive criteria; a negative score counts as zero. The dataset I reviewed is pinned on Hugging Face at revision \texttt{1bfa904c} \citep{lrbaudit}. Neither release permits reconstructing the review history of a disputed criterion.

There are two noteworthy differences between the benchmarks: LAB's all-pass scoring rule means that one failed criterion results in a zero on an otherwise excellent deliverable, while LegalReasonBench imposes a points-based penalty but does not zero tasks for defects. Additionally, the task environments in Harvey's LAB are primarily synthetic with no recognizable connection to real cases, whereas micro1's LegalReasonBench tasks plainly are adapted from real litigation matters. There were tradeoffs and different failure modes associated with each of these approaches (Section~\ref{sec:errors} and Appendix~\ref{app:mtd}).

\subsection{AI screening and two sampling frames}

To conduct the audits, I gave GPT-6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 examples of defective rubrics and asked them to flag any defective or arguably defective criteria. For LAB, Sol flagged \labSolFlagged{} criteria and Opus flagged \labOpusBlind{}; after Opus reviewed Sol's findings, \labFlagged{} criteria (\labFlaggedPct\%) were flagged by one model or both. For LegalReasonBench, Sol flagged \lrbSolFlagged{} criteria and Opus \lrbOpusFlagged{}; between them they flagged \lrbFlagged{} of \lrbCriteria{} criteria (\lrbFlaggedPct\%), and a later reconciliation pass retained \lrbReconciled{} of the \lrbFlagged{} \citep{lrbaudit}. In both audits an Opus pass was permitted to review both models' findings. This was intended to operate as two standalone reviews of these benchmarks, not a formal statistically valid comparison of the defect rate between them.

\subsection{The standard I applied}

After the AI agents flagged defective or arguably defective criteria, I drew a simple random sample of \labSample{} criteria from each benchmark without replacement, with a fixed seed, using a sampling protocol that was committed before the draw \citep{labreview,lrbreview}. Rubrics where the answer key contains legal or factual errors, or rests on objective discrepancies or errors in the task environment, were considered defective and labeled objectively incorrect.

An additional set of rubrics was labeled defective as a matter of professional judgment; this was used in situations where the rubric required a decision that, in my view, top lawyers would consider inaccurate. Professional judgment defects also include a few instances where the rubric would fail a response that, in my view, most lawyers would see as a judgment call---and the rubric effectively forces the AI model to take one particular position on an issue where there is actually room for debate, which can itself be a kind of legal error. For example, LegalReasonBench required advising a governmental client who lost an administrative review petition in district court to both appeal the loss and redo the decision at the agency; that's a reasonable judgment, and probably the default path most lawyers would take, but in my view it's erroneous to require that recommendation in the internal memo to the client. The circumstances of that case are such that the client's objectives could be fully achieved by simply redoing the decision at the agency level and forgoing the appeal (which likely would become moot once the agency reinstated its decision with new reasoning). Thus, in the context of the assignment for an internal memo, the rubric ideally would credit a memo that presents both options to the client and allows the client to decide whether the added expense of the appeal is worthwhile instead of simply assuming that appealing the adverse ruling is the best approach.

After preparing my initial draft of the verdicts, I asked AI models (GPT-6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5, and Claude Fable 5.1) to stress test them and argue both sides of each call, then did a consistency pass through the full set. A handful of verdicts changed based on the AI-assisted stress testing and additional review that I did.

\subsection{Native runs and citation checks}

I ran both benchmarks on GPT-6 Luna (xhigh reasoning) and Claude Opus 5.5 (low reasoning), using each benchmark's own code (including the benchmark's harness, judge models, and scoring code). This was intended merely as a diagnostic, not a model comparison, to understand where a given rubric might pass for one model but fail for the other and whether that was based on genuine differences in the quality of the legal work product or pointed to potential defects in the rubrics (Appendix~\ref{app:native}).

The rubric audit revealed that some of the task documents on Harvey's LAB contained hallucinated case law; some of the LAB answer keys similarly referred to hallucinated cases. I had AI agents check every citation in the LAB litigation environments against CourtListener, then reviewed a random sample of the flagged citations myself under a pre-committed stopping rule (specifically, I drew 20 and committed in advance to stop after the first 10 if all 10 were confirmed errors, and otherwise to review all 20). A separate AI citation census covered the LegalReasonBench litigation documents (Appendix~\ref{app:citecheck}).

In LAB, \labObjective{} of the \labDefective{} defective criteria are objective errors, such as these: the environment's time entries add up to \$215,680, not the answer key value the rubric assumes of \$193,372.50; the rubric states the opposite of Georgia tortious interference law; the rubric finds a conflict in a preservation notice that contains none. In LegalReasonBench, \lrbObjective{} of \lrbDefective{} are objective errors, and the remaining \lrbJudgment{} are errors of professional judgment; \lrbFromEnv{} arise from the task materials rather than the criterion alone.

In cases where both AI auditors considered a rubric defective, they were almost always right. In LAB, \labBothDefective{} of the \labBothFlagged{} criteria flagged by both models were defective, compared with \labSingleDefective{} of \labSingleFlagged{} flagged by one; in LegalReasonBench, all \lrbBothFlagged{} flagged by both blind reports were defective, compared with \lrbSingleDefective{} of \lrbSingleFlagged{}.

The two model runs I did with the benchmarks illustrate how much all-pass scoring (used in Harvey's LAB) impacts the results. Across the \labTasks{} LAB litigation tasks, averaging each judge's per-task pass fraction, GPT-6 Luna passed 83.8\% of criteria and Claude Opus 5.5 passed 90.8\%. The all-pass rate, by contrast, was 1.9\% for Luna and 2.9\% for Opus, and each model passed exactly one task outright with both judges. Under LAB's all-pass approach, one defective criterion is enough to fail a task. Thus, LAB's headline number is unusually sensitive to the correctness of each criterion.

I also did a sweep for hallucinated case law, which found a significant number of hallucinations in Harvey's LAB (likely owing to the synthetic data used to create it). Within LAB's litigation environments, an AI check of 3,618 citations found 436 seemingly unintentional errors in citations to purported real authority, including 76 authorities that could not be found. I reviewed a random sample under a pre-committed stopping rule and confirmed that the first ten I reviewed were all true citation errors. Statistically, the lower bound is that at least 303 of the 436 are real errors. While a similar AI audit yielded some instances where cases were mischaracterized in LegalReasonBench, it did not flag any hallucinated cases.

\section{How the criteria misgrade legal work}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

The defects I identified, \labDefective{} in LAB and \lrbDefective{} in LegalReasonBench (LRB), are grouped here into four main categories: errors of law or fact in the rubrics; misspecified tasks or incorrect requirements; rewarding hallucinations or unsafe drafting; or errors of professional judgment. Table~\ref{tab:defects} shows the count by group, and Tables~\ref{tab:errors} through \ref{tab:strategy} list each defect in its group. The complete review of all 50 sampled criteria is in Appendices~\ref{app:labreview} and \ref{app:lrbreview}.

These groups are somewhat overlapping. For example, the motion-to-dismiss criterion (LAB item 18) rewards models for raising a factual dispute on a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), but courts are not permitted to resolve factual disputes under the rule. I consider that rubric an error of law, because it wants the model to cite facts from the defendant's internal documents that are not incorporated into the complaint, which Rule 12(d) generally prohibits. \emph{See Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues \& Rights, Ltd.}, 551 U.S. 308, 322 (2007). But it is also a strategic problem: raising that argument makes it easy for the opponent to point out to the judge that the movant is really trying to use the motion to dismiss to argue the facts, which makes it more likely the judge will simply deny the motion, even if there were other, better arguments. In that case, and others, a given rubric could fit under more than one of these groupings and I simply assigned it to the one that fit best. With one exception, the first group coincides with the \labObjective{} LAB and \lrbObjective{} LegalReasonBench criteria coded as unambiguous objective errors, and the other three groups are the professional-judgment defects. The exception is the parol-evidence criterion (LAB item 17), an objective error that I have placed in the third group because of what it rewards.

\paragraph{Errors of law.} There were several instances of straightforward errors of law, where the answer key is simply wrong.
\begin{itemize}
\item The jury-instruction task requires the memo to explain that under Georgia law tortious interference with a contract does not require an independently wrongful act while interference with business relations does. The AI auditors flagged that Georgia actually applies the same elements to both, and I did research to confirm they are correct. The fictional court order from which the rubric's law seems to be taken cites a case that cannot be found (LAB item 23).
\item A LegalReasonBench land-use criterion rewards models that invoke Fla.\ Stat.\ \S~163.3167(10). But that statute applies only if a development is approved under regulations, no one challenges the approval within the deadline, and the regulations at issue are later invalidated (for example, in a subsequent case involving a different development that results in a court challenge). In this case, the approval at issue was timely challenged and quashed by the court (item 5) so the statute cited is irrelevant. Other criteria in this same matter similarly try to apply a county rule that by its terms governs only remands from the Board to the hearing examiner, when this case involves a remand from the examiner to staff (items 1 and 6).
\item A jury-instruction task rewards arguing that the defendant's plea to conspiracy bars a self-defense defense under Iowa Code \S~704.6, a provision that precludes a defendant from claiming justification if the defendant was committing a forcible felony (LRB item 21). But that means a different forcible felony than the one for which the defendant is claiming justification; the paradigmatic case would be that, say, an armed robber who tries to rob someone with a baseball bat cannot claim ``self-defense'' if the victim turns out to have a gun and puts the robber in fear for his life. It does not mean, as the rubric suggests, that a defendant who is attacked and uses force in response thereby negates his own claim of self-defense; that circular logic would eliminate self-defense completely, since self-defense is asserted precisely when the defendant claims to have used force to defend himself or others.
\item LAB includes a motion to dismiss drafting assignment (LAB item 18), where the model is required to prepare a memorandum of law supporting a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss a complaint. Criterion C-045 requires the brief to blame plaintiff's own failures (including late specifications and a vacancy in the project manager role) ``as causes of the Go-Live delay.'' But this in effect demands that the model raise disputes over causation in a motion to dismiss, which are paradigmatic fact issues; moreover, the factual assertion that plaintiff caused the delays is grounded in the defendant's internal documents, not allegations made on the face of the complaint. On a motion to dismiss, the court must take the complaint's allegations as true so there is no legal basis for introducing the defendant's claimed version of events in the procedural posture of a motion to dismiss. The AI models that completed this task correctly left those issues out, and were failed on C-045 as a result, effectively penalizing them for a procedural judgment they got right.
\end{itemize}

In the course of discussing these results with people involved in creating these benchmarks, one argument raised was that environments that are internally inconsistent about the law are genuinely problematic, but that environments that are internally consistent are not problematic simply because they assert legal propositions that contradict the real-world legal rules. I have not accepted that position. Environments that reward models for applying some sort of fictitious, stylized version of the law teach the model that its knowledge of the law from pre-training is not useful for earning reward. In an adversarial legal setting, models should start with reasonably strong priors about the right answer and not blindly defer to evidence in the environment that contradicts their memory \citep{knowledgeConflicts}. A good lawyer uses their extrinsic knowledge and training to identify claims or arguments that appear unsound or potentially debatable, and to investigate those before accepting them. Here, the reward seems to punish this desirable behavior. On LAB item 17, for example (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}), the model that checked the two cases, found they did not say what the memo claimed, and cited the right authority was failed. When given a task environment, the model has no way to know whether law supplied in the environment is a deliberate fiction that the environment's creators want the model to simply treat as true for purposes of a stylized exercise, or an error or misstatement of the law that the model is expected to identify and correct. While an environment can properly modify the specific facts of a given case (since these vary from case to case), the law itself is public and changes only slowly. While an environment can vary the law (e.g., introduce newly passed legislation to train the model to check for recent updates rather than relying blindly on its memory from pre-training in every case), environments should be designed so that models are rewarded for being skeptical of legal propositions that are completely at odds with their pre-training data, unless they are given a clear explanation (like a new authority that changed the law since the model's training cutoff).

\paragraph{Errors of fact and arithmetic.} The invoice task rubric says the budget overage is 17.2\%, which is what is shown on the invoice's summary page. But the 117 individual time entries sum up to \$215,680, which puts the overage at 30.7\%, far outside the rubric's tolerance (LAB item 1). The discovery-gap criterion requires the memo to say that only five documents relate to the counterclaim damages, when a sixth plainly does (item 3). The interrogatory criterion gives the model only calendar-year purchase data for a request that seeks contract-year purchases, which are not supplied (item 19). In LegalReasonBench's jury-instruction matter, 25 points are awarded for asserting that there's no evidence about who fired first. While that was true of the real case on which this environment is based, this task was constructed as a ``closed universe'' and includes a statement of facts that modifies the facts somewhat from the real-world case, including that the statement says explicitly who fired first (item 23).

\paragraph{Errors in reading interacting provisions.} Reading a provision together with its qualifications and exceptions is a transferable skill, and two criteria do this incorrectly (one of them, LAB item 20, is discussed below). A travel policy permits business class on domestic flights of four hours or more, and the rubric treats a flight it describes as four hours and twenty minutes as a borderline issue that must be flagged for discussion, when the policy expressly says that's allowed (LAB item 25).

\paragraph{``No conflict'' is an answer.} Two criteria, one in each benchmark, require the model to find a discrepancy that is not there. In LAB's preservation notice task, a Department of Justice notice defines the relevant period as January 1, 2019 forward, ``unless otherwise specified herein,'' and then provides that ``[n]otwithstanding the Relevant Period defined in Paragraph 12,'' certain financial records must be preserved from January 1, 2017. Pursuant to Criterion C-002, the model is rewarded if the draft memo recommends asking the DOJ to provide clarification ``regarding the conflicting date ranges.'' But there is no genuine conflict. The notice sets a time period applied to all requests by default, and then, for one, makes an exception and uses a longer period. Luna understood this correctly, but was failed on this rubric by both AI judges. Opus's memo was failed by one AI judge and passed by the other, on the strength of an unrelated clarification request about category numbering.

In LegalReasonBench's police-discipline matter, criterion 8 of the first assignment awards 28 of 71 available points for identifying ``that the comparator memorandum's stated coverage years conflict with the dates of the cases it summarizes.''

\begin{itemize}
\item The 60.5\% penalty described in the introduction of this paper is the clearest case.

The defendants' motion to transfer relied on the 60.5\% clearance rate in Cook County as a reason to move the case elsewhere. The models drafting the response correctly realized that statistic was misleading because it includes a variety of other case types, not just civil cases, and made that point to rebut the defendants' argument. The rubric then punished them for that, because the rubric penalizes any mention of the issue. Presumably the environment's creator only meant to penalize endorsing or adopting the 60.5\% statistic as the correct figure, rather than disputing its relevance.
\item The same mechanism appears in the criminal appeal (LRB item 22), where a $-$5 penalty fires if the brief ``mentions'' that the defendant pled guilty in state court to intimidation with a dangerous weapon. Opus was penalized for including a drafting note to the user correctly explaining that it was \emph{not} citing that other incident because it was from an unrelated case (the point the rubric was meant to test).
\item The first forum non conveniens assignment had the same verbal formula (``Mentions \ldots{} that Cook County lacks interest''). Although it did not fire on my two test runs, I labeled that one defective as well because the formulation clearly can (depending on how the LLM judge interprets it in a given run) punish models that simply recite defendants' argument that Cook County lacks an interest to rebut it.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Requirements the assignment does not justify.} The second subgroup consists of cases where the prompt to the model does not reasonably convey what work the rubric expects the model to have done. One case involved a prompt that asked a model to write a memo analyzing a specific conflicts issue impacting a particular case; the rubric rewarded the model for making recommendations to improve the firm-wide conflicts database, an entirely different project that (even if the model could infer it might be desirable) belongs in a different task and memo.

LegalReasonBench uses sequential assignments, where the model completes two tasks in the same environment and sandbox. While in most cases the two tasks reviewed were sufficiently independent that they could be graded separately, in one instance, the second task was an assignment to supplement the analysis from the first task with a new case. The models' outputs appeared substantively competent and useful but lost points because the second task repeats the criteria from the first task, and the models did not appreciate that their output for the second task needed to first repeat everything they had just output for the first task. The criteria reward a particular mechanical approach to revising documents that would normally be handled at the harness level and where the expectation is not clearly communicated to the model in the task environment. While not every requirement needs to be spelled out in detail, and it's appropriate to test the model's ability to infer things from the environment, the specific rubrics here tested requirements that do not follow reasonably from the assignment and context provided (and where the rubric is not really rewarding better legal judgment, just a particular mechanical approach to editing documents within the environment).

\paragraph{Hallucinations.} LAB's motion-to-dismiss task (discussed under item 18 above, Criterion C-037) requires the brief to cite ``relevant Delaware authority on the parol evidence rule and integration clauses, such as SIGA Technologies v. PharmAthene or Eagle Industries v. DeVilbiss.'' The task environment contains a research memo with hallucinated quotations from both cases for language that does not actually appear in them. The rubric also conflates two distinct legal doctrines: under Delaware law the parol evidence rule and an integration clause are not the doctrines that bar a fraud claim on this fact pattern; rather, the issue is whether the plaintiff can reasonably allege reliance on pre-signing representations after agreeing contractually that it was not relying on any such representations. In its test run on this rubric, Opus correctly did not cite these cases and instead cited the correct doctrine from Delaware (the anti-reliance cases). That resulted in a failing grade.

\paragraph{Unsafe drafting (presenting hoped-for evidence as if it had been obtained).} LegalReasonBench had two sequential forum non conveniens tasks. The first states in the task environment that another lawyer on the case team has spoken with five Cook County physicians and learned they were willing to testify. But no affidavit from any of them (or from the plaintiff) is in the record, and the prompt states that a document not uploaded ``does not exist for purposes of this analysis.'' This is an undesirable training signal, as currently formulated. While preliminary phone conversations indicating that third-party witnesses are willing to help are useful, actually obtaining executed affidavits from third-party witnesses that counsel does not control (in time to submit them in opposition to an already-filed motion to transfer) is a substantial undertaking. Several things can go wrong that result in the contemplated affidavits not being received or executed in time to support this opposition, as set forth more fully in the appendix entry for this rubric (Appendix~\ref{app:lrbreview}, item 14). There are several reasons to question whether it is strategically beneficial to submit any of these affidavits, let alone all five, and even if it is, there is no guarantee that the witnesses ultimately will follow through in time and sign affidavits that say exactly what counsel wants them to say. As a general matter, moreover, lawyers are extremely concerned about models hallucinating things that could result in sanctions, as a public database of court decisions had identified more than 2,200 cases worldwide, as of November 2026, in which lawyers or litigants were found to have relied on fictitious citations or other AI-generated material, nearly 900 of them involving lawyers \citep{charlotinDatabase}. Training models to just output a draft citing to affidavits that don't exist or that are still in draft form creates a genuine practical risk of human error that could lead to sanctions; the draft circulated within the firm might make a senior partner think that someone else has already obtained the necessary affidavits when in fact that's an open issue. If a careless junior associate or paralegal files the brief without checking, a brief that asserts the case team has affidavits that do not exist could hit the docket, creating a substantive issue in the case.

For these reasons, I think the approach of models that do not cite to these as of yet nonexistent documents is much safer and preferable in an age of AI drafting. Moreover, if an AI product wanted to test a rubric like this, a much better approach would be to require that the model clearly indicate in draft brackets that the citation is to expected or contemplated testimony that the case team hopes to achieve but has not yet. The actual drafts that Opus prepared (which were rewarded) had no draft brackets around the citations so, by industry convention, they look like final citations to documents that actually exist. Having a citation of the form ``[Proposed Vance Aff.\ \P~\_\_ (not yet executed)]'' allows testing the same substantive output but is much safer because it avoids the risk of miscommunication within the case team or an erroneous filing being submitted to the court.

The LAB interrogatory-response criterion (item 19, which I counted in the first group) involves an interrogatory that is seeking contract-year data but the environment contains only calendar-year figures, which are not the same. The rubric passes an answer that conceals this gap, by just providing the calendar-year data from the environment. (It appears that the mismatch was unintentional, but the consequence is that this rubric might train models to think that details like that don't matter, when they do.)

\subsection{Errors of professional judgment}
\label{sec:strategy}

The last group is for rubrics that take a position on a debatable question of professional judgment, and would fail a response that is nuanced, thoughtful, and at least as good as the rubric's preferred response. In some cases, these are rubrics that require models to advance arguments or take litigation steps that are probably not necessary and which involve costs or drawbacks that may or may not be appropriate given the client's objectives. I ultimately labeled these rubrics defective not because they are wrong in isolation but because they often incentivize the model to reach one particular result on a judgment call, where the better answer would be to acknowledge that there are two (or more) options, both of which entail costs and tradeoffs, so that the client can make an informed decision. Three examples illustrate this point, all instances where the rubrics require models to take a certain step in litigation or advance a certain argument. The rubrics seem to proceed on the assumption that it is desirable to have more arguments and points raised, but quite often, the opposite is true. As a Supreme Court justice and a leading legal writing professor have argued: ``The most important---the very most important---step you will take in any presentation, whether before a trial court or an appellate court, is selecting the arguments that you'll advance'' \citep[\S~12]{scaliaGarner}. Less is often more in litigation. ``Scattershot argument is ineffective,'' \citep[\S~12]{scaliaGarner} and spends down the advocate's credibility with the court, while distracting from more important points. The Supreme Court has said the same in holding that appointed appellate counsel need not raise every nonfrivolous issue a client wants raised: ``Experienced advocates since time beyond memory have emphasized the importance of winnowing out weaker arguments on appeal and focusing on one central issue if possible, or at most on a few key issues,'' \emph{Jones v. Barnes}, 463 U.S. 745, 751--52 (1983), and, quoting Justice Jackson, ``[l]egal contentions, like the currency, depreciate through over-issue,'' \emph{id.} at 752.

\paragraph{Work whose cost exceeds its value.} For this reason, I considered defective LAB's rubric that requires models to argue that an expert's testimony should be excluded under \emph{Daubert} because the expert's report lists five categories of damage that sum to \$14,211,000 and then claims total damages of \$14,200,000. The task environment characterizes this as an ``arithmetic error'' and tries to suggest it is evidence that the expert's work cannot be trusted. But it's just rounding, and while rounding in an expert report is slightly unorthodox, it does not come close to the kind of fundamental reliability defect that a movant would need to show to get a ruling that the expert's opinion is completely inadmissible. The rounding is in movant's favor (the expert has rounded down the amount of damages the defendant might have to pay), and if the precise dollar amount were actually material for some reason, the non-rounded total could simply be clarified in a non-argumentative one-line footnote. It could be fine to train a model to flag this issue briefly in a user message or internal memo, but training the model to raise points like this as a basis for excluding testimony under \emph{Daubert} weakens the model's judgment.

A similar issue applies to a criterion that requires that the model recommend a motion to dismiss an unjust-enrichment count (LAB item 4) pleaded in the alternative to a contract claim that the rubric considers strong. In a situation where the defendant faces at least one clearly strong claim that is expected to survive a motion to dismiss no matter what, a motion becomes less attractive unless it will somehow reduce the financial exposure/settlement value (by taking higher-value claims off the table) or narrow the scope of discovery (by removing certain factual issues from the litigation). Neither appears to be the case from this environment, so rather than reflexively recommending another motion simply because the rules allow it, a better memo to the client would explain that the benefit of a motion is limited and may not be worth the costs and fees involved (and the tactical downside of spending credibility with the judge on weak arguments that do not advance the client's strategic objectives).

LegalReasonBench presented a similar tradeoff in the land-use matter, requiring that the county's memo recommend both an appeal and a remand to the hearing examiner. A protective appeal is reasonable and probably the default most lawyers would recommend, but in the context of an internal memo, the better answer would be to explain to the client that, while there are some advantages to at least filing a protective appeal, curing the defects on remand may be faster and more effective---and the models are trained here into the simpler answer. In the real case, the county did both, but its appellate filing explicitly acknowledged that the remand might end up mooting the appeal (items 3 and 4). While the advice those lawyers gave to their client is not public, the filings suggest they likely at least did discuss the limited value of the appeal here.

\section{Implications for evaluation and training}
\label{sec:implications}

The key result of this audit is that a meaningful minority of the audited rubrics reward worse lawyering---for example, by penalizing models that properly rebut an adversary's arguments, or by rewarding models that miscite or misapply governing case law. This has three main implications. First, these two benchmarks, as released, may not reliably separate the models at the top of their leaderboards. The two benchmarks audited here were chosen precisely because they are industry-leading and likely among the best available metrics for long-horizon agentic legal tasks, but criteria that AI auditors flagged as defective accounted for a disproportionate share of the criterion failures in the model runs I did, and the two models failed the defective criteria in my sample at different rates (though not by a statistically significant margin), which at least raises questions about the stability of the ranking. Second, a large share of the specific defects identified are easily fixable. This audit demonstrated that AI-led auditing techniques can be effective at identifying erroneous criteria, and \pending{Harvey has already made changes to LAB intended to address findings from this audit}. Third, beyond fixing specific identified defects, this audit illustrates the enormous difficulty of reducing legal professional judgments to binary criteria. Section~\ref{sec:scores} considers what the scores can establish, Section~\ref{sec:training} the rewards that favor worse lawyering, and Section~\ref{sec:improve} what the audit suggests might be effective methods for improving reward construction in the legal domain.

\subsection{Legal benchmark rankings may be unreliable}
\label{sec:scores}

The audit raises questions about how reliably current legal benchmarks can distinguish between frontier models, and how accurately they can depict capabilities. Table~\ref{tab:defects-and-spreads} shows that the defect rate found in this audit for litigation tasks is larger than the scoring gaps between the frontier models on these benchmarks (although the two panels measure different populations and a defect rate does not translate cleanly into a score error, as explained in the table notes). In the case of LegalReasonBench, even the conservative lower-bound estimate of the defect rate in litigation tasks (25.1\%) is greater than the percentage point difference in scores between the highest and lowest rated model (18.89 percentage points). Harvey's LAB has been used on a much larger set of models, including some older models released in 2025, so the gap between the highest and lowest score on LAB is 81.59 percentage points, much larger than the lower-bound estimated defect rate of 13.4\%. But as an illustration, GPT-5.6 Luna (ranked 17th) had a criteria pass score that was 7.58 percentage points less than the highest-ranked model on Harvey's LAB, and the four highest-ranked models are within 1.93 points of one another, so the difference between the top-scoring models and recently released small models is smaller than the defect rate observed in litigation tasks.

That means that, if even a portion of the defects end up penalizing one model more than another, correcting them could conceivably change the rankings---and based on this audit, I could not rule out the possibility that much of the gap between frontier models could be explained by differences in how those models are affected by defective rubrics.

There is at least some indication that the defects could differentially impact different models. In the audit, GPT-6 Luna failed \labLunaDefectiveFailPct\% of the judge grades on the defective LAB criteria, while Opus failed \labOpusDefectiveFailPct\%. That difference was not statistically significant ($p = \labPairedP$, exact paired permutation test over the \labDefective{} criteria, averaging the two judges per criterion). LegalReasonBench's forum non conveniens task illustrates how inaccurate criteria can differentially affect models. Opus scored 53.2\% while Luna earned only 1.0\% (Appendix~\ref{app:native}). The models' scores on these two runs were separated by 105 of the 201 available rubric points, 35 of which came from twelve criteria that require citations to the affidavits discussed in Section~\ref{sec:evidence}: Opus drafted the affidavits and cited them, and Luna did not. (The remaining 70 points came from criteria that were not in my sample or that I did not judge defective, mostly on the first assignment, where the judges graded Opus's memorandum as covering far more of the rubric than Luna's.)

These issues also mean that benchmarks could understate model capabilities somewhat. The AI auditors' flags illustrate the impact. Although I reviewed only a sample of the criteria that AI auditors flagged as defective, across the full population of litigation criteria, criteria that AI auditors flagged as defective or potentially defective were graded as failures under LAB's scoring \labFlaggedGradeFailPct\% of the time (\labFlaggedGradeFails{} of \labFlaggedGrades{} judge grades across the two model runs), compared with \labUnflaggedGradeFailPct\% for criteria that no AI auditor flagged as potentially defective (\labUnflaggedGradeFails{} of \labUnflaggedGrades{}). The pattern holds for each model separately (Luna \labLunaFlaggedGradeFailPct\% versus \labLunaUnflaggedGradeFailPct\%; Opus \labOpusFlaggedGradeFailPct\% versus \labOpusUnflaggedGradeFailPct\%). The criteria that AI auditors flagged were only \labFlaggedPct\% of the population but accounted for \labFlaggedFailSharePct\% of all failed grades. While I have not validated the assertion of AI auditors that those other criteria are defective, the results suggest that a meaningful share of failures in real-world grades could result from defective criteria, rather than deficiencies in the models being evaluated.

That said, there are limits to what this kind of audit can show: the audit was limited to the publicly available tasks, and both Harvey and micro1 use a private held-out set, which was not audited and which could differ in material respects from the audited population. \pending{Harvey has told me that outside expert review was more extensive on the held-out set (Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}), so the defect rate there may be lower.} Harvey continuously updates its benchmark and has made several updates since this audit \pending{(including changes intended to address the audit findings in particular)}, so this audit does not demonstrate the defect rate, if any, in the revised, operative v1.1 benchmark. And the audit was limited to litigation, which is one of LAB's 24 practice areas; the defect rate in non-litigation tasks could be higher or lower than the rate estimated for litigation tasks, and the effect of litigation defects on an all-practice-area leaderboard is diluted accordingly.

Nevertheless, the audit results suggest that legal benchmarks should be considered one signal and piece of evidence about the quality of models' legal AI work, not a definitive ranking as they are sometimes treated. The audit points to the need to consider a variety of data sources, not just one set of rubrics, given the challenges inherent to reducing legal judgment to rubrics.

\subsection{Rubrics sometimes end up rewarding lower-quality lawyering}
\label{sec:training}

The audit found several places where the benchmarks unintentionally reward demonstrably worse lawyering, including:
\begin{itemize}
\item penalizing models that explained an opponent's argument in order to show why it was wrong (Section~\ref{sec:stating}; LRB items 13, 16, and 22);
\item rewarding models for misstating propositions of law or fact (Section~\ref{sec:errors}; for example, LAB item 23 and LRB items 5, 10, and 21);
\item rewarding models for asserting information that cannot be extracted from the environment (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}; LRB items 14, 15, and 17 through 20, and the nonexistent conflicts in LAB item 20 and LRB item 9).
\end{itemize}
These signals show that at least some rubrics can be counterproductive. Since versions of Harvey's LAB have been used for training, and micro1 sells training environments of the same construction, this raises the question whether these environments could reinforce undesirable behaviors in some cases \citep{harveyTenet,appliedCompute,micro1LRB}. These defects are not necessarily noise that simply averages out across a large number of tasks; in some cases, the defects appear to constitute a consistent signal that creates undesirable incentives around a specific behavior (LegalReasonBench's penalties for mentioning the opponent's argument are one clear example of an inaccurate signal that several different task environments apply).

To be clear, the point should not be taken too far: the vast majority of criteria are likely to be sound, and thus are likely providing valuable training signal. (AI auditors did not raise any issue with the vast majority of rubrics.) Models could still learn valuable skills from legal environments, even if some individual criteria have defects, if the positive training signal from the correct criteria outweighs the negative signal from defective ones. Litigation is also an especially challenging subdomain, because the environment is inherently adversarial and models need to approach evidence with due skepticism, distinguishing between reliable and unreliable information from the surrounding context.

Nevertheless, the audit's results suggest that it could be valuable to try to isolate the impact that legal-domain-specific training environments have on model quality, using benchmarks or techniques that have a meaningfully different construction than the training environment. Models are clearly getting better at legal tasks over time, but on the current publicly available evidence, it is impossible to determine how much of that gain comes from transfer from other task environments: teaching the models to do better work in easier-to-verify domains like math and coding could yield skills that improve the models' ability to handle legal tasks as well.

Exacerbating the difficulty of isolating the effect of legal-domain training is that many providers use one set of benchmark tasks as training signal, and then evaluate the model's improvement based largely on its performance on a held-out set from the same benchmark family. Examples are collected in Table~\ref{tab:same-benchmark}. This kind of evidence proves that legal-domain training changes the models' outputs in ways that generalize beyond the specific tasks that they were trained on. But this does not address the underlying problem of construct validity. The rubrics ultimately are only a proxy for gains in legal capabilities. Evaluating models only on a held-out set from the same family of tasks the models were trained on does not establish that there were real gains in underlying legal ability; just as a teacher might improve students' scores a few points by teaching them ``tricks'' to do well on a standardized test, training on a particular task family could make models better at scoring well on tasks from that specific family, without meaningfully improving the underlying legal capabilities. Several authors have previously noted the danger of relying on essentially the same rubrics as proof that a given training signal is useful \citep{geirhosShortcut,gaoOveropt,skalseRewardHacking,mahmoudRubricHacking,yangRubricDropout}. And the phenomenon has been observed in legal RL specifically; LEGIT, which scores legal reasoning against issue trees built from court judgments, found that reinforcement learning raised a model's overall score on the benchmark's test set while reducing the share of legal issues the model addressed. The rubric penalized a wrong answer more heavily than an omission, so the model learned to address straightforward issues and skip more difficult ones to avoid losing points \citep{legit}. The benchmark's own score went up even though the output arguably was worse in that the model dodged difficult questions.

We ideally would want to see extrinsic evidence, from lawyer preference scores or perhaps a range of legal benchmarks that are constructed in a completely different way and that involve meaningfully different challenges, to be confident that the training improved underlying legal capabilities and not merely a proxy. In the legal domain, such evidence is currently scarce because there are so few rigorous benchmarks that challenge the models at the relevant frontier.

There is some reason to be optimistic that legal training environments, despite the challenges, are in fact improving model capabilities. Harvey has reported that its legal post-training work has resulted in capability gains that are observable in separately developed benchmarks (although the benchmarks were created in a similar way to LAB, with expert-annotated rubrics) \citep{harveyTenet}. Ultimately, it may be wise to look at a variety of signals. For example, Thomson Reuters reported practitioner preference scores in addition to results from rubric-based benchmarks as part of its model evaluation \citep{thomson}. Evidence that post-training actually improved outputs in a way that lawyers consider improvements could be much stronger evidence that validates that a given set of rubrics is a good proxy for underlying capability gains. 


\subsection{Possible paths for improving RL legal environments}
\label{sec:improve}
\label{sec:environments}

Although this audit found that some of the leading benchmarks have defects, rubric-based tasks and environments remain useful for several reasons. Rubrics are easy to audit and understand. This audit was possible precisely because Harvey and micro1 made a selection of their rubrics available for public inspection, and invited practitioners to audit them and point out ways they can be improved. The same approach would not work with preference comparison techniques because it may be difficult to understand why a given reviewer preferred one response over another (and such approaches have well-known biases, such as a tendency to sycophancy \citep{sycophancy}). Rubrics can also be applied efficiently at scale: once created, rubrics can be run autonomously, with agents completing tasks and evaluating the results against rubrics, while approaches that rely on human intervention introduce bottlenecks and delays. And rubrics are the natural tool for open-ended work product, where no answer key exists; tasks with verifiable answers, such as the classification and extraction tasks in LegalBench, do not need them \citep{legalbench}. Accordingly, while rubrics have drawbacks and can encode errors, they clearly are an important component of benchmarking and training in the legal domain.

This audit suggests that there may be approaches that could improve rubric design and reward signals in evaluation and training data for legal work.

\subsubsection{Quality control}

\paragraph{Audit environments before training on them.} Using frontier models to audit tasks and environments before using them as training signal could significantly improve quality. In this audit, instances where two distinct models (GPT-6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5) both found a defect in an environment were overwhelmingly real defects: \labBothDefective{} of \labBothFlagged{} such criteria in the LAB sample and \lrbBothDefective{} of \lrbBothFlagged{} in LegalReasonBench's. An automated citation check of the environment documents is cheaper still; the LAB check established at least 303 real citation errors and flagged 76 citations to authorities that could not be found (Appendix~\ref{app:citecheck}). Environment buyers may wish to have models adversarially inspect the environment and provided rubrics for defects, and to ask sellers to improve environments if the defect rate is unusually high.

\paragraph{Review rubrics against real model runs.} Rubric authors should review how the rubrics apply in practice and confirm how well they are being applied by judges. For example, the recurring issue in LegalReasonBench where models were punished for rebutting an opponent's argument (Section~\ref{sec:stating}) likely could have been caught by reviewing the scores from a real frontier model run, as I did in this audit. While it appears to be the case that, in many instances, one company (such as a data vendor) will create rubrics and a different one (a lab) actually uses them for training, the quality of these training signals could be improved significantly if one of the organizations involved in the process takes on the responsibility of validating rubrics end to end to ensure that their practical application provides the intended training signal. Otherwise, misspecifications can go undetected.

\subsubsection{Rubric design}

\paragraph{Rubrics can be highly effective at testing bright-line rules, instead of open-ended standards.} Law professors often speak of the difference between bright-line rules and open-ended standards. The canonical example could be the difference between a posted speed limit of 55 miles per hour, versus a law that prohibits driving at ``excessive speed'' \citep{kaplow}. Rubrics in principle should be an excellent way of encoding bright-line rules, and there may be even more opportunities to develop rubrics that specifically exhaust the universe of bright-line rules that apply to a problem. For example, the motion to dismiss task environments I reviewed did not contain any overarching rubrics that penalize models for citing evidence and facts outside the four corners of the complaint (or documents incorporated by reference therein or subject to judicial notice). But unless a model explicitly argues that the motion should be framed as a motion for summary judgment under Rule 12(d), it is almost always wrong to cite evidence that is not referenced in the complaint or subject to judicial notice. Rubrics that try to enforce all of the bright-line rules could improve the quality of legal environments. Similarly, rubrics can be used to penalize miscitations or misrepresentations of a cited source, as that is always unacceptable in effective advocacy, no matter how fluent or persuasive the document otherwise seems.

\paragraph{Rewarding good judgment is harder, but rubrics may be an effective way to enforce the permissible range of professional judgment.} Many questions fall within a range of acceptable professional judgment, and reasonable lawyers can disagree about which is best; selecting among them is the heart of advocacy \citep[\S~12]{scaliaGarner} and the hard part of evaluating open-ended legal answers \citep{naglSoK}. The misspecified requirements and the errors of professional judgment (Sections~\ref{sec:stating} and \ref{sec:strategy}), and most of the unsafe-drafting rewards (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}), are standard-like: the rubric chose one answer where the law and good practice permit a range. Ideally, rubrics on questions of judgment or standards would be designed to track something like the abuse of discretion standard the appellate courts apply when reviewing a decision committed to the trial court's discretion: enforce hard constraints first, but allow any permissible approach within the accepted boundaries of discretion, failing those that exceed those boundaries (an ``abuse of discretion,'' in judicial parlance). Many of the rubrics I reviewed suggested that rubric authors were trying to capture a few seemingly obvious aspects of what a good legal output might entail in response to a given task, but often these involved something like a ``local maximum'' that occurred to the rubric author, not necessarily the optimal output overall. It may be more effective to conceive of strict rubrics like the ones Harvey and micro1 wrote as enforcing the outer limits on legal output---and using techniques like comparing briefs to decide which outputs are better amongst a category that complies with the basic requirements. Indeed, JudgmentBench reported that on 30 legal tasks, pairwise comparisons recovered a constructed quality ordering substantially better than rubric scores \citep{judgmentBench}, and preference optimization can train on such comparisons \citep{dpo}. 

\paragraph{General rubrics for all litigation documents.} It may be desirable to develop a taxonomy of general rubrics that are not specific to a particular task environment, in addition to the rubrics that are more specific to a particular environment. One noteworthy aspect of LAB and LegalReasonBench was that the rubrics were almost all customized to a specific task and environment, so the rubrics tended to test case-specific knowledge. For example, in a random sample of 120 LAB litigation criteria that I had an AI agent classify, 104 were task-specific, and 178 of LegalReasonBench's 179 litigation criteria were task-specific \citep{rubricGenerality}. No criterion in either benchmark mentions hallucination, and the one LAB criterion that addresses citation authenticity asks for ``at least three case law authorities (whether real or plausibly cited)'' (draft-motion-for-preliminary-injunction, C-051). (LAB v1.1, which I did not audit, adds hallucination checks.) Although the approach of focusing on task-specific criteria certainly makes sense, it was noteworthy to me that few of the rubrics enforce general principles of writing and argumentation that apply to a variety of different kinds of legal tasks. In principle, there could be value in carefully designing some overarching criteria that apply to, say, all litigation documents (e.g., a rubric that rewards citations for each contested factual or legal assertion made, and punishes any hallucinations or miscitations) and rubrics that apply to all briefs (e.g., a reward for sequencing the arguments roughly in order of their persuasive power and importance), in addition to task- or environment-specific rubrics (requirements grounded in the facts of a specific assignment).

\subsubsection{Environment design}

\paragraph{Adapt real cases with care.} Environments grounded in real cases have advantages over purely AI-generated synthetic environments, but they must be adapted with great care, and AI can plainly contribute at multiple phases of the environment construction process. My impression from the audit was that the LRB environments, which were often grounded in real-world cases, were more realistic and more likely to train models on what a real litigation would look like. The audit was not designed to compare the two benchmarks' defect rates, however, and the real-case grounding introduced a failure mode of its own: the rubrics sometimes supplied the answer that would be accurate in the real case, but the task environment had been modified in a way that changed the answer (e.g., in one real-world case, there was a fact dispute over who opened fire first, and a rubric wanted the model to say that, but the task environment had been modified from the real-world case to specify who fired first). When modifying task environments that have a real-world basis, environment authors need to take great care to consider how the modifications could change the task or its answers. Even simply ``updating'' the dates in a task by a few months could completely change the outcome because a new statute may have gone into effect or an old one been repealed with an effective date between the old and the new dates. It may be desirable to modify the task environment and the answers from the real-world case, so that a model cannot simply score well by repeating the answer to a case it learned in pre-training. But careful auditing is then required to ensure that the resulting task environment is accurate.

\paragraph{Other task formats.} The environments I reviewed focused largely on drafting tasks, but there are a range of other tasks that could be useful. For example, it might be useful to give a model a brief where an important argument was ablated and have it identify and add the missing piece---or give it a longer version of the brief and test its ability to cut without losing important details from the original. In the context of training as opposed to evaluation, there may also be ways to combine reward signals from different sources. For example, LegalForecastBench asks models to predict a judicial disposition from the record the judge received \citep{lfbpaper}. With a fixed record and opening brief, models might be tested on the ability to generate candidate response briefs. After disqualifying briefs with fabrications or ethical violations (or concessions that compromise the client's interests in other matters), one could offer reward for the briefs that seem likely to maximize the chance of a favorable disposition, as scored by a well-calibrated prediction model. Such an approach would need to be tested to ascertain whether the forecasting model responds to real improvements in advocacy and to ensure that the training does not merely exploit the forecaster \citep{gaoOveropt}.

These suggestions are all indicative. The audit does not establish that they will work better than existing approaches, but their common purpose is to reward models for making legal work better, rather than for reproducing the arguments a rubric author expected to see.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

There are several limits of this audit:

\begin{itemize}
\item I am the sole reviewer, as the author. Future audits like this could be made more rigorous by having multiple lawyers participate in the audit.
\item This audit was limited to the publicly released litigation tasks for LAB and LegalReasonBench. The audit does not cover the held-out private data sets, or practice areas other than litigation.
\item The samples I reviewed were drawn only from criteria that AI auditors flagged, so the confidence bounds do not establish the defect rate, if any, in the universe of criteria that the AI auditors did not identify as potentially problematic.
\item The sampling method used for LAB and LegalReasonBench was slightly different. The two audits are meant to be independent, not a controlled comparison of the two.
\item The confidence interval boundaries cover only sampling error, not errors in my verdicts.
\item Several sampled criteria share one underlying mistake or issue, so the numerical results could shift materially if the handling of a given issue were changed.
\item My review was conducted with the AI auditors' findings, and the results of test model runs, in front of me. Although I endeavored to analyze the rubrics independently and often reached my own conclusions, I could have been influenced by the AI models' arguments.
\item The test benchmark runs used were one execution per model, with different reasoning settings, so these were intended as a diagnostic, not a comparison. 
\item \pending{Harvey and micro1 commented on earlier drafts of this work. I believe their comments improved the paper, and I did not change any verdicts after sharing a draft with these companies for feedback.}
\end{itemize}

\section{A synthetic task environment: the motion-to-dismiss case study}
\label{app:mtd}

In addition to auditing the accuracy of all \labCriteria{} rubrics, I did a deep-dive review of one substantive LAB litigation task. This review highlighted a different but equally important issue: the use of synthetic or AI-generated data may result in task environments that are unrealistic in a variety of subtle ways that may not be obvious to nonexperts but that are striking and clear to practicing attorneys. The task \texttt{analyze-counterparty-motion-to-dismiss} asks AI agents to prepare a list of issues for the opposition brief to a motion to dismiss, on a fictitious software service dispute.

I observed several ways in which the synthetically generated task environment seemed unrealistic and could potentially train models on a distorted view of what genuine practice looks like \citep{labtask}.

\paragraph{Planted issues.} A number of the legal disputes in the task environment are obviously ``planted'' in a way that feels artificial and ultimately presents issues in a way that is meaningfully different from how real-world legal disputes manifest. For example, the contract the parties are suing over has a forum selection clause that requires any lawsuit to be filed in Travis County, Texas. The plaintiff literally quotes this entire provision in the complaint but nevertheless files suit in the Northern District of Georgia and offers no explanation for why they believe the forum selection clause they quoted does not apply or otherwise permits the suit in Georgia. It of course happens regularly that a lazy or incompetent litigant files suit in the wrong forum; a competent lawyer potentially could decide to ignore the clause and hope that their adversary overlooks the issue. But in both of those scenarios, the forum selection clause would not be explicitly flagged in the complaint as it is here; a real version of this legal task would require the lawyer to dig through a number of documents and realize that buried in one of them is a forum selection clause that allows the lawyer to have the case moved to Texas.

This is one example, but a significant number of the issues in these environments were similarly planted in a way that's artificial and unlike how disputes around these issues arise in real cases. Environments like this can distort an evaluation of a model's true capabilities, since simply looking for the obviously planted issues will satisfy the benchmark without testing whether the model would be able to identify this issue from more realistic source material akin to what it will see in real cases.

\paragraph{Environment documents violate court rules.} Many of the documents in these environments do not resemble real lawyer work product. The environment provides counterparty litigation documents in docx format, and the native files from the git repository often cannot be opened, although agents and various CLI tools can recover the document text. In real litigation, lawyers rarely share their docx files with their opponent; the opponent's memorandum of law is ordinarily filed with the court as a PDF. The motion papers also lack a proper caption, include a stale table of contents, contain markdown formatting artifacts that render in Word as junk characters even in files that can be opened normally, lack citations for propositions that generally require citations, and are single spaced rather than double spaced in violation of the applicable court rules.

The motion that the agent is being asked to respond to is so far below professional norms that you would want any analysis of the motion to include consideration of whether the judge would strike the motion or require it to be refiled in compliance with the rules. That is not included in the rubric for this task, presumably because the quality control issues appear to be unintentional, not part of the test for the agent. But it is a genuine gap: if a document like this were filed in real litigation, the quality of the work product could itself become a substantive issue in the litigation and thus would need to be flagged given the goal motivating the all-pass rule, which was to ensure that the AI does not overlook any important issues \citep[section ``Evaluating a task'']{harveyLAB}.

There are valid reasons to prefer rubric-based reinforcement learning approaches. For example, while a benchmark like LegalForecastBench (which asks models to predict how federal judges ruled on motions to dismiss) can test a model's legal reasoning and analytical ability over a narrow task, it does not test the model's ability to generate new work product, which is an important consideration for a company like Harvey that is developing legal agents to prepare and revise different kinds of legal work product. But these task environments contain departures from practice that could weaken their value for training or evaluation. The model is in effect being tested on its ability to recognize a distinct environment, very unlike what it will encounter in production, and adhere to a stylized set of norms and rules that were artificially constructed in that environment but do not comport with the real norms or rules that govern real-world tasks. While creating LegalForecastBench illustrated that there are real challenges in working with real-world litigation data as well, real records contain contextual signals that these environments did not consistently preserve.

In order to validate the AI findings and determine how many of the AI-flagged rubrics were genuinely defective, I reviewed a simple random sample of \labSample{} of the flagged rubrics, drawn with a fixed seed from \labSampleTasks{} tasks. I committed the protocol for that review before it began \citep{labreview}. I marked a rubric as defective when it could produce an inaccurate grade, meaning: either the rubric could fail a response that a thoughtful litigator would consider appropriate, or the rubric could pass a response that a thoughtful litigator would consider wrong on the issue the rubric is testing. A rubric that forces one of several permissible answers is therefore defective, because it would fail the others.

During the review, I decided to distinguish between defects in the rubrics themselves and defects in the task environment. The line between the two was based on whether the defect could change the grade a model is given on the task. For example, one rubric wanted the work product to contain a specific metric sourced from the task environment, but the AI auditors pointed out that the task environment actually contained two contradictory numbers for that particular metric. Accordingly, an AI agent that relied on one of the numbers (which was not the one the rubric was expecting) would be failed incorrectly because of the underlying defect in the task environment. I treated that as a defect in both the rubric and the task environment. By contrast, a different task environment contained a notice of an OSHA regulatory violation from a state-level regulatory agency in Ohio that does not actually have jurisdiction over the violations charged. The rubric wanted work product to simply flag the existence of the charge in the work product. I coded that as a defect in the task environment but not as a defect in the rubric (because an AI agent handed that task could, and likely still would, pass the rubric by simply mentioning the charged violation).

Of the \labSample{} criteria, I graded \labDefective{} rubrics defective in ways that affect grading, including \labObjective{} that I considered to be unambiguous objective errors in which the record, the law, the cited authority, or the arithmetic contradicts the rubric. \labArguable{} were arguable defects, and \labNotDefective{} were not defective. \labEnvItems{} of the \labSample{} come from task environments that contain defects (\labEnvTasks{} of the \labSampleTasks{} tasks). Only \labNoDefect{} had neither a defective nor an arguable criterion and came from an environment without a defect (Table~\ref{tab:labreview}).

Since the sample was a draw without replacement from the \labFlagged{} criteria that AI agents had flagged as defective, the one-sided 95\% lower confidence bounds are: at least \labDefectiveLB{} flagged criteria are defective in ways that affect grading (point estimate \labDefectivePoint{}; the bound is \labDefectiveShare\% of all criteria in the practice area); at least \labObjectiveLB{} are unambiguous objective errors (point estimate \labObjectivePoint{}); and at least \labEnvLB{} come from task environments that contain defects (point estimate \labEnvPoint{}). At least \labAnyLB{} involve a defective criterion, a defective environment, or both (point estimate \labAnyPoint{}). In the sample, \labBothDefective{} of the \labBothFlagged{} criteria flagged by both auditors were defective, against \labSingleDefective{} of the \labSingleFlagged{} flagged by only one.

I did not attempt to estimate defects in criteria that were not flagged by the AI agents; it is possible there could be some additional defects that the agents did not flag. These estimates count criteria/rubrics, not the individual mistakes that give rise to the defects. In some cases, the defect was specific to the particular rubric at issue, but in other cases, a single error in a task environment could make several criteria defective. After I prepared an initial set of verdicts, I asked AI models (GPT-6 Sol and Opus 5.5) to stress test my answers and to draft additional arguments both for and against each call and a summary of those they would change. I changed a handful of verdicts after reflecting on these points, and did a second pass through the full set of my verdicts to ensure that they applied the principles I adopted during the review consistently to each call.

The audit of rubrics revealed instances where the task environment, and sometimes grading rubrics, appeared to rely on AI-generated hallucinated case law citations. The citation checks that followed are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:citecheck}; several entries below cite their per-task results.

\noindent\textbf{My analysis.} The rubric wants the plaintiff's opposition brief (in response to defendants' forum non conveniens motion) to state that the plaintiff's ``response is supported by the affidavit of Liam Vance, a physician who provided care to Baby Davis at Cook County Hospital.'' But no such affidavit is provided in the environment, and the prompt does not explicitly instruct the model to create it. The prompt further says ``Do not rely on, reference, or infer any legal authority, factual information, or data beyond what is contained in these uploaded files. If a document is not uploaded, it does not exist for purposes of this analysis.'' While the prompt elsewhere says, ``Please include all affidavits and information you referenced from Assignment \#1,'' that suggests the model should ``include'' documents that were referenced in the environment, not generate new ones or assume that they will be created separately. Although the assignment 1 rubric rewards recommending that the plaintiff obtain affidavits from these physicians, and the assignment 2 prompt asks the model to include the affidavits referenced in assignment 1, I would still consider drafting a brief that assumes what third-party witnesses will sign to be hazardous and unreliable.

Drafting an opposition brief that cites to affidavits (without placeholders in the draft) is hazardous behavior to train models on in a legal setting. In a real run, Opus was rewarded for drafting affidavits and including in the brief citations to them without placeholders, while Luna did not create the affidavits or cite to them. If forced to choose, I would consider Luna's approach more correct in this instance, as a matter of professional judgment. Although Luna's approach suffered from its own flaws (including that it incorrectly said the record does not reflect the physicians' willingness to testify), I would prefer the approach of not citing affidavits that don't yet exist.

The contemplated affidavits are from nonparty physicians that the case team does not represent; while it is helpful that a lawyer on the case team spoke to these witnesses and obtained an understanding of their views that can be turned into a draft affidavit, ultimately a nonparty is entitled to decide exactly what it is willing to affirm in writing and a lawyer cannot simply assume that the witness will be willing to sign an affidavit based on the lawyer's understanding from an initial call. The normal approach in a situation like this would be to draft placeholder citations to an affidavit that make clear the language must be adjusted to conform to the witness's final testimony. Opus's draft affidavit includes one form for five physicians, and in practice, I would expect the physicians to object to some of what is included in the affidavit (and in fact getting all five to actually sign these affidavits in time would be a fairly significant undertaking, so I would not assume all five affidavits ultimately enter into the record as this assignment contemplates). The Opus work product creates a real risk that a human lawyer, through inadvertence or mistake, might file a brief that claims to be supported by affidavits that have not in fact been sworn, which is a serious ethical lapse.

Overall, the practical effect of this rubric seems to be to nudge the model in the direction of thinking it is OK to invent witness testimony that has not yet been approved by a witness. The issue could be avoided either by including an actual affidavit approved by the witness in the environment or by asking the model to merely include bracketed text/placeholders that are clearly labeled as draft so that the draft is clear and explicit that the affidavits are contemplated or expected, rather than already secured, as Opus's draft of the brief implies.

The biggest issue here is that the affidavit does not actually exist, so this is training models to hallucinate. But there are also reasons why this would flunk professional judgment.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The environment indicates that the physicians work at hospitals, which almost certainly have their own in-house counsel. There's no indication in the environment that a lawyer has been consulted about the plan to inject the doctors' testimony into the forum issue. Once consulted, he may advise the physicians not to take a position on the forum issue even if their inclination was to be helpful.
\item A witness's agreement on a telephone call that they're willing to testify or be helpful is one thing, but actually getting them to sign an affidavit with the specific language you want is quite another. The affidavit needs to be supported by the witness's own personal knowledge, and the factual basis for each witness to assert inconvenience is unclear from this environment; only a conclusory assertion of inconvenience is made. On such a record, counsel may need to gather additional information to assess whether the assertion is well grounded and to explain, for each doctor, the claimed inconvenience.
\item There are also strategic considerations that may make it unwise to submit five identical affidavits, as the task environment proposes, since getting involved in this dispute could impact the credibility of witnesses who will be needed for more important points later. Submitting five identical affidavits, as the rubric contemplates, will make it obvious that plaintiff's counsel recruited the doctors, drafted affidavits for them to sign, and coordinated with them on a disputed issue in the case. While not impermissible, that signals to the court that the doctors are effectively partisans supporting the plaintiff, which sacrifices some of their credibility when it comes to the merits of the case. In this spot, most lawyers would prefer to preserve the doctors' credibility by leaving them out of minor procedural disputes and presenting them as neutral experts when it comes to the merits. One would not want to reveal at this early stage that witnesses the court might otherwise view as neutral experts are in fact willing to sign whatever you put in front of them. A better strategy would be to leave them out of the minor procedural fight and make their only testimony in the case the merits testimony they will offer.
\item It is particularly strange to do so many affidavits here because it's somewhat incredible to suggest that it's truly ``inconvenient'' under the applicable standards for doctors to attend a case in adjoining jurisdictions. The defendant's own claim of inconvenience is not well supported and is the sort of conclusory assertion that the court can easily reject. So the better strategy for plaintiff in this spot is simply to point out that it's nonsense for defendant's doctor to argue that it's some grave inconvenience to drive an hour from DuPage County to Chicago for trial testimony, which is likely to be the extent of the doctor's in-person appearances. It's much easier to attack defendants' claim as bogus, rather than respond by putting in an equally bogus claim that the doctors on your side could not possibly drive an hour in the other direction. The more principled position is that neither jurisdiction can truly be inconvenient to the other side since they're only an hour's drive apart, which does not come close to proving \emph{forum non conveniens}. It's defendants' burden to prove inconvenience. Although not absolute given that the plaintiff does not live in the county, the presumption is still that the case will be heard in plaintiff's forum.
\item Even if the plaintiff really wishes to make this ``inconvenience'' argument for some reason, five affidavits is overkill. The point could be made adequately by picking one or two doctors who have the strongest claim to genuine inconvenience (or perhaps the doctor whose testimony is less important at the merits stage) and submitting their affidavits. In the context of a case this size, there's no need for five affidavits.
\end{enumerate}

This issue is distinct from items 7 and 8, where my view was that it was appropriate for a model to refer in a brief to expert analysis that was referred to in the environment but not supplied. In that case, I had clear indications that the expert analysis described really existed; the full details were simply not provided in the model prompt, so it is important to flag the existence of that evidence apparently already present in the record. Here, the environment makes clear that, for purposes of this assignment, the affidavits do not exist, yet the rubric rewards a model for creating a draft that could easily be filed mistakenly with the false assertion that these doctors have provided affidavits to support their views (which may or may not ultimately come to pass).

\noindent\textbf{My analysis.} The rubric rewards models for a legal error. The case involves federal charges against a gang member who shot at a rival gang member after following him from a mall. The defendant pled guilty to state charges of conspiracy to commit a forcible felony in connection with the incident. The rubric wants the prosecution's brief to argue that the commission of conspiracy to commit a forcible felony precludes the justification (self-defense) defense, under Iowa Code § 704.6.

But that statute only applies when the defendant commits a different forcible felony than the one he is alleging is justified. The paradigmatic case would be a robber who waves a hammer at a store clerk to take the cash in the register, intending only to frighten the clerk, and who cannot claim self-defense if the clerk responds by shooting at him (robbery is a forcible felony under the supplied Iowa Code § 702.11(1)). Iowa courts apply the bar in that setting. \emph{See State v. Roby}, No. 22-1017, slip op. at 8 (Iowa Ct. App. Sept. 27, 2023) (defendant who brought a gun to an apartment and shot an occupant who drew his own weapon: “A justification defense was unavailable to Roby because he was participating in the forcible felony of robbery. See Iowa Code § 704.6(1).”).

Here, the defendant is arguing that the shooting itself was an act of self-defense; there is no contention that he was committing a different forcible felony prior to the shooting that would negate his ability to claim self-defense for the shooting itself. (Although I did not find an Iowa case expressly holding that the forcible felony must be independent of the act claimed to be justified, judges in at least one other state with a similar statute have expressly held this. \emph{See Martinez v. State}, 981 So. 2d 449 (Fla. 2008) (holding that for the forcible-felony bar to apply, ``there must be an independent forcible felony other than the one which the defendant claims he or she committed in self-defense''). Obviously, the law could not be to the contrary because, if it were, the exception would swallow the rule; self-defense would never be available, because absent the defense, the use of force would almost always constitute a forcible felony.)

This environment is based on a real case, and in the real case, neither the Eighth Circuit nor the government's brief opposing certiorari relied on the argument (that the rubric requires) that the conspiracy to commit a forcible felony precludes the justification defense. Instead, the government argued that the defendant as a felon in possession had a duty to retreat safely and could have done so instead of shooting as he did. The evidence indicates this was a scenario where two rival gangs were actively seeking opportunities to shoot members of the other, not that an innocent person was attacked and then defended themselves.

\noindent\textbf{My analysis.} This benchmark does two assignments, sequentially, in separate model sessions that are in the same sandbox. In this task, assignment 2 was to build on the assignment 1 output by adding discussion of an additional case. The intent is for models to output response1.md in response to assignment 1, and to output response2.md in the same sandbox for assignment 2.

The model is told, ``Build on [the assignment 1] analysis as the prompt directs,'' but not clearly told that it is expected to output a full revised version of the brief (as opposed to supplemental or revised sections that can be inserted into a revised brief). Since assignment 2 is framed as a mere supplementation task, the model might expect that it should write only the new material.

However, five of the assignment 1 rubrics are repeated on the assignment 2 output---so a model is penalized heavily if it interprets the instructions as requesting simply an update to the specific section to which the new authority is relevant.
