\begin{abstract}
Legal AI benchmarks now double as training environments. Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB) and micro1's LegalReasonBench grade AI-drafted legal work against rubrics written by lawyers, and rubrics of the same kind are used as reinforcement-learning rewards. I audited the litigation portions of both public releases. AI auditors screened every criterion (\labCriteria{} in LAB's \labTasks{} litigation tasks; \lrbCriteria{} in LegalReasonBench's \lrbAssignments{} litigation assignments), and I reviewed a random sample of \labSample{} flagged criteria from each under sampling protocols committed before the draws. In LAB, \labDefective{} of \labSample{} sampled criteria were defective, \labObjective{} of them objective errors in which the record, the law, or the arithmetic contradicts the rubric. In LegalReasonBench, \lrbDefective{} of \lrbSample{} were defective, \lrbObjective{} objective. Within the flagged sets, at least \labDefectiveLB{} LAB criteria (\labDefectiveShare\% of all litigation criteria) and at least \lrbDefectiveLB{} LegalReasonBench criteria (\lrbDefectiveShareLB\%) are defective, each at 95\% confidence.

The defective criteria contradict their sources, penalize accurate statements because they ignore the statement's function, credit representations the record does not support, or make one lawyer's choice on a debatable question the only acceptable answer. Consistent grading cures none of this: three judges can agree that a brief triggers a penalty while the penalty itself is wrong. These failures matter because legal competence includes selection. Recognizing an argument does not establish that it belongs in the deliverable. The audit does not measure the effect of training on these criteria. It shows why gains in rubric scores need independent validation before they can be read as gains in professional judgment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Extraordinary gains in the quality of frontier models' legal capabilities over the past 2--3 years have saturated many older legal benchmarks, which tested models on the knowledge of well-settled propositions of law. This extraordinary growth in legal capabilities has led recently to a wave of new benchmarks (which often also serve as training environments) that aim to push frontier models' legal capabilities further. The leading example today is Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB), which evaluates AI agents on a range of legal work product tasks, using LLM judges to score performance against human-annotated rubrics. Harvey has trained its own legal AI models using environments that share LAB's structure, and Applied Compute has used LAB as a reinforcement learning reward \citep{harveyLAB,harveyTenet,appliedCompute}. More recently, micro1, a data vendor that sells training environments to AI labs, built LegalReasonBench the same way \citep{micro1LRB}.

The objective of these benchmarks, and their related training environments, is to create reward signals for evaluating or training models on high-level legal tasks. The expert-written rubrics aim to reduce at least a major portion of a lawyer's professional judgment to a list of criteria that can help judge models distinguish good from bad legal work product. Recognizing that this process is imperfect, both micro1 and Harvey open-sourced a portion of their benchmark tasks; Harvey expressly invited practicing lawyers to audit the criteria their benchmarks use and to offer feedback on how these benchmarks can be improved \citep{harveyLAB}. Taking up that invitation, I audited the open-source litigation tasks from both benchmarks in order to understand how effectively the AI training techniques used at the frontier teach legal models legal judgment. I am a litigator with sixteen years of practice at top 10 law firms, with a broad generalist litigation practice that has given me exposure to everything from criminal trials and mass torts to securities class actions and bankruptcy; as such I audited the litigation tasks. After reviewing an initial sample, I gave AI auditors instructions on how to assess every criterion in LAB's \labTasks{} publicly available litigation tasks and in the \lrbAssignments{} litigation assignments of LegalReasonBench's public sample. The AI auditors flagged criteria they believed were defective or arguably defective under the standards I had provided. I then reviewed a simple random sample of \labSample{} AI-flagged criteria from each benchmark, reading the task instructions, the supplied record, the governing law, and how each benchmark's own judges had graded two frontier models on the same criterion.

The results showed that improving legal capabilities further, from the already high base that frontier models have achieved, has become extraordinarily difficult. Both benchmarks, even though they were developed by industry leaders applying cutting-edge reinforcement-learning techniques, had a significant defect rate, including a mix of legal or factual errors, or rubrics that trained models to take steps that I considered to be unreasonable professional judgments. The audit established, with 95\% confidence, that at least \labDefectiveShare\% of LAB's litigation criteria and at least \lrbDefectiveShareLB\% of LegalReasonBench's are defective. To be clear, this audit does not suggest that the benchmarks are flawed relative to industry standards, and \draft{Harvey's research team welcomed the feedback and told me they would use it to improve future versions of the benchmark}. Rather, the audit illustrates the challenges of translating lawyer judgment into machine-readable rubrics at scale and raises the question of how and whether AI researchers can be confident that measured gains in legal benchmarks correspond to meaningful improvements in legal capabilities.

One of LegalReasonBench's litigation assignments illustrates the issue. In the task environments, AI models are told they represent a plaintiff who has filed a medical malpractice case in Cook County (Chicago) and need to draft a brief opposing the defendants' motion to transfer venue to a different county where the defendants reside. The defendants' brief argues, as one reason for the transfer, that the Cook County courts have only a 60.5\% clearance rate, but the report from which this statistic is taken is included in the environment and makes clear that the defendants' argument is misleading. It conflates criminal and other cases with the clearance rate for civil matters, which is the relevant rate for a medical malpractice case and is much higher. The expert-written rubric indicates that the model drafting the plaintiff's brief should be penalized if its brief ``states'' that the report shows a 60.5\% clearance rate. The expert presumably meant to make the point that the model should not \emph{endorse} or \emph{adopt} defendants' position that the 60.5\% clearance rate is the relevant comparison. The task presumably wants to check that the model realizes the defendants' statistic is misleading and thus refrains from endorsing it in the opposition brief. But the AI judges interpret ``states'' literally: one of the AI models we tested on the benchmark, Claude Opus 5.5, dealt with the defendants' argument exactly the way a good lawyer would have---only to be punished for it. Under the heading ``Defendants rely on the wrong statistic,'' it stated the defendants' figure, explained that the statistic lumps together criminal, traffic, family, and juvenile matters with civil cases, and it cited the civil case statistic that actually matters: 97.9\% for Cook County and 96.3\% for the proposed forum---meaning, if anything, that on defendants' preferred measure, plaintiff's chosen forum of Cook County was better. But all three AI judges imposed a 30-point penalty on Opus for that response (the heaviest penalty weight in the assignments I reviewed), because the rubric as written literally requires the agents not to mention the statistic at all, not even to refute it---even though that's exactly what a good lawyer should do. Holding every other grade fixed, that one penalty took the assignment score from 86\% to 45\%.

I observed several other kinds of defects in the audit. In several instances, criteria simply got the law wrong---or demanded that models assert a fact that was contradicted by the task environment provided to the model. Some rubrics required models to take a particular position on a question of professional judgment where, in my view, the best lawyers either would reach a different position from the one the rubric required or at least would consider a position that the rubric would fail to reward at least as reasonable as the rubric's preferred position.

Section~\ref{sec:design} reports the results of the audits. Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms} explains how professional judgment can be evaluated, and gives examples of the key kinds of defects I observed and how those defects could impact the evaluation and training signals applied to models. Section~\ref{sec:implications} explores what implications this might have for legal-domain evaluations and training. The appendices have a detailed write-up on each criterion I reviewed, with notes on how the AI models we tested performed on those criteria and a narrative description of my reasoning for each determination I made.

\section{Audit design and results}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{The benchmarks}

Harvey AI Corporation, the leading vertical legal AI company today, released LAB in May 2026. \checkcite{In recent model releases, LAB has been the most widely cited legal benchmark used by frontier labs to illustrate the legal capability of their current frontier models.} The initial release had roughly 1,250 tasks across 24 practice areas (of which litigation, the area I audited, is just one) and more than 75,000 expert-written rubrics. Harvey has said LAB uses real client matters handled by practicing lawyers as inspiration for AI-generated task documents and environments \citep{harveyLAB}. Harvey uses AI generation to get around the difficulty of getting clients to share real legal documents, which risks waiver of privilege over internal materials. Each LAB criterion is a binary pass or fail. Somewhat unusually, Harvey took the position that in legal work missing even one issue is a critical mistake, so a task is graded successful only if every criterion passes. A portion of the benchmark is open source on GitHub and available for lawyers to audit, while a separate private hold-out set is used to report LAB results on the Artificial Analysis leaderboard \citep{labAAboard}. I audited the \labTasks{} tasks and \labCriteria{} criteria in the public litigation and dispute resolution folder at Harvey commit \texttt{1dd81403}.

micro1 is a data and research lab that produces reinforcement learning environments used by frontier labs to improve their models, in legal and several other domains, using experts such as practicing attorneys to help with the work. LegalReasonBench has 50 matters, 122 assignments, and 2,236 criteria, of which 353 are penalty criteria. micro1 states that a practicing lawyer created each assignment, the associated case file, and its rubrics \citep{micro1LRB}. The open-source public sample on Hugging Face has 15 assignments and 221 criteria. I audited a litigation subset that covers \lrbAssignments{} assignments, with \lrbCriteria{} criteria across four matters. At least three of the four appear to be adapted from real cases with names and some facts changed. Each criterion carries a point value, and a panel of three AI judges decides each criterion by strict majority. An assignment's score is the points earned on the positive criteria, less any penalties the judges impose, divided by the total points available on the positive criteria; a negative total counts as zero. The dataset I reviewed is pinned on Hugging Face at revision \texttt{1bfa904c} \citep{lrbaudit}.

There are two noteworthy differences between the benchmarks: LAB's all-pass scoring rule means that one failed criterion results in a zero on an otherwise excellent deliverable, while LegalReasonBench imposes a points-based penalty but does not zero tasks for defects. Additionally, while both benchmarks use synthetic environments, the task environments in Harvey's LAB are primarily synthetic with no recognizable connection to real cases, whereas micro1's LegalReasonBench tasks plainly are adapted from real litigation matters. There were tradeoffs and different failure modes associated with each of these approaches (Section~\ref{sec:provenance}).

\subsection{AI screening and two sampling frames}

To conduct the audits, I gave GPT-6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 examples of defective rubrics and asked them to flag any defective or arguably defective criteria. For LAB, Sol flagged \labSolFlagged{} criteria and Opus flagged \labOpusBlind{}; after Opus reviewed Sol's findings, \labFlagged{} criteria (\labFlaggedPct\%) were flagged by one model or both. For LegalReasonBench, Sol flagged \lrbSolFlagged{} criteria and Opus \lrbOpusFlagged{}; between them they flagged \lrbFlagged{} of \lrbCriteria{} criteria (\lrbFlaggedPct\%), and a later reconciliation pass retained \lrbReconciled{} of the \lrbFlagged{} \citep{lrbaudit}. In both audits an Opus pass was permitted to review both models' findings. The procedures differed in some details, chiefly that the LAB sampling frame was fixed after that review and the LegalReasonBench frame before it (Appendices~\ref{app:labreview} and~\ref{app:lrbreview}). Those differences affect which criteria were eligible for sampling, not the validity of either lower bound: each bound counts only the defects found in that benchmark's frame and treats every criterion outside the frame as sound. This was intended to operate as two standalone reviews of these benchmarks, not a formal statistically valid comparison of the defect rate between them.

\subsection{The standard I applied}

After the AI agents flagged defective or arguably defective criteria, I drew a simple random sample of \labSample{} criteria without replacement, with a fixed seed, using a sampling protocol that was committed before the draw \citep{labreview,lrbreview}. Rubrics where the answer key contains legal or factual errors, or rests on objective discrepancies or errors in the task environment, were considered defective and labeled objectively incorrect.

An additional set of rubrics were labeled defective as a matter of professional judgment; this was used in situations where the rubric required a decision that, in my view, top lawyers would consider inaccurate. Professional judgment defects also include a few instances where the rubric would fail a response that, in my view, most lawyers would see as a judgment call---and the rubric effectively forces the AI model to take one particular position on an issue where there is actually room for debate, which can itself be a kind of legal error. For example, LegalReasonBench required advising a governmental client who lost an administrative review petition in district court to both appeal the loss and redo the decision at the agency; that's a reasonable judgment, and probably the default path most lawyers would take, but in my view it's erroneous to require that recommendation in the internal memo to the client. The circumstances of that case are such that the client's objectives could be fully achieved by simply redoing the decision at the agency level and forgoing the appeal (which likely would become moot once the agency reinstated its decision with new reasoning). Thus, in the context of the assignment for an internal memo, the rubric ideally would present both options to the client and allow the client to decide whether the added expense of the appeal is worthwhile instead of simply assuming that appealing the adverse ruling is the best approach.

After preparing my initial draft of the verdicts, I asked AI models (GPT-6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5, and Claude Fable 5.1) to stress test them and argue both sides of each call, then did a consistency pass through the full set. A handful of verdicts changed based on the AI-assisted stress testing and additional review that I did.

\subsection{Native runs and citation checks}

I ran both benchmarks on GPT-6 Luna (xhigh reasoning) and Claude Opus 5.5 (low reasoning), using each benchmark's own code (including the benchmark's harness, judge models, and scoring code). This was intended merely as a diagnostic, not a model comparison, to understand where a given rubric might pass for one model but fail for the other and whether that was based on genuine differences in the quality of the legal work product or pointed to potential defects in the rubrics (Appendix~\ref{app:native}).

The rubric audit revealed that some of the task documents on Harvey's LAB contained hallucinated case law; some of the LAB answer keys similarly referred to hallucinated cases. I had AI agents check every citation in the LAB litigation environments against CourtListener, then reviewed a random sample of the flagged citations myself under a pre-committed stopping rule (specifically, I drew 20 and committed in advance to stop after the first 10 if all 10 were confirmed errors, and otherwise to review all 20). A separate AI citation census covered the LegalReasonBench litigation documents (Appendix~\ref{app:citecheck}).

In LAB, \labObjective{} of the \labDefective{} defective criteria are objective errors, such as these: the environment's time entries add up to \$215,680, not the answer key value the rubric assumes of \$193,372.50; the rubric states the opposite of Georgia tortious-interference law; the rubric finds a conflict in a preservation notice that contains none. The other seven fail work an elite lawyer could consider at least as good as what the rubric requires. In LegalReasonBench, \lrbObjective{} of \lrbDefective{} are objective errors, and the remaining \lrbJudgment{} are errors of professional judgment; \lrbFromEnv{} arise from the task materials rather than the criterion alone.

In cases where both AI auditors considered a rubric defective, they were almost always right. In LAB, \labBothDefective{} of the \labBothFlagged{} criteria flagged by both models were defective, compared with \labSingleDefective{} of \labSingleFlagged{} flagged by one; in LegalReasonBench, all \lrbBothFlagged{} flagged by both blind reports were defective, compared with \lrbSingleDefective{} of \lrbSingleFlagged{}.

The two model runs we did with the benchmarks illustrate how much all-pass scoring (used in Harvey's LAB) impacts the results. Across the \labTasks{} LAB litigation tasks, averaging each judge's per-task pass fraction, GPT-6 Luna passed 83.8\% of criteria and Claude Opus 5.5 passed 90.8\%. The all-pass rate, by contrast, was 1.9\% for Luna and 2.9\% for Opus, and each model passed exactly one task outright with both judges. Under LAB's all-pass approach, one defective criterion is enough to fail a task. Thus, LAB's headline number is unusually sensitive to the correctness of each criterion.

I also did a sweep for hallucinated case law, which found a significant number of hallucinations in Harvey's LAB (likely owing to the synthetic data used to create it). Within LAB's litigation environments, an AI check of 3,618 citations found 436 seemingly unintentional errors in citations to real authority, including 76 authorities that could not be found. I reviewed a random sample under a pre-committed stopping rule and confirmed that the first ten of ten were all true citation errors. Statistically, the lower bound is that at least 303 of the 436 are real errors. While a similar AI audit yielded some instances where cases were mischaracterized in LegalReasonBench, it did not flag any hallucinated cases.

\section{How the criteria misgrade legal work}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

The exhibits are organized by mechanism, not by benchmark. Each gives the task context, the criterion as written, the human assessment, and how the benchmark's own judges graded the saved runs. The complete review of all 50 sampled criteria is in Appendices~\ref{app:labreview} and \ref{app:lrbreview}.

\subsection{Mention is not endorsement}
\label{sec:stating}

The 60.5\% penalty that opens this paper is the clearest case.

\begin{lrbquote}{micro1 LegalReasonBench, \texttt{criterion\_\allowbreak{}18}, forum non conveniens, assignment 2, \href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/micro1-inc/LegalReasonBench/blob/1bfa904c8f255925baf2610c0f86aa7c05d94ed9/tasks/lrb-forum-non-conveniens-response/steps/assignment-2/tests/fixtures/rubric.json\#L174}{rubric at revision \texttt{1bfa904c}}}
\textbf{Penalty criterion, $-$30 points.} States in Assignment \#2 that the 2023 Illinois Annual Report Statistics report a 60.5\% clearance rate in Cook County, Illinois.
\end{lrbquote}

The report in the record does say 60.5\%. The defendants' motion relies ``principally'' on it. A response brief has to answer it, and the rubric's own criteria 19 and 20 reward the answer, which is the civil-case comparison. The persuasive way to make that point is to name the defendants' figure and show what it measures. The penalty judge, however, sees only the criterion text and is told to answer pass if the response commits the failure. Both saved responses were penalized by all three judges. Opus's brief set out the civil rates and explained why the all-case rate is the wrong comparator; Luna's said the figure ``expressly covers all case types'' and gave it limited weight. Only a brief that relied on the 60.5\% figure as the right measure of congestion should be penalized. I coded this as a professional-judgment defect: the text, the votes, and the point contribution are observable facts, and the assessment that quoting an adversary's figure to rebut it is permissible advocacy is a professional conclusion. It is not a close one.

The same mechanism appears in the criminal appeal, where a $-$5 penalty fires if the brief ``mentions'' that the defendant pled guilty in state court to intimidation with a dangerous weapon. The plea concerns a different incident, and the criterion is plainly meant to test whether the model keeps the two apart. Opus was penalized, two votes to one, for a drafting note that told the supervising lawyer it had decided \emph{not} to rely on the other incident because it was unrelated. A note that mentions a fact in order to set it aside is correct work. The first forum non conveniens assignment has a third penalty of the same form (``Mentions \ldots{} that Cook County lacks interest''), which did not fire on the saved runs but would fire on a memo that stated the defendants' argument and then explained why the uploaded authority defeats it. What these criteria share is that they test for the presence of a proposition rather than its function. In legal writing the function is everything: the same sentence is a concession in one paragraph and the setup for a rebuttal in the next.

\subsection{Proposed evidence is not evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

The second forum non conveniens assignment asks for the opposition brief and says, ``Please include all affidavits and information you referenced from Assignment \#1.'' The record names five Cook County physicians a lawyer on the team has spoken to and reports that they are willing to testify. No affidavit from any of them, or from the plaintiff, is in the record, and the prompt states that a document not uploaded ``does not exist for purposes of this analysis.'' Ten criteria then award points for stating that the response is supported by each physician's affidavit and that each affidavit shows DuPage County would be inconvenient for the affiant, and an eleventh does the same for the plaintiff's own affidavit; six of the eleven were in my sample.

\begin{lrbquote}{micro1 LegalReasonBench, \texttt{criterion\_\allowbreak{}1}, forum non conveniens, assignment 2, \href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/micro1-inc/LegalReasonBench/blob/1bfa904c8f255925baf2610c0f86aa7c05d94ed9/tasks/lrb-forum-non-conveniens-response/steps/assignment-2/tests/fixtures/rubric.json\#L4}{rubric at revision \texttt{1bfa904c}}}
\textbf{Reward criterion, +3 points.} States in Assignment \#2 that the response is supported by the affidavit of Liam Vance, a physician who provided care to Baby Davis at Cook County Hospital.
\end{lrbquote}

Opus's brief states that ``Plaintiff submits sworn affidavits from five named Cook County treating physicians'' and that ``[e]ach of the five treating physicians has executed an affidavit in the same form \ldots{} separately signed,'' then attaches one unsigned template with ``[Name]'' and ``[specialty]'' blanks. All three judges credited it. Luna's brief, which identifies the five as physicians ``with whom counsel has spoken'' and cites no affidavit, received nothing on these criteria. The instruction can be read to invite drafting proposed affidavits, and drafting a form for a witness to review is ordinary practice. What the criteria reward is a representation to the court that supporting evidence has already been executed and sworn. A nonparty witness decides what she is willing to swear to, and five physicians do not sign one lawyer's form on schedule. The hazard is that a human lawyer, through inadvertence, files a brief that says the affidavits exist. The scoring system credited the representation and gave nothing to the brief that declined to make it. I coded these as professional-judgment defects arising from the environment, because the drafting instruction admits competing readings; the fix is either to supply executed affidavits or to reward bracketed placeholders that say the affidavits are contemplated.

A rejected flag in the land-use matter marks the boundary. Two criteria there reward a brief for citing the expert testimony and the staff report that supported the county's rezoning. Neither document is attached, and the auditors flagged both criteria on that ground. But the petition quotes the testimony with transcript citations, and other supplied documents establish that the staff report exists. A competent lawyer would cite both. A missing attachment does not bar a supported inference; the defect in the affidavit criteria is that proposed evidence silently becomes completed evidence. Both saved responses failed both land-use criteria, which is the benchmark working as intended.

\subsection{``No conflict'' is an answer}
\label{sec:noconflict}

Two criteria, one in each benchmark, require the model to find a discrepancy that is not there. In LAB's preservation-notice task, a Department of Justice notice defines the relevant period as January 1, 2019 forward, ``unless otherwise specified herein,'' and then provides that ``[n]otwithstanding the Relevant Period defined in Paragraph 12,'' certain financial records must be preserved from January 1, 2017. Criterion C-002 requires the memo to recommend seeking clarification from the DOJ ``regarding the conflicting date ranges.'' There is no conflict. The notice sets a general period and an express longer period for one category, which is a common and clearly drafted request. Luna's memo read it correctly, said the two periods were complementary, and was failed by both judges. Opus's memo was failed by one judge and passed by the other, on the strength of an unrelated clarification request about category numbering.

In LegalReasonBench's police-discipline matter, criterion 8 of the first assignment awards 28 of 71 available points for identifying ``that the comparator memorandum's stated coverage years conflict with the dates of the cases it summarizes.''

\begin{lrbquote}{micro1 LegalReasonBench, \texttt{criterion\_\allowbreak{}8}, police discipline, assignment 1, \href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/micro1-inc/LegalReasonBench/blob/1bfa904c8f255925baf2610c0f86aa7c05d94ed9/tasks/lrb-dcca-challenge-police-discipline/steps/assignment-1/tests/fixtures/rubric.json\#L88}{rubric at revision \texttt{1bfa904c}}}
\textbf{Reward criterion, +28 points.} Identifies that the comparator memorandum's stated coverage years conflict with the dates of the cases it summarizes.
\end{lrbquote}

The memorandum says it reviewed records covering calendar years 2018 through 2021. Its four cases arose in August 2018, November 2019, May 2020, and March 2021 and were resolved between January 2019 and October 2021. Every date is inside the window. Both saved responses correctly reported no conflict and received zero of 28 points. A response that invented one would have been paid for it. These two are accessible to readers without litigation experience, and they show the cost of a rubric that always needs one more issue. Recognizing that there is nothing to flag is a skill. A rubric can reward the conclusion that there is nothing to flag; these criteria instead presuppose a conflict, so the only way to earn the points is to assert one.

\subsection{The posture and the sources}
\label{sec:posture}

LAB's motion-to-dismiss drafting task asks for a Rule 12(b)(6) brief seeking dismissal of a software-licensing complaint. Criterion C-045 requires the brief to identify the plaintiff's own failures, a 45-day project-manager gap and late API specifications, ``as causes of the Go-Live delay.'' Those facts come from the defendant's internal report, which the complaint disputes. On a motion to dismiss the court takes the complaint's allegations as true, and it does not resolve contested questions of causation. Both saved briefs left the argument out, and both were failed on C-045 by both judges. The rubric penalized the one procedural judgment on which both models were right.

A neighboring criterion on the same task, C-037, shows how an error in the environment propagates into the rubric. It requires the brief to cite ``relevant Delaware authority on the parol evidence rule and integration clauses, such as SIGA Technologies v. PharmAthene or Eagle Industries v. DeVilbiss.'' The task's research memo quotes both cases for propositions that do not appear in them: the language about integration clauses attributed to \emph{SIGA} is not at the cited page, which concerns the duty to negotiate in good faith, and the holding attributed to \emph{Eagle} is not in \emph{Eagle}. The rubric also asks for authority on the wrong doctrine. Under Delaware law the parol evidence rule and an integration clause do not bar a fraud claim; the question for the fraud count is whether the plaintiff can plead reliance on pre-signing representations despite the contract's non-reliance language. Opus declined to cite either named case, cited Delaware anti-reliance authority instead, and was failed by both judges. A rubric built from an unchecked memo inherits the memo's mistakes, and here it failed the brief that caught them. The citation check found problems in 28 of the 53 citations in this task's documents.

\subsection{One strategy among several}
\label{sec:strategy}

The remaining defects are of the second kind. The criterion does not contradict the record or the law. It takes a question on which the best lawyers would differ and fails every answer but one.

LAB's motion in limine task is the example I present with the most care, because it is the one where I am applying judgment rather than reading a source. A damages expert's report itemizes five categories that sum to \$14,211,000 and states total damages of \$14,200,000. At her deposition she called it a rounding discrepancy. The task's strategy memo treats it as an ``arithmetic error'' and a ground for excluding her testimony under \emph{Daubert}, and criterion C-009 fails any motion that does not identify the discrepancy; its unsampled companion C-010 requires tying it to exclusion. A rounded total is not an error, and an \$11,000 error in a \$14.2 million calculation would not be a basis for exclusion in any event; at most it would get the erroneous figure replaced with a corrected one, which raises the client's exposure by the amount the expert rounded off. Raising the point also carries a real risk of annoying the judge: a motion that spends the court's time on a rounding difference signals that the movant had nothing better. All four saved grades on C-009 are passes, and the judges split on Luna's treatment of C-010, one failing it for downplaying the point; the defect here is a potential failure, not an observed one. My view is that the better motion leaves the discrepancy out and concentrates on the real methodological problems with the report. Another advocate might put it in a footnote. What the rubric does is make omission a failing answer, and under all-pass scoring that means a motion that keeps the court's attention on the strong arguments fails the task. I coded C-009 as a professional-judgment defect, not an objective error.

The pattern recurs. A conflicts-memo criterion in LAB requires recommending that an associate who shares an apartment with his sister, a paralegal at the adverse party, be screened; informed consent with a confidentiality protocol is a permissible resolution, and the same task's other criteria accept consent for comparable conflicts. A case-assessment criterion requires recommending a motion to dismiss an unjust-enrichment count pleaded in the alternative to a contract claim the rubric itself calls strong, where the motion would not reduce exposure or narrow discovery; a careful memo could weigh that cost and advise against filing. In LegalReasonBench's land-use matter, two criteria worth 20 points each require the county's memo to recommend both an appeal and a remand to the hearing examiner. A protective appeal is reasonable and probably the default, but curing the defects on remand without an appeal is a course many government lawyers would prefer, and the criteria give that recommendation nothing; the real county did both, and its own appellate filing acknowledged the remand might moot the appeal. In each case the required answer is permissible. So is the answer the criterion fails.

\section{Evaluating professional judgment}
\label{sec:frame}

\subsection{Constraints, latitude, and quality}

Suppose the posted limit is 55 miles per hour. Driving at 60 breaks the rule. Driving at 54 in dense fog may still be negligent, because the law also requires a speed that is reasonable and prudent under the conditions; New York's statute states both requirements in one section, Veh.\ \& Traf.\ Law \S~1180(a), (b). An examiner who passes the 60 has contradicted the rule. An examiner who passes every speed below 55 has ignored the standard. And an examiner who insists that the only competent speed in fog was exactly 35 has turned one reasonable choice into the sole correct answer, which the law never did.

Legal work mixes the two kinds of question throughout, and evaluating it means asking three things. What constrains an acceptable answer: the record, the arithmetic, the governing law, and the assignment. What choices remain defensible within those constraints: strategy, emphasis, selection, and presentation. And what makes one defensible answer better than another: persuasiveness, materiality, economy, and usefulness to the reader. Legal theory draws the first line as rules against standards \citep{kaplow}, and appellate review draws something like it: conclusions of law are reviewed de novo, discretionary decisions only for abuse of discretion, and the reviewing court may not substitute its preference for a decision inside the permitted band. The analogy is an analogy. Rules can be ambiguous, standards can be clearly violated, and a court ``by definition abuses its discretion when it makes an error of law,'' \emph{Koon v. United States}, 518 U.S. 81, 100 (1996). But the structure is the right one for a lawyer's work. \emph{Strickland v. Washington}, 466 U.S. 668, 688--89 (1984), recognizes ``a wide range of reasonable professional assistance'' and explains that ``[n]o particular set of detailed rules for counsel's conduct can satisfactorily take account of the variety of circumstances faced by defense counsel or the range of legitimate decisions regarding how best to represent'' a client. \emph{Strickland}'s floor is too low to define excellent work. Its recognition that competent lawyers legitimately differ is what a rubric that fails the omission of an \$11,000 rounding point lacks.

The closest prior work in the evaluation literature draws the same line from German examination law, under which a defensible and consistently argued solution may not be marked wrong, while weak argument may still earn fewer points: ``Latitude protects the result, not the mark'' \citep{naglSoK}. Nagl favors rubric-centered grading for auditable legal assessment and identifies defensible alternatives and follow-on errors as the largest open validation gap. That is the gap this audit documents in two live benchmarks. The exhibits map onto the three questions. The nonexistent conflicts and the Rule 12 posture are constraints the criteria got wrong. The mention penalties and the affidavit criteria fail answers no top lawyer would rank below the ones they prefer. The strategy criteria choose among answers top lawyers would consider comparable, and under all-pass scoring that choice decides the task.

Two things follow for rubric design. A rubric should enforce constraints, and its author should be held to the sources when it does. A rubric may apply a standard where the judge applying it can add real signal: a page limit can be checked; whether the argument is organized so the court finds the dispositive point first needs a judge that can tell. Comparative judgment can supplement criteria for the third question, which is where excellence lives; on 30 legal tasks, attorneys' pairwise comparisons recovered a constructed quality ordering far better than rubric scores did \citep{judgmentBench}. One refinement keeps the frame honest. Whether a question has a right answer is independent of whether getting it wrong matters. A typographical error is objectively wrong and immaterial; an unsupported statement that a critical affidavit has been signed is a judgment call with real consequences. The audit did not code materiality separately. A benchmark should, and all-pass scoring, which treats every criterion as material, cannot.

\subsection{More arguments are not better lawyering}
\label{sec:selection}

One response to the strategy defects is that a criterion which elicits an additional defensible point is at worst harmless: the model learns to raise more. That is wrong. The range of plausible-sounding arguments available in any position far exceeds what a court, an adversary, or a client will consider. A defendant can usually find a colorable objection to venue, process, or service of process, and must raise it in its first response or lose it, Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(h)(1); it is rare that more than one is worth the court's time, and leading with a weak one teaches the judge to discount the strong one. Every lawyer learns to winnow. The Supreme Court said so in holding that appointed appellate counsel need not raise every nonfrivolous issue a client wants raised: ``Experienced advocates since time beyond memory have emphasized the importance of winnowing out weaker arguments on appeal and focusing on one central issue if possible, or at most on a few key issues,'' \emph{Jones v. Barnes}, 463 U.S. 745, 751--52 (1983), and, quoting Justice Jackson, ``[l]egal contentions, like the currency, depreciate through over-issue,'' \emph{id.} at 752.

The reason is attention. There is a context window for the model, and there is also one for the reader: the judge who gives the motion twenty minutes, the adversary deciding whether the letter is serious, the general counsel reading the memo on a phone. A second-tier argument does not sit harmlessly beside the first-tier one. It dilutes it, signals that the author could not tell the difference, and spends credibility the strong argument needed. Practitioners are seeing a version of this now: negotiators complain of AI-generated markups that propose dozens of trivial changes to boilerplate with a settled meaning. That is an observation from practice, not a finding of this audit. But the audited criteria point the same way. A rubric that fails a motion for omitting a rounding point, or a conflicts memo for choosing consent over screening, rewards inclusion over justified omission and supplies no signal that omission was the better choice.

The principle I would extract is this. A benchmark that claims to measure professional judgment must distinguish discovering an argument from deciding that the argument belongs in the deliverable, and it must be able to accept a sound answer its author did not anticipate. Whether an omission is sound depends on the assignment, the audience, the stakes, and the alternatives. A comprehensive internal memorandum should cover more than a brief. That is a reason to write the rubric to the assignment, not a reason to reward coverage everywhere.

\section{Implications for evaluation and training}
\label{sec:implications}

\subsection{What the scores establish}

A defect rate cannot be subtracted from a score. A criterion that misgrades every model the same way leaves the ordering unchanged; a smaller number of criteria that misgrade models differently can move it. Which happened here is unknown, because the audit graded criteria and not model outputs across the full set. What the audit supports is narrower: the robustness of reported performance differences to substantive correction of the criteria has not been established, and establishing it would take paired regrading of actual outputs on the same evaluation set. On the private LAB leaderboard the leading models are separated by a few points of criterion pass rate \citep{labAAboard}, and the defect share estimated here for the public litigation set is larger than those gaps. That is a reason to ask whether the ordering is robust, not evidence that it is wrong. Coding went through the same cycle: in February 2026 OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified after an audit found problems with test requirements and task specification \citep{swecritique}. As models improve, errors a benchmark could once absorb show up as failures of models that are now better than the benchmark.

\subsection{What the training gains establish}
\label{sec:training}

Three propositions need to be kept apart: that training improves a model's score on a legal benchmark; that it improves the legal work practitioners care about; and that legal specialization improves that work beyond what a comparable frontier model already does. Evidence for the first does not establish the second or the third, and the audit is a direct challenge to the inference between them. If a criterion rewards an unsupported assertion or penalizes a correct rebuttal, improved compliance with it is not evidence of better lawyering, however consistently the judge applies it.

The published results deserve their due, and most are of the first kind. Applied Compute reports full-parameter reinforcement learning on GLM-5.1 with LAB as the reward, graded by a model judge, and an improvement on a held-out LAB split from a 0.853 to a 0.913 rubric pass rate, with the all-pass rate rising from 0.059 to 0.126; its grader was validated by agreement between two frontier models, and no practitioner evaluation of the outputs is described \citep{appliedCompute}. Harvey's Tenet, trained from a Kimi K3 base by asynchronous reinforcement learning on environments that share LAB's structure, with a model judge over each task's rubric, reports gains on LAB's hold-out set and transfer to two separately developed benchmarks, APEX Agents and RedlineBench \citep{harveyTenet}; its reported Elo gain of roughly 130 over its base and preference in more than two of three comparisons come from a generative reward model, not from a blinded human trial \citep{harveyPreference}. Transfer to independently built benchmarks is more informative than improvement on one, and these are substantive training results. Their principal quality endpoint is rubric scoring of the kind audited here.

Two results come closer to the question, and neither isolates it. LegalOne reports a stage ablation on Chinese legal tasks: adding a curriculum reinforcement-learning stage after mid-training and supervised fine-tuning raises its 8B model from 56.37 to 62.36 on JEC-QA and from 68.32 to 71.19 on LexEval \citep{legalOne}. That is a measured RL increment, on smaller models and constrained tasks, without independent validation of professional work. The Thomson technical report supplies two kinds of evidence: a matched comparison of its reinforcement-learning checkpoint against its initialization in the same harness, scored on judge-based dimensions such as factuality and completeness, and a blind comparison in which experts rated more than 3,000 conversations and preferred the full Thomson system over each of five external systems; the authors call the latter a system demonstration rather than an isolation of the model's contribution, and the systems had different tool and data access \citep{thomson}. So the evidence on legal reinforcement learning is not all downstream of rubric benchmarks. Among the public studies I reviewed through this draft's date, however, I did not find a matched frontier comparison, before and after a legal RL stage, validated by an independent assessment of professional quality.

The burden should be stated precisely. The paper does not have to show that legal RL contributes nothing, and I do not believe that. It has to show that the measure on which the gains are reported does not, on its own, license the inference to better professional work. The audit shows that. ``But the score went up'' answers a question the audit did not ask.

\subsection{What independent validation would look like}

Independent does not mean rubric-free. It means an assessment that does not reproduce the assumptions in dispute: a second rubric benchmark built the same way is weak corroboration, and a second model judge applying the same defective requirement is almost none. What would count: blinded practitioner comparisons on independently prepared matters; source checks of factual assertions against the record; assessment of consequential omissions and of what the lawyer chose to leave out; matched pairs in which a small change in the facts should change the answer and a superficial change should not; and separately developed, expert-validated criteria for the rule-like requirements. Lawyer preference is not an unquestioned ground truth either. Harvey's own study of 24 LAB tasks, three lawyers each, found only modest agreement on any particular task (Fleiss' $\kappa$ of 0.129) and found that model judges were ``willing to trade-off critical hallucinations or mistakes for other features in ways lawyers found unacceptable'' \citep{harveyPreference}, which is a candid statement of the aggregation problem.

Evaluation research points the same way. Rubric rewards work in domains with expert-grounded, instance-specific criteria \citep{rubricsAsRewards}; routing each criterion to a deterministic verifier where one exists and weighting essential criteria above supplementary ones reduces exploitable false positives \citep{rlrr}; rubrics can evolve during training rather than stay frozen \citep{drTulu}; and in law specifically, reinforcement learning against issue-tree rubrics built from court judgments improved correctness ``albeit with reduced coverage,'' a direct observation of a reward trading one skill for another \citep{legit}. The general reason to validate a reward against an evaluator it was not optimized for is the one Gao, Schulman, and Hilton demonstrated: optimization against a proxy eventually diverges from the gold evaluator \citep{gaoOveropt}. Measurement theory names the two failure modes: construct-irrelevant variance, where scores move for reasons unrelated to the ability measured, and construct underrepresentation, where the assessment misses part of it \citep{messick}. The defects documented here are the first kind. Table~\ref{tab:principles} sets the audit's implications against that work.

\section{How the rubrics were made}
\label{sec:provenance}

Both projects describe their construction and review at a high level. Harvey says it started from real client matters, split them into tasks, and had the rubrics written and reviewed by experts; its launch post names Mercor and Snorkel AI as contributing research groups, and a later talk by Harvey's co-founder describes those providers as running expert review and verification of every generated LAB task \citep{harveyLAB,harveyResearchLab}. \draft{In comments on an earlier draft of this paper, Harvey told me that the outside providers had a more limited role on the public LAB tasks than on the held-out set used for the Artificial Analysis evaluation.} \checkcite{A public clarification by one of LAB's authors describes substantial internal lawyer review \citep{pereyraClarification}.} micro1 states that a practicing lawyer authored each matter and its rubrics end to end, and reports a three-judge classification of its criteria into IRAC categories with high agreement \citep{micro1LRB}; that is a classification, not a substantive validation of each criterion. Neither release permits reconstructing the review history of a disputed criterion.

What the audit establishes is that substantive errors survived into the audited releases: a rubric that states Georgia law backwards, a research memo that quotes language not in the cited cases, a criterion that rewards a date conflict the record does not contain. It does not identify which stage introduced them or why review did not remove them. In other domains that question matters less, because automated checks validate the environment; in law the checks are the lawyers. Publishing the materials made these errors discoverable and makes correction possible. The reasonable demand is a review process whose source checks, disagreements, and corrections can themselves be inspected: who authored and reviewed each task, what sources the reviewers could open, how disagreements were resolved, and how corrections are versioned. The reasonable inference is that producing valid legal rubrics at scale is harder than it looked.

Synthetic records fail one way and adapted real cases another. LAB's environments are generated, and the generation introduces inconsistencies, some of which a non-lawyer would not notice: a state agency that issues OSHA citations to a private employer in a state with no federally approved plan for private employers; an invoice whose line items do not sum to its subtotal; a complaint that quotes the forum-selection clause it is violating. Appendix~\ref{app:mtd} walks through the opposition-issues task in which that complaint appears. I coded the sampled criterion on the OSHA task as sound, because the rubric only asked the memo to address the citation and a memo can do that while noticing the environment is incoherent. The harder question is what a model should do with knowledge from pretraining that the environment contradicts. A good lawyer brings prior knowledge to every record and looks for what seems out of place, and a reward that pays only for working within the supplied documents could, in principle, teach a model to stop doing that. That is a hypothesis about a training mechanism, not something this audit observed. LegalReasonBench's adapted matters fail differently: twice in my sample the criterion was correct for the real case and wrong for the task, because the task had changed the facts and the rubric had not followed. In the police-discipline matter the rubric rewards the proposition that no uploaded authority requires the department to prove the comparability of its comparator cases, which was the department's argument in the real litigation; in the task, the department's own final notice relies on the comparators, and the uploaded \emph{Douglas} factors place the burden on the agency. In the jury-instruction matter the rubric awards 25 points for noting a lack of evidence about who fired first, which was disputed in the real case; the task's closed-universe statement of facts says who fired first. Both saved responses declined to assert what the record contradicted and received nothing.

\section{Directions}
\label{sec:directions}

Three directions follow, each stated at the level the evidence supports.

The commission defects are established. Whether the benchmarks also underrepresent the skills that cut across matters is a hypothesis this audit cannot test, and it is worth testing. Several recurrent skills can be stated explicitly: reading text with its exceptions and interacting provisions, reasoning from authority and distinguishing it, respecting procedural posture and burdens, identifying what is missing from a record, and judging what is material. The canons of interpretation are a published taxonomy of one of them, though canons vary in authority and their interaction is contested, so recognizing a canon's name is not the same as applying it in an unfamiliar matter. LegalBench includes interpretation tasks \citep{legalBench}. My impression from reading all \labCriteria{} LAB litigation criteria and all \lrbCriteria{} LegalReasonBench criteria is that nearly all specify content to include and few ask whether the model selected, prioritized, or qualified well; micro1's own classification assigns 57\% of positive weight to Application and 5\% to Issue \citep{micro1LRB}, so ``predetermined content'' is the right label, not ``issue spotting.'' Turning that impression into a result takes a declared taxonomy and a coverage inventory of both benchmarks.

Published judicial opinions are a vast body of reasoned decisions, each stating facts, competing positions, the court's resolution, and often its reasons for rejecting the losing argument, and in the United States they are public domain as government edicts, \emph{Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org, Inc.}, 590 U.S. 255 (2020), which is not true of the briefs filed alongside them. They are published explanations of decisions, not transcripts of a judge's thinking, and some current work already builds rubrics from them \citep{legit}. Law school has used them for a century and a half to teach the transferable skills above, by having students meet a principle in example after example and then test it on new facts; Singer describes the method and the problem-solving cases that start before the facts and issues are settled \citep{singer}. That is a design for a learning environment: reading opinions resembles learning from demonstrations, predicting dispositions is supervised learning, and the Socratic variation of facts is a transfer test. Three targets need to stay distinct in any such program: predicting what a court did, reconstructing its stated reasoning, and advising competently on the information available before the decision.

LegalForecastBench, my earlier benchmark, asks models to predict how federal judges ruled on motions to dismiss from the record the judge received \citep{lfbpaper}. Its contribution here is an externally observable label that no rubric author wrote. Outcome prediction is a sparse signal, and the proposition that learning to predict how courts and lawyers react to a fact pattern will teach general principles of legal reasoning, as predicting the next token taught general principles of language, is a hypothesis I have not tested. The audit supplies a reason for care: in the land-use matter the real county pursued the strategy the rubric requires, and I still coded the criterion defective, because what a lawyer actually did is not the unique correct answer. Denser signals are available in the same records, including issue-level holdings and arguments a court expressly accepted or rejected, with the discipline that silence about an argument is not a negative label. For the evaluation problem itself, the approaches worth investigating map onto the failures observed: source checks for assertions that can be verified, judging that can tell endorsement from rebuttal, multiple acceptable paths for questions inside the band of discretion, comparison against expert specimens for overall quality, and real-world labels where an outcome exists. Supervised fine-tuning on expert work product and rubrics updated during training are candidates, not demonstrated solutions, and if the training reward moves, the evaluation has to stay held out.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

One reviewer, who is also the author, applied the protocol; an independent second lawyer re-deciding some or all of the 50 items would strengthen the result, and I would welcome it. The samples are from AI-flagged frames, so the bounds are lower bounds on those frames and say nothing about criteria the screening missed, and the frames differ between benchmarks. The audited releases are the public litigation sets at pinned revisions, not the private material used for leaderboards or training, and not the other practice areas. The native runs are one execution per model and condition, used as diagnostics. The audit grades criteria and does not correct scores, and nothing in it measures the effect of training against these rewards.

These audits identify substantive failures in the criteria used to evaluate legal work. Consistent application does not cure an incorrect requirement, and improved compliance does not by itself establish improved professional quality. Legal evaluation must also recognize justified restraint: a capable model should be able to identify a fact, an argument, or a possible criticism and decide whether to assert it, qualify it, rebut it, investigate it, or leave it out. The public releases examined here make that question testable. Their next contribution can be to support evaluation that distinguishes those choices accurately.

\section{A synthetic task environment: the motion-to-dismiss case study}
\label{app:mtd}

In addition to auditing the accuracy of all \labCriteria{} rubrics, I did a deep dive review of one substantive LAB litigation task. This review highlighted a different but equally important issue: the use of synthetic or AI-generated data may result in task environments that are unrealistic in a variety of subtle ways that may not be obvious to nonexperts but that are striking and clear to practicing attorneys. The task \texttt{analyze-counterparty-motion-to-dismiss} asks AI agents to prepare a list of issues for the opposition brief to a motion to dismiss, on a fictitious software service dispute.

I observed several ways in which the synthetically generated task environment seemed unrealistic and could potentially train models on a distorted view of what genuine practice looks like \citep{labtask}.

\paragraph{Planted issues.} A number of the legal disputes in the task environment are obviously ``planted'' in a way that feels artificial and ultimately presents issues in a way that is meaningfully different from how real-world legal disputes manifest. For example, the contract the parties are suing over has a forum selection clause that requires any lawsuit to be filed in Travis County, Texas. The plaintiff literally quotes this entire provision in the complaint but nevertheless files suit in the Northern District of Georgia and offers no explanation for why they believe the forum selection clause they quoted does not apply or otherwise permits the suit in Georgia. It of course happens regularly that a lazy or incompetent litigant files suit in the wrong forum; a competent lawyer potentially could decide to ignore the clause and hope that their adversary overlooks the issue. But in both of those scenarios, the forum selection clause would not be explicitly flagged in the complaint as it is here; a real version of this legal task would require the lawyer to dig through a number of documents and realize that buried in one of them is a forum selection clause that allows the lawyer to have the case moved to Texas.

This is one example, but a significant number of the tasks in these environments were similarly planted in a way that's artificial and unlike how disputes around these issues arise in real cases. Environments like this can distort an evaluation of a model's true capabilities, since simply looking for the obviously planted issues will satisfy the benchmark without testing whether the model would be able to identify this issue from more realistic source material akin to what it will see in real cases.

\paragraph{Environment documents violate court rules.} Many of the documents in these environments do not resemble real lawyer work product. The environment provides counterparty litigation documents in docx format, and the native files from the git repository often cannot be opened, although agents and various CLI tools can recover the document text. In real litigation, lawyers rarely share their docx files with their opponent; the opponent's memorandum of law is ordinarily filed with the court as a PDF. The motion papers also lack a proper caption, include a stale table of contents, contain markdown formatting artifacts that render in Word as junk characters even in files that can be opened normally, lack citations for propositions that generally require citations, and are single spaced rather than double spaced in violation of the applicable court rules.

The motion that the agent is being asked to respond to is so far below professional norms that you would want any analysis of the motion to include consideration of whether the judge would strike the motion or require it to be refiled in compliance with the rules. That is not included in the rubric for this task because the quality control issues appear to be unintentional, not part of the test for the agent. But it is a genuine gap: if a document like this were filed in real litigation, the quality of the work product could itself become a substantive issue in the litigation and thus would need to be flagged given the goal motivating the all-pass criterion, which was to ensure that the AI does not overlook any important issues \citep[section ``Evaluating a task'']{harveyLAB}.

There are valid reasons to prefer rubric-based reinforcement learning approaches. For example, while a benchmark like LegalForecastBench (which asks models to predict how federal judges ruled on motions to dismiss) can test a model's legal reasoning and analytical ability over a narrow task, it does not test the model's ability to generate new work product, which is an important consideration for a company like Harvey that is developing legal agents to prepare and revise different kinds of legal work product. But reviewing these environments suggests that the current state of the art in synthetic data generation introduces drift on a variety of dimensions that could undermine the efficacy of the task environment as a training or evaluation signal. The model is in effect being tested on its ability to recognize a distinct environment, very unlike what it will encounter in production, and adhere to a stylized set of norms and rules that were artificially constructed in that environment but do not comport with the real norms or rules that govern real-world tasks. While creating LegalForecastBench illustrated that there are real challenges in working with real-world litigation data as well, for a variety of tasks that data has a lot of rich, implicit signal that data providers generating synthetic data are unable to capture.

In order to validate the AI findings and determine how many of the AI-flagged rubrics were genuinely defective, I reviewed a simple random sample of \labSample{} of the flagged rubrics, drawn with a fixed seed from \labSampleTasks{} tasks. I committed the protocol for that review before it began \citep{labreview}. For each rubric, I reviewed the grading rubric itself, the findings from both AI auditors, how LAB's AI judges graded two real model runs (GPT-6 Luna and Opus 5.5) on that rubric, and the relevant documents from the task environment. In a few cases I also did research into the relevant governing law. I marked a rubric as defective when it could produce an inaccurate grade, meaning: either the rubric could fail a response that a thoughtful litigator would consider appropriate, or the rubric could pass a response that a thoughtful litigator would consider wrong on the issue the rubric is testing. A rubric that forces one of several permissible answers is therefore defective, because it would fail the others.

During the review, I decided to distinguish between defects in the rubrics themselves and defects in the task environment. The line between the two was based on whether the defect could change the grade a model is given on the task. For example, one rubric wanted the work product to contain a specific metric sourced from the task environment, but the AI auditors pointed out that the task environment actually contained two contradictory numbers for that particular metric. Accordingly, an AI agent that relied on one of the numbers (which was not the one the rubric was expecting) would be failed incorrectly because of the underlying defect in the task environment. I treated that as a defect in both the rubric and the task environment. By contrast, a different task environment contained a notice of an OSHA regulatory violation from a state-level regulatory agency in Ohio that does not actually have jurisdiction over the violations charged. The rubric wanted work product to simply flag the existence of the charge in the work product. I coded that as a defect in the task environment but not as a defect in the rubric (because an AI agent handed that task could, and likely still would, pass the rubric by simply mentioning the charged violation).

Of the \labSample{} criteria, I graded \labDefective{} rubrics defective in ways that affect grading, including \labObjective{} that I considered to be unambiguous objective errors in which the record, the law, the cited authority, or the arithmetic contradicts the rubric. \labArguable{} were arguable defects, and \labNotDefective{} were not defective. \labEnvItems{} of the \labSample{} come from task environments that contain defects (\labEnvTasks{} of the \labSampleTasks{} tasks). Only \labNoDefect{} had neither a defective nor an arguable criterion and came from an environment without a defect (Table~\ref{tab:labreview}).

Since the sample was a draw without replacement from the \labFlagged{} criteria that AI agents had flagged as defective, the one-sided 95\% lower confidence bounds are: at least \labDefectiveLB{} flagged criteria are defective in ways that affect grading (point estimate \labDefectivePoint{}; the bound is \labDefectiveShare\% of all criteria in the practice area); at least \labObjectiveLB{} are unambiguous objective errors (point estimate \labObjectivePoint{}); and at least \labEnvLB{} come from task environments that contain defects (point estimate \labEnvPoint{}). At least \labAnyLB{} involve a defective criterion, a defective environment, or both (point estimate \labAnyPoint{}). In the sample, \labBothDefective{} of the \labBothFlagged{} criteria flagged by both auditors were defective, against \labSingleDefective{} of the \labSingleFlagged{} flagged by only one.

I did not attempt to estimate defects in criteria that were not flagged by the AI agents; it is possible there could be some additional defects that the agents did not flag. These estimates count criteria/rubrics, not the individual mistakes that give rise to the defects. In some cases, the defect was specific to the particular rubric at issue, but in other cases, a single error in a task environment could make several criteria defective. After I prepared an initial set of verdicts, I asked AI models (GPT-6.1 Sol and Opus 5.5) to stress test my answers and to draft additional arguments both for and against each call and a summary of those they would change. I changed a handful of verdicts after reflecting on these points, and did a second pass through the full set of my verdicts to ensure that they applied the principles I adopted during the review consistently to each call.

\noindent\textbf{My analysis.} The rubric rewards models for a legal error. The case involves federal charges against a gang member who shot at a rival gang member after following him from a mall. The defendant pled guilty to state charges of conspiracy to commit a forcible felony in connection with the incident. The rubric wants the prosecution's brief to argue that the commission of conspiracy to commit a forcible felony precludes the justification (self-defense) defense, under Iowa Code § 704.6.

But that statute only applies when the defendant commits a different forcible felony than the one he is alleging is justified. The paradigmatic case would be a robber who waves a hammer at a store clerk to take the cash in the register, intending only to frighten the clerk, and who cannot claim self-defense if the clerk responds by shooting at him (robbery is a forcible felony under the supplied Iowa Code § 702.11(1)). Iowa courts apply the bar in that setting. \emph{See State v. Roby}, No. 22-1017, slip op. at 8 (Iowa Ct. App. Sept. 27, 2023) (defendant who brought a gun to an apartment and shot an occupant who drew his own weapon: “A justification defense was unavailable to Roby because he was participating in the forcible felony of robbery. See Iowa Code § 704.6(1).”).

Here, the defendant is arguing that the shooting itself was an act of self-defense; there is no contention that he was committing a different forcible felony prior to the shooting that would negate his ability to claim self-defense for the shooting itself. (Although we did not find an Iowa case expressly holding that the forcible felony must be independent of the act claimed to be justified, judges in at least one other state with a similar statute have expressly held this. \emph{See Martinez v. State}, 981 So. 2d 449 (Fla. 2008) (holding that for the forcible-felony bar to apply, ``there must be an independent forcible felony other than the one which the defendant claims he or she committed in self-defense''). Obviously, the law could not be to the contrary because, if it were, the exception would swallow the rule; self-defense would never be available, because absent the defense, the use of force would almost always constitute a forcible felony.)

This environment is based on a real case, and in the real case, neither the Eighth Circuit nor the government's brief opposing certiorari relied on the argument (that the rubric requires) that the conspiracy to commit a forcible felony precludes the justification defense. Instead, the government argued that the defendant as a felon in possession had a duty to retreat safely and could have done so instead of shooting as he did. The evidence indicates this was a scenario where two rival gangs were actively seeking opportunities to shoot members of the other, not that an innocent person was attacked and then defended themselves.

\noindent\textbf{My analysis.} This benchmark does two assignments, sequentially, in separate model sessions that are in the same sandbox. In this task, assignment 2 was to build on the assignment 1 output by adding discussion of an additional case. The intent is for models to output response1.md in response to assignment 1, and to output response2.md in the same sandbox for assignment 2.

The model is told, ``Build on [the assignment 1] analysis as the prompt directs,'' but not clearly told that it is expected to output a full revised version of the brief (as opposed to supplemental or revised sections that can be inserted into a revised brief). Since assignment 2 is framed as a mere supplementation task, the model might expect that it should write only the new material.

However, five of the assignment 1 rubrics are repeated on the assignment 2 output---so a model is penalized heavily if it interprets the instructions as requesting simply an update to the specific section to which the new authority is relevant. In effect, the rubric is testing for one particular response to somewhat ambiguous instructions. This rewards work beyond the scope the prompt states, and a complete revised brief is not what the prompt clearly requests.
