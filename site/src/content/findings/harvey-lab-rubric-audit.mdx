Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB) grades long-form legal work product against expert-written rubric criteria. Its headline metric is strict: a run scores 1 only if **every** criterion passes, and two LLM judges (Claude Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.5) must both agree ([`docs/eval-strategies.md`](https://github.com/harveyai/harvey-labs/blob/main/docs/eval-strategies.md)). That design makes each criterion a potential single point of failure. If a criterion is legally wrong, a correct memo scores zero.

We read two LAB tasks closely against their records and the governing law. The core issue-spotting criteria are mostly sound. But in the litigation task, about a dozen of the 34 criteria are legally wrong, test something the prompt never requested, or would fail a correct memo.

## The rubric contradicts itself

The clearest sign that the answer key was not checked against the law it tests is an internal contradiction:

- **C-020** says Georgia law may govern the tort claims.
- **C-012** says an integration clause cannot bar fraudulent inducement under "Georgia and/or Texas" law.
- Under Georgia law, a plaintiff who affirms a contract containing a merger clause cannot claim reliance. *Ekeledo v. Amporful*, 281 Ga. 817, 819 (2007). The plaintiff here affirmed: it performed for 21 months, terminated for cause under the contract's own § 8.2, and sued for damages. It did not rescind and tender back before suing, as *Novare Grp. v. Sarif*, 290 Ga. 186, 188 (2011), requires.

If the key's Issue 11 is right, its Issue 5 is wrong.

## Where the answer key rewards the wrong answer

**Integration clause (C-012, C-024).** The key treats the defendant's integration-clause argument as a critical defect. It is one of the defendant's stronger arguments. Under Texas law, a pure merger clause does not bar fraudulent inducement (*Italian Cowboy Partners v. Prudential*, 341 S.W.3d 323, 334 (Tex. 2011)), but a clear disclaimer of reliance generally does between sophisticated, counseled parties (*Schlumberger*; *Forest Oil*; *IBM v. Lufkin Indus.*, 573 S.W.3d 224 (Tex. 2019)). The contract has such a disclaimer in § 14.7's second sentence, which neither brief quotes. Under Georgia law, the merger sentence alone suffices once the plaintiff affirms. The plaintiff's real counters are narrow: the defendant argued only the merger sentence, and a declaration describing the contract as an unmodified standard form creates a fact question on the first *Forest Oil* factor.

**Contractual limitations period (C-016).** The key requires an unconscionability argument against the one-year limitations clause. The stronger argument is statutory: Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 16.070 voids contractual limitations periods shorter than two years, with an exception only for sales of a business entity. Georgia courts routinely enforce one-year periods, including on a 12(b)(6) motion in a commercial contract (*Cobra Tactical v. Payment Alliance*, 315 F. Supp. 3d 1342 (N.D. Ga. 2018)). Nothing in the rubric rewards § 16.070. (The key also attributes Texas's four-year period to § 16.004; the residual statute is § 16.051.)

**Accrual as a "factual dispute" (C-013, C-014).** The complaint itself pleads when the plaintiff knew of each defect, and most of those dates fall more than a year before suit:

| Knowledge event pleaded in the complaint | One-year deadline | Timely? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| August 2022: first defect | August 2023 | No |
| November 2022: second defect | November 2023 | No |
| December 5, 2022: written notice of both | December 5, 2023 | No |
| April 15, 2023: patch fails | April 15, 2024 | Yes, if treated as a new breach |

A "full extent of harm" theory fails under *La Grasta v. First Union Sec.*, 358 F.3d 840, 846 (11th Cir. 2004), and a January 2023 admission by the defendant confirms knowledge rather than delaying it, so C-014's treatment of it as an alternative accrual date is wrong. The better arguments are legal: § 16.070, the failed April 2023 patch as a separate breach, and (weakly) estoppel from repair promises.

**UDTPA (C-017).** The criterion misses that Georgia's Uniform Deceptive Trade Practices Act provides injunctive relief only, while the complaint seeks damages from a party that has already been terminated.

## Criteria the prompt never asked for

The task prompt asks for "a comprehensive opposition issues memo." It does not ask for severity ratings. Yet C-022 through C-025 grade them ("PASS if Critical/Significant; FAIL if Minor"). A memo that assigns no ratings is neither, so the verdict falls to each judge's discretion, and both judges must pass all four. The rubric also conflates two questions: how flawed the defendant's argument is, and how much it threatens the client. An easy, low-stakes point is exactly what a careful memo would call minor, and "minor" fails C-025.

## What the key never tests

Every criterion targets a weakness in the defendant's motion. None targets the client's own exposure, though a real opposition memo would have to confront at least three problems in the record: an email projecting 5,000–6,000 concurrent records against a complaint pleading a "non-negotiable" 10,000–15,000; an unanswered question about whether a demo ran in staging; and a complaint quotation of an executive's email that does not match the email.

## A second task shows the same pattern

In a privilege-review task, log entry #072 is an executive asking the general counsel whether there is "any legal basis for holding off" on reporting an environmental exceedance to the state regulator. The general counsel mostly counsels compliance. Asking whether conduct is lawful is core privileged advice, and the Third Circuit's crime-fraud test requires that the client was committing or planning a crime or fraud and used the advice to further it (*In re Grand Jury*, 705 F.3d 133 (3d Cir. 2012)). Nothing in the 55 documents shows the report was delayed. A memo concluding "privileged; the exception does not apply on this record" likely fails C-081, which requires special handling.

Worse, **C-079 requires an "October 1, 2024" motion-to-compel deadline that appears nowhere in the 55 documents**; the log mentions only an "anticipated" motion. That criterion cannot be passed without inventing the date. C-069 through C-071 also require a spreadsheet column layout and High/Medium/Low risk levels that the prompt never requests.

## Why it matters

These are not stylistic disagreements. Under all-pass scoring with two concurring judges, a criterion that misstates the law or the record converts correct work into failure, and a criterion that grades an unrequested format measures compliance with an unstated preference. Rubric benchmarks can still be valuable, but their answer keys need the same legal review we would give a brief. Outcome-graded benchmarks such as LegalForecastBench avoid this particular failure mode because the answer key is what the court actually did, though they have their own limits, discussed in the [methods](/methods/).
