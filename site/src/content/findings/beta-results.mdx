An initial "beta" version of LegalForecastBench, a benchmark that tests models' ability to predict real-world outcomes on motions to dismiss in federal litigation, showed that models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and SpaceXAI each sit on the observed Pareto frontier of cost and predictive performance on at least one metric.

## Why this benchmark matters

This benchmark gives models motion to dismiss briefs from real federal cases and asks the model to predict how the judge will resolve each issue in the motion. There are several benefits to this approach.

**Objectively verifiable, high-level task.** Testing models' ability to predict motion outcomes is valuable because it is an objectively verifiable, high-level judgment task. In real-world litigation, predicting how a judge is likely to react to a contemplated litigation strategy or a potential motion is usually performed by the most senior and highly paid lawyers on a litigation team. Unlike many legal work-product tasks, moreover, the observable judicial disposition provides objective ground truth. We operationalize that disposition as a binary outcome (whether the motion's first written disposition fully dismisses a given claim and defendant). This approach could be more reliable and objective than rubric-based approaches like Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB) ([Harvey's methodology](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/introducing-harveys-legal-agent-benchmark)).

**Contamination resistance.** This benchmark is also designed to be versioned and contamination resistant, so future versions will remain useful even if models have trained on earlier benchmark releases or the judicial decisions they test. Each version can be a temporal holdout; the beta uses judicial decisions from June 30 to August 7, 2026, which postdate the documented training cutoffs for most evaluated models, reducing the risk that models memorized the outcomes during training. Cutoff information is uncertain for Kimi, Muse, and Grok. I've built a pipeline that ingests and labels new cases, so that once we have models with training cutoffs after July of this year, we can create a new version that uses the most recent judicial decisions available at that point in time. This approach provides the best "point in time" snapshot comparing models' predictive capabilities; the one downside is that scores cannot be equated across versions since each version uses a different set of cases, which could entail a different difficulty level. (We may run future models against both the latest version and historical versions and report results separately, so users can see improvement over time and a contamination-resistant snapshot.)

One open question is construct validity: whether prediction is a good proxy for the models' formal legal reasoning ability. Part of the task is modeling judge effects; a judge's leanings (pro-plaintiff or pro-defendant) could influence the outcome, so testing with the judge's identity present (as we do) in part tests the model's ability to identify the leanings of the particular judge who decided that case. My hypothesis is that this will not predominate over legal reasoning on this kind of benchmark; most garden-variety litigation matters are straightforward enough that the outcome does not turn on the judge's judicial philosophy, so the most effective way to predict the outcome of most cases will simply be to be better at analyzing and understanding the facts and applicable legal doctrine.

Even to the extent that this benchmark tests prediction (rather than formal legal reasoning ability), the fact is that predicting how a real judge might react to a real fact pattern is far more valuable than mere abstract reasoning about the doctrinally "correct" legal answer. Legal realists would argue that there is no such thing as a "correct" legal answer; as Oliver Wendell Holmes put it, "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law." Oliver Wendell Holmes, Jr., *The Path of the Law*, 10 Harv. L. Rev. 457 (1897). Regardless of one's view on the debate between legal formalists and legal realists, both sides would agree that the experience and judgment to understand what factors impact real-world outcomes is much more economically useful than formal legal reasoning in the abstract.

## Results

The completed beta covers 91 cases and 387 scored claim-defendant units (for example, a motion challenging five distinct claims against two separately scored defendant groups would have ten claim-defendant units). Models predicted the probability that each unit would be fully dismissed in the motion's first written disposition. Micro Brier gives every scored unit equal weight; equal-case Brier gives every case equal weight. Lower Brier is better. Unit accuracy classifies a prediction of at least 50% as dismissal.

Models received the pre-decision docket and documents through a common tool-enabled harness, in a sandbox with Internet access disabled. The official runs were done in GitHub Actions, with each run outputting a package with the raw results. Each configuration produced one retained successful prediction per unit; this beta did not measure run-to-run variance. We estimated uncertainty using a paired cluster bootstrap (one million resamples of the 91 cases, units clustered by case), and applied a Bonferroni correction across 45 model pairs and three metrics. The analysis is exploratory; cross-case dependence (e.g., related cases in the same MDL) has not yet been verified.

## Interpretation

At least one model from Anthropic, OpenAI, and SpaceXAI was on the observed Pareto frontier of cost/quality on one or more metrics.

- **Value champion:** GPT-5.6 Luna was an excellent, inexpensive model, with performance that this sample did not statistically distinguish from the top-scoring models.
- **Mid-range value:** Grok 4.6 had better observed Brier scores than GPT-5.6 Luna, for about 10.6 times Luna's price, but remained inexpensive at $15.72 for the cohort. Its unit accuracy was slightly lower than Luna's.
- **Top observed performance:** Fable 5.1 had the best micro Brier and unit accuracy; its cost was $83.18, or roughly 56 times Luna's cost. GPT-5.6 Sol, at $24.91 or about 16.8 times Luna's cost, had the best equal-case Brier. The choice of weighting therefore changes the observed leader.
- **Honorable mention:** GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5, Claude Sonnet 5, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and Kimi K3 were not on the observed cost/performance Pareto frontier, but none had significantly worse Brier scores after multiple-comparison correction.

### The contrast with LAB

One particularly interesting result is the contrast with LAB. Muse Spark was one of the top-performing models on LAB, but was the lowest performing model here. Specifically, on Vals AI's September 10 LAB leaderboard, Muse Spark 1.2 ranked first and Muse Spark 1.3 (the version tested here) ranked third. In our analysis, Muse Spark 1.3 was the only model with statistically distinguishable underperformance: worse than Grok on both Brier metrics and worse than Opus on micro Brier. LAB rewards models that give specific kinds of answers, which might make it more vulnerable to benchmark overfitting; models could improve at satisfying the rubric requirements without comparable gains in generalizable abilities. LAB's headline metric (the all-pass rate) rewards recall against its rubrics, essentially requiring models to flag the large number of issues LAB tests. But that may not be the best optimization target, since practicing lawyers would often prefer precision, focusing on a small number of dispositive points, not a large number of technically accurate but minor issues ([Vals AI's LAB results](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/hlab)).

### Why GPT-6 Astra did not lead

One other surprising result was that GPT-6 Astra did not beat Sol or Luna. Sol had better observed scores on all three metrics; Luna had better micro Brier and unit accuracy, while Astra had better equal-case Brier.

One explanation is that this beta was too small to reliably separate the leading models (only Muse underperformed to a statistically significant degree). A larger sample would be needed to establish a reliable ordering among the models other than Muse.

Another possible contributor is that the OpenAI models forecast more sharply, with far more predictions at 90% confidence or more, but were less well calibrated: mean stated confidence of 95–97% but accuracy of only 89–90% within that subset. Brier score evaluates probabilistic forecasts, rewarding both calibration and discrimination, so confident errors incur large squared losses.

Here, "90% confidence" means at least 90% probability of dismissal or at most 10% probability of dismissal. These are scored units, not independent cases; several misses can arise from one case.

Fable was never wrong among its 81 high-confidence predictions, and Grok and Kimi missed only one each. By contrast, Luna missed 20, Astra missed 21, and Sol missed 26, although each made substantially more high-confidence predictions. The OpenAI models had thoughtful and perceptive analyses of the underlying cases, but several overly confident predictions cost a lot of points: their mean stated confidence within these subsets was roughly 95–97%, while accuracy was roughly 89–90%. The sample was small enough that wrong, overconfident predictions on a handful of cases could materially affect the ordering.

### Amendment and mootness

I audited cases that had the largest negative relative impact on Astra's score and found that, in addition to some overconfident predictions, Astra sometimes appeared to underweight the possibility that the motion to dismiss would become moot because the judge would allow the plaintiff to amend the complaint. This counted as a nondismissal outcome here, but in many cases after a plaintiff amends, the defendant can file a renewed motion to dismiss so the amended complaint might ultimately be dismissed. In some cases, Astra, Sol, and Luna explicitly considered the possibility that an amendment would moot the motion to dismiss. But in other instances, Astra seemed to ignore the possibility of amendment or mootness in coming up with its probabilities. This suggests that task specification could be improved to obtain more consistent and accurate results. The prompt here explicitly asked for a prediction of the first written disposition, not the ultimate merits outcome (after a plaintiff amended the complaint), but more clearly distinguishing between the judge dismissing the complaint but giving the plaintiff leave to amend (which counts as dismissal) and the plaintiff amending proactively followed by the judge denying or terminating as moot the motion to dismiss the prior version of the complaint (which does not count as dismissal) might yield more consistency in future versions.

## Conclusion and next steps

We are preparing to publish these results soon, and to work on improving case labeling, curation, and task specification for the next version of the benchmark. The LegalQuants community is also interested in repeating these tests through agent harnesses (e.g., Codex, Claude Code, etc., running in a sandbox with network egress only to the model provider's API) to test whether the harness itself has any noticeable impact on accuracy; I am building a package to facilitate that community test run. We welcome comments from researchers on any steps we could take to improve future versions of the benchmark and make it more useful, or anything we should include in the public presentation of it.

We are also investigating whether litigation prediction can serve as an RL environment with verifiable rewards, since the outcome is checkable against the real disposition. One challenge is that, while in principle it should be easy to have frontier models label outcomes and then train and test models on prediction without the answer included, I discovered while preparing this benchmark that real-world litigation data is much messier than one would expect and a fair amount of manual work is needed to clean it up and provide an accurate answer key. Case data must be purchased; many cases we purchased turned out to be unsuitable candidates for testing or training; and court data is frequently mislabeled, so we had to add several quality control steps to ensure the testing data included everything the judge would have seen. Frontier models also often disagreed on even how to label a judge's decision or which claims and defendants were at issue in the motion to dismiss; high-level legal judgment is required to properly specify the task and interpret the judge's ruling into a reliable answer key. While for purposes of the public open-source benchmark we are planning to use an AI pipeline with various QC measures to label cases, we are hand-labeling a separate set of training data (and using AI for QC) to understand whether properly labeled data could provide a useful training signal.

Although this benchmark run does not answer the question, I am particularly interested in exploring whether the hand-labeled training data provides meaningful signal that generalizes beyond prediction. In principle, teaching models to predict outcomes could help them better understand which issues actually matter in litigation (versus which points are minor or unlikely to impact outcomes) and thus help the models exhibit better "legal judgment." Current models are excellent at research and summarizing legal doctrine, but they tend to focus on minor, technical points when asked to give advice on high-level strategic decisions. The most interesting question is whether training them to discern the issues that motivate federal judicial outcomes could improve that higher-level strategic thinking ability.
